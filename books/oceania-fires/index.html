Australian Lamb Chops

Lamb chops with salt, garlic, and lemon, seared over a hot grill.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Lamb chops are a standard Australian grill, cooked at home and at club barbecues in towns such as Albury and across the sheep country inland. The chop is a whole muscle from a loin or a leg steak, salted, grilled, and rested. Australia raises a large share of the world's sheep, and the chop is one ordinary way that meat is cooked. There is no chili in this recipe; the seasoning is salt, garlic, and lemon.

Cultural background

Australian home cooks grill these chops on a gas or charcoal grate. There is lamb, and no wheat, dairy, or peanut in the seasoning. It is a weeknight grill, not a ceremonial roast and not a minced kebab.

Why it tastes this way

A hot grate browns the fat cap on a chop, and salt is enough for lamb that already tastes like lamb. Garlic and lemon sit on that crust. Inland sheep country produces the meat. The fire is what turns a raw chop into dinner, without a long marinade to hide it.

How it is made

Salt lamb chops, grill them over high heat, and rest them.

Authentic touch. Take the chops out of the refrigerator before they hit a ripping-hot grate, and do not press them flat. Pressing squeezes out the juice you rested them to keep.

How it is used

Eat them with lemon, a green salad, and potatoes.

Field notes

  • Lamb chops are an ordinary Australian home grill.
  • The chop is a whole cut and is rested after it leaves the fire.

Ingredients

  • 8 lamb loin chops
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 lemon, cut in wedges

Steps

  1. Rub the chops with salt, pepper, garlic, and oil. Refrigerate them until the grill is hot. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill until the grate is very hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the center of the thickest chop reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the chops 3 minutes. Serve with lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Rosemary on the side is common. It is not required for the salt and fire to work.
  • A thicker leg steak needs more time than a thin loin chop. Use the thermometer.

Pairings and uses

  • lemon
  • green salad
  • potatoes

Australian Grilled Prawns

Prawns with garlic, lemon, and parsley, grilled until the flesh turns opaque.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Grilled prawns are a coastal Australian meal, especially along the tropical north and the Queensland shore around Cairns, where prawns are landed and cooked the same day when the catch allows. The method is a hot grate, garlic, lemon, and a short cook. Shellfish are done when they are opaque. This is a plain coastal grill, seasoned and taken off the heat before the prawns toughen.

Cultural background

Coastal home cooks and fish shops grill large prawns in the shell or peeled. There is shellfish, and no wheat, dairy, or chili in this recipe. Parsley and lemon finish the plate. Cook them the day you buy them.

Why it tastes this way

Prawns are sweet, and a short blast of heat sets that sweetness before it turns rubbery. Garlic browns in the oil, and lemon cuts the richness. The tropical coast supplies the prawns. The recipe does not need a slogan. It needs a hot grate and a thermometer or a clear look at opaque flesh.

How it is made

Toss prawns with garlic, oil, and lemon, and grill them until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Pull them as soon as they turn opaque. Extra time on a hot grate is how sweet prawns become dry.

How it is used

Eat them with lemon, parsley, and bread only if you want wheat on the side. The prawns themselves have none.

Field notes

  • Large prawns are a coastal Australian grill, cooked briefly over high heat.
  • They are ready when the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds large prawns, peeled and deveined
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley

Steps

  1. Toss the prawns with oil, garlic, salt, and lemon juice. Refrigerate them for 15 minutes. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The prawns are still raw. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  2. Heat a grill until the grate is hot. Oil it lightly. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  3. Grill, turning once, until the flesh is opaque and the center reaches 145°F (63°C). Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  4. Toss with parsley and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).

Cook shrimp, lobster, and crab until the flesh is opaque and pearly. An internal temperature of 145°F (63°C) is the target for lobster and shrimp.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leaving the shells on protects the meat and makes them messier to eat.
  • A pinch of chili is a personal addition. This batch has none.

Pairings and uses

  • Shellfish
  • lemon
  • parsley
  • green salad
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Grilled Australian Barramundi

Barramundi fillets with salt, lemon, and a little oil, grilled until they flake.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Barramundi is a well-known Australian fish, Lates calcarifer, caught and farmed in the north, including waters around Darwin. Fillets are grilled with salt and lemon and served the day they are cooked. The flesh is mild and is ready when it reaches 145°F (63°C) and flakes. This is a northern Australian fish grill, not a chili curry and not a smoked whole fish.

Cultural background

Cooks in Darwin and other northern towns grill barramundi at home and in small restaurants. There is fish, salt, and lemon. There is no wheat, dairy, or peanut. The skin can stay on if you like it crisp.

Why it tastes this way

Barramundi is mild and a little sweet, so salt, lemon, and a hot grate are enough. The skin, if you leave it on, crisps and protects the flesh. Overcooking dries it, which is why the temperature matters more than a heavy sauce. The northern coast and the rivers are where the fish lives. The plate is that fish and the fire.

How it is made

Salt barramundi fillets and grill them until the center is opaque and flakes.

Authentic touch. Oil the grate and start skin-side down if the skin is on. Turning too soon tears the fillet.

How it is used

Eat the fish with lemon, a salad, and potatoes or rice.

Field notes

  • Barramundi is an Australian coastal and river fish, often grilled as a fillet.
  • The flesh is cooked to 145°F (63°C), until it flakes.

Ingredients

  • 4 barramundi fillets, about 6 ounces each
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 lemon, cut in wedges
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley

Steps

  1. Pat the fillets dry and rub them with salt, pepper, and oil. The fish is still raw. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  2. Heat a grill and oil the grate. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the center reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve with lemon and parsley. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole small fish can be grilled if it fits the grate. Check the temperature at the thickest point.
  • Dill or parsley on top is a fresh finish and does not change the cook.

Pairings and uses

Australian Damper

A plain wheat loaf, crusty outside and dense inside, with salt and the smell of the fire.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Damper is a settler campfire bread of wheat flour, water, and salt, baked in the coals or in a camp oven by stockmen and colonial travelers. Aboriginal Australians milled native seeds and cooked their own breads long before wheat arrived. This recipe is the settler loaf. It is not an Aboriginal ceremony, and it is not a native-seed bread; Ballarat and other inland camps are one historical setting for flour-and-water bread on a journey; the loaf contains wheat.

Cultural background

Settler cooks mixed this dough when yeast and an oven were not at hand. There is wheat, salt, and water. There is no dairy, egg, or meat, so the written loaf is vegan. It is camp bread, not a ritual. Do not present it as Aboriginal food.

Why it tastes this way

Wheat flour and salt, cooked against heat, make a dense crumb and a hard crust. A campfire dries the outside faster than a kitchen oven and can leave a little ash and smoke. That plainness is the point of a ration bread. Native-seed breads have their own flavors and their own makers. This one tastes of wheat and fire because that is what the settlers carried.

How it is made

Mix flour, salt, and water, shape a round, and bake it in a camp oven or a home oven until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Cook it through. A handsome crust can hide a wet center of raw dough. Break one open and look.

How it is used

Tear it and eat it warm with grilled meat or with nothing but salt.

Field notes

  • Damper is a settler campfire bread of wheat flour, water, and salt.
  • It is not an Aboriginal ceremony. Aboriginal Australians have their own breads from native seeds.

Ingredients

  • 3 cups wheat flour, plus more for the bench
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder
  • 1 cup water, plus more if the dough is dry

Steps

  1. Stir the flour, salt, and baking powder. Add the water and mix a soft dough. There is no meat in this bread.
  2. Shape a round loaf and dust it with flour. Heat a camp oven by a fire, or heat a home oven to 400°F (200°C) and call it an oven.
  3. Bake until the crust is firm and the center is no longer wet dough, about 30 minutes. If you used a camp oven, keep the coals around it, not only under it, so the bottom does not burn.
  4. Cool it a few minutes, then break it open. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours if the weather is hot. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A camp oven in the coals is the older method. A home oven is the kitchen method and should be named as an oven.
  • A spoon of dairy fat would change the loaf. This one uses water.

Pairings and uses

  • Breads
  • grilled lamb
  • salt
  • a green salad
  • Vegetarian
  • Vegan

Torres Strait Kup Murri

Chicken and sweet potato steamed with chili, garlic, and leaf, in a covered pot.

30 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Kup murri is a Torres Strait earth oven: food layered with hot stones and leaves and covered so it steams. Torres Strait Islander cooks prepare it for community gatherings. A backyard pot is not that oven, and it is not a ceremony. This page uses a heavy covered pot with chicken, sweet potato, and chili, and it says so; the chicken is cooked to a poultry temperature; there is no raw cassava in the pot.

Cultural background

Torres Strait Islander families on islands such as Thursday Island have their own earth-oven practice. The written recipe is a home covered cook for people who are not holding that ceremony. There is chicken, and no wheat, dairy, or peanut. Treat the pot as dinner, not as the community oven.

Why it tastes this way

A closed pot steams chicken and sweet potato together, so the tuber takes on the meat juices and the chili and garlic perfume the steam. An earth oven does this against hot stones and leaves. The home pot can echo the steam and the seasoning. It cannot stand in for the gathering, and it should not be described as if it did.

How it is made

Season chicken and sweet potato, seal them in a heavy covered pot, and cook until the chicken is done. Call this a home pot, not a ceremony.

Authentic touch. Keep the lid on. If you stir it often, you are boiling a stew, not steaming a closed cook.

How it is used

Serve the chicken and sweet potato together, with the juices.

Field notes

  • Kup murri is a Torres Strait earth oven used for community gatherings.
  • A home covered pot is not that oven and is not a ceremony.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds chicken pieces
  • 2 pounds sweet potato, peeled and cut in chunks
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 hot chili, sliced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Toss the chicken with onion, garlic, chili, salt, and oil. The chicken is still raw. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. Put the chicken and sweet potato in a heavy pot with the water. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Cover the pot tightly. This is a home pot. It is not a Torres Strait ceremony and it is not an earth oven. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook over medium heat, lid on, until the thickest chicken piece reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone, and the sweet potato is tender. Do not serve the chicken rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Banana leaves can line the pot when they are sold. Parchment is only a liner.
  • Fish can replace chicken in some cooks and would need a fish temperature. This pot is chicken.

Maori Hangi

Chicken, sweet potato, and cabbage steamed in a covered pot, salty and soft.

30 min prep · 2 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

A hangi is a Maori earth oven. Heated stones, baskets of meat and vegetables, and wet coverings steam the food in the ground, including at gatherings on the marae. Rotorua is one place visitors see public hangi. A backyard imitation is not a marae hangi; this page uses a heavy covered pot with chicken, kumara, and cabbage, and it names that limit; the chicken is cooked to a poultry temperature; do not dig a pit in a lawn and call it the ceremony.

Cultural background

Maori cooks run a hangi for hui and for manuhiri, the guests. The foods often include chicken, pork, kumara, potato, and cabbage. This pot uses chicken and vegetables only, so one temperature rules the meat. There is no wheat and no dairy. It is a home steam, not a marae hangi.

Why it tastes this way

Steam from a closed oven makes the chicken and the kumara taste of each other, without a hard crust. Cabbage sweetens in that steam. Stones and wet sacks in the ground hold the heat for hours at a real hangi. A covered pot on a stove can borrow the softness and cannot borrow the marae. The honesty is part of cooking it at home.

How it is made

Pack chicken, kumara, and cabbage in a heavy covered pot and cook until the chicken is done. Say plainly that this is not a marae hangi.

Authentic touch. Season the food before it goes in the pot. A hangi seasons the baskets, not the table afterward only.

How it is used

Serve the chicken, kumara, and cabbage together.

Field notes

  • A hangi is a Maori earth oven, including the one used for gatherings on the marae.
  • A backyard covered pot is not a marae hangi.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds chicken pieces
  • 2 pounds kumara or sweet potato, peeled and chunked
  • 1/2 small cabbage, cut in wedges
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Toss the chicken with onion, salt, and oil. Layer it with kumara and cabbage in a heavy pot. Add the water. The chicken is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Cover the pot. A backyard imitation is not a marae hangi. Do not present this pot as one. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook over medium heat, lid on, until the thickest chicken piece reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone, and the kumara is tender. Do not serve the chicken rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork is common in a hangi and would need its own temperature if you add it. This pot is chicken.
  • Potato can sit beside the kumara. Keep the lid on either way.

Pairings and uses

Fijian Lovo

Chicken steamed with coconut, garlic, and sweet potato, mild and rich.

30 min prep · 1 hr 40 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

A lovo is a Fijian earth oven, a pit of heated stones used for feasts, with meat, root vegetables, and sometimes a coconut parcel in leaves. This page is a home covered pot, not a full lovo and not a ceremony. Chicken, canned coconut milk, spinach in place of leaves that may not be sold, and sweet potato are the foods. There is no raw cassava; the chicken is cooked to a poultry temperature.

Cultural background

Fijian cooks prepare a lovo for community meals. The home pot borrows the coconut and the closed steam. There is chicken and coconut. Coconut milk is not dairy. There is no wheat and no peanut. Name the pot as a pot.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut milk and closed steam make the chicken rich, and sweet potato soaks up the juice. Garlic and a little chili perfume the pot. In a lovo the leaves and the stones add their own smell. At home the coconut and the chicken are the tastes you can actually make, without pretending the kitchen floor is a pit.

How it is made

Cook chicken, sweet potato, spinach, and canned coconut milk in a covered pot until the chicken is done.

Authentic touch. Use canned coconut milk, shaken, and do not try to process raw cassava for this meal.

How it is used

Serve the chicken, greens, and sweet potato with the coconut juices.

Field notes

  • A lovo is a Fijian earth oven. This page is a covered home pot, not a pit.
  • The recipe uses sweet potato and canned coconut milk, not raw cassava.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds chicken pieces
  • 1 can coconut milk, 13.5 ounces
  • 1 pound sweet potato, peeled and chunked
  • 4 cups spinach
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 mild chili, sliced
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Put the chicken, coconut milk, sweet potato, spinach, garlic, chili, and salt in a heavy pot. Do not add cassava. The chicken is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Cover the pot. This is a home cook, not a Fijian lovo pit and not a ceremony. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook over medium heat until the thickest chicken piece reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone, and the sweet potato is tender. Do not serve the chicken rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Taro leaves can replace the spinach when a shop sells them as a food leaf, not a random garden leaf.
  • A little more chili makes the pot hotter. This one is mild.

Samoan Umu

Pork shoulder steamed with canned coconut milk and spinach, rich and salty.

30 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

An umu is a Samoan earth oven, used for Sundays and family gatherings, with pork and parcels of coconut and leaves. This home pot uses canned coconut milk and spinach when taro leaves are not sold, and it does not use raw cassava. A covered pot is not an umu pit. The pork is a shoulder, cooked until it is safe and tender enough to slice.

Cultural background

Samoan cooks run an umu for household gatherings. This recipe is the home covered cook: pork, canned coconut, and spinach. There is no wheat and no dairy. Coconut is not a tree nut in this allergen list. Do not grate raw cassava into the pot.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat and coconut milk are both rich, and a closed pot keeps them together so the greens taste savory. Taro leaves have a mineral taste. Spinach is the shop substitute and is milder, which is an honest swap when the leaf is not sold. The umu pit adds smoke and leaf. The pot still gives you the coconut pork that is the edible idea of the parcel.

How it is made

Cook a pork shoulder with canned coconut milk and spinach in a covered pot until it is safe and tender. Do not use raw cassava.

Authentic touch. Shake the can of coconut milk so the fat is mixed through. A separated can makes a watery pot and a lump of coconut fat.

How it is used

Slice the pork and serve it with the greens and the coconut juice.

Field notes

  • A Samoan umu is an earth oven. This recipe is a covered pot with canned coconut milk.
  • Spinach stands in for taro leaves when the leaves are not sold. Raw cassava is not used.

Ingredients

  • 1 boneless pork shoulder, about 4 pounds
  • 1 can coconut milk, 13.5 ounces
  • 8 cups spinach
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Rub the pork with oil and salt. Put it in a heavy pot with onion, spinach, coconut milk, and water. Do not add cassava. The pork is still raw. Cook the pork to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Cover the pot. This is a home cook, not a Samoan umu pit. Cook the pork to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Cook over low heat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). For pork you want tender, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Cook the pork to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  4. Rest it, then slice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook the pork to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Taro leaves can replace the spinach when you can buy them as food. Do not use an unknown garden leaf.
  • A Tongan umu uses different foods. It is a different page.

Tongan Umu

Lamb steamed with canned coconut milk, tomato, and sweet potato.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

A Tongan umu is an earth oven, used for Sunday meals and gatherings, and the foods are not the same as a Samoan umu. One Tongan parcel, lu sipi, cooks lamb with coconut and onion in leaves. This page follows that lamb-and-coconut pattern, with sweet potato and tomato, in a covered home pot. It uses canned coconut milk; it does not use raw cassava, and it does not use the pork shoulder from the Samoan page; the lamb is a whole cut and is rested.

Cultural background

Tongan cooks prepare umu food for family Sundays. The home pot is not an earth oven and not a ceremony. There is lamb and coconut, and no wheat, dairy, or peanut. The different meat is deliberate: lamb here, pork on the Samoan page.

Why it tastes this way

History

Mumu, in Papua New Guinea, is an earth-oven cook, and the practice differs by community. One village's leaves, meats, and stones are not a national recipe. This page does not pretend they are. It is a home covered cook of chicken, sweet potato, ginger, and greens, for a kitchen in Port Moresby or anywhere else; it is not a ceremony; there is no raw cassava; the chicken is cooked to a poultry temperature.

Cultural background

People across Papua New Guinea cook with earth ovens in ways that are local. A single written pot cannot represent them. This pot is chicken and sweet potato, with ginger. There is no wheat, dairy, or peanut. Call it a home covered cook.

Why it tastes this way

Ginger and closed steam scent the chicken, and sweet potato takes the juices. Greens wilt into the pot and keep it from tasting only of meat. A community mumu can include foods and timings this page will not universalize. The home pot tastes of the ingredients you actually put in it, which is the honest limit.

History

An ahima'a is a Tahitian earth oven, a pit of heated stones used to steam pork, breadfruit, and banana for gatherings. This page is a covered home pot, not that pit and not a ceremony. There is no raw cassava; breadfruit can replace the sweet potato when you can buy it ripe or already cooked, not as a raw project; the pork is cooked until it is safe and tender.

Tahitian cooks prepare an ahima'a for feasts. The home pot uses the same idea of pork, coconut, and a sweet root or fruit. There is no wheat and no dairy. Name it as a home cook. Papeete is the city on this page, not the whole of the islands' practice.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat, coconut, and a ripe banana make a sweet and savory steam, and sweet potato drinks the juice. An earth oven adds the smell of leaves and hot stone. A covered pot keeps the moisture, which is the part a kitchen can do. The banana should be ripe and eaten as a fruit, not a starch you processed from a bitter root.

How it is made

Cook pork shoulder with canned coconut milk, sweet potato, and ripe banana in a covered pot until the pork is safe and tender.

Vanuatu Laplap Packet

Grated sweet potato and canned coconut milk, steamed in a packet until thick.

25 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Laplap is a Vanuatu dish of grated starchy root mixed with coconut and cooked in leaves, often in an earth oven. This packet uses sweet potato and canned coconut milk. It does not use raw bitter cassava, which is not safe to grate and bake at home as if it were a potato. A home packet in a covered pot is not a ceremony; it is a kitchen version of the sweet potato and coconut idea, for a table in Port Vila or elsewhere.

Cultural background

Ni-Vanuatu cooks have their own laplap, and the root and the occasion differ. This written packet is vegetarian, with sweet potato and coconut. There is no meat, wheat, dairy, or peanut. It is not a ceremony, and it is not a cassava recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Grated sweet potato and coconut milk set into a dense, slightly sweet cake when they steam. The coconut fat keeps it from tasting like plain mashed potato. Leaves, when you have banana leaves sold for cooking, add a green smell. The home packet has the starch and the coconut, which is the part you can make without a pit and without a dangerous root.

How it is made

Mix grated sweet potato with canned coconut milk and salt, wrap it, and steam it in a covered pot until it is thick and hot. Do not use raw bitter cassava.

Authentic touch. Grate the sweet potato, do not cube it and hope. The texture of laplap is a grated mass, not a stew of chunks.

How it is used

Cut the packet into squares and eat it warm.

Field notes

  • This laplap packet is sweet potato and coconut. It is not raw bitter cassava.
  • A home packet is not a ceremony.

Ingredients

  • 1 teaspoon salt

  • kitchen string

Steps

  3. Cover the pot and steam until the packet is thick and hot in the center, about 60 minutes. This home packet is not a ceremony.
