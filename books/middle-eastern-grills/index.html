Adana Kebab

Hand-minced lamb, hot pepper, and tail fat, smoky from a close charcoal fire.

30 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 wide skewers

History

Adana kebab is a hand-minced spicy lamb kebab from the city of Adana, pressed onto wide flat skewers. Hot red pepper and lamb fat are part of the mix, not optional decorations. It is a distinct kebab from the milder Urfa version. The meat is ground, so it is cooked through. This home mix uses a knife or a coarse grind and does not claim a guild certificate.

Cultural background

Adana's kebab cooks and the home cooks who follow them make this spicy skewer. It is not Urfa kebab, not chicken shish, and not a stew. The heat is the city's signature.

Why it tastes this way

Fat from the lamb bastes the mince as it sits over charcoal, and hot pepper blooms in that fat. Hand mincing leaves a coarser bite than a fine supermarket grind. A wide skewer keeps the soft meat from falling apart. The result is smoky, rich, and hot, which is why it is not interchangeable with a mild cousin.

How it is made

Knead spicy minced lamb onto wide skewers and grill over hot coals until the center is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Work the mince until it sticks to a flat skewer. Keep a little fat in the mix or the kebab will dry before it browns.

How it is used

With sliced onion, parsley, and grilled tomato. Sumac can be sprinkled at the table.

Field notes

  • Adana kebab is hand-minced lamb with hot pepper, formed on a wide skewer.
  • It is a different kebab from Urfa, which is the milder cousin.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb, coarse
  • 3 oz lamb fat, finely chopped
  • 2 tablespoons hot red pepper flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 4 wide flat metal skewers

Steps

  1. Knead the lamb, fat, pepper flakes, salt, and black pepper until the paste is sticky. Chill 15 minutes.
  2. Wet your hands and press the meat along the wide skewers in a long sausage shape.
  3. Grill over hot coals, turning carefully, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C). Ground lamb is not served pink.
  4. Rest a minute and serve with onion. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A coarse grinder if you cannot hand-mince, with the same pepper and fat.
  • A less fiery amount of pepper for children at the table, still not the Urfa recipe.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Onion and parsley
  • Grilled tomato
  • Sumac
  • Gluten-free

Urfa Kebab

Mild minced lamb with pepper paste, fat, and charcoal, without Adana's chile heat.

30 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 wide skewers

History

Urfa kebab is the milder cousin of Adana kebab, associated with Sanliurfa. It is still hand-minced lamb on a wide skewer, but the seasoning is not the hot pepper load of Adana. Many cooks use a mild pepper or isot paste for color and a rounded taste rather than burn. Treating it as the same recipe with the heat turned down misses the point: the balance is different. The meat is ground and cooked through.

Cultural background

Sanliurfa kebab houses and home cooks make this as its own skewer. It is not Adana with fewer flakes, and it is not a stew. The mildness is deliberate.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat and a mild pepper paste give a sweet-smoky surface instead of a chile blast. Charcoal still marks the crust. Because the heat is low, you taste the meat and the pepper's fruit more clearly. That is the distinction worth keeping on a separate page.

How it is made

Knead mildly seasoned minced lamb onto wide skewers and grill until the center is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Use a mild pepper, not the hot flakes from the Adana mix. Press the meat firmly so a soft, fatty mince does not drop into the fire.

How it is used

With onion, parsley, and grilled pepper. It can share a table with Adana, and the two should still taste different.

Field notes

  • Urfa kebab is the milder minced-lamb cousin associated with Sanliurfa.
  • It is a distinct kebab from Adana, not the same formula with the chile removed in name only.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb, coarse
  • 3 oz lamb fat, finely chopped
  • 2 tablespoons mild red pepper paste or mild paprika
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 small onion, grated and squeezed dry
  • 4 wide flat metal skewers

Steps

  1. Knead the lamb, fat, mild pepper paste, salt, and onion until sticky. Do not add hot pepper flakes. Chill 15 minutes.
  2. Press the meat onto wide skewers.
  3. Grill over hot coals, turning carefully, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Serve with onion and herbs. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of mild tomato and pepper paste if you have no isot pepper.
  • A little more onion in the mix for a softer texture.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Onion and parsley
  • Grilled pepper
  • Sumac
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Erzurum Cag Kebab

Sliced lamb, salted and stacked, with a grilled edge and a juicy center.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Cağ kebab is associated with Erzurum and a horizontal spit: slices of lamb are stacked on the spit and turned beside the fire. That apparatus is the local method. The home version here is layered sliced lamb grilled on a grate. It is labeled as that, and it is not a horizontal spit. The meat is a whole-muscle cut, salted, not a mince. Do not rename a pan of slices as the Erzurum spit.

Cultural background

Erzurum's spit cooks are the reference, and this page is honest about the home substitute. It is not Adana mince and not doner shaved from a vertical stack.

Why it tastes this way

Thin slices of lamb brown quickly and stay pinkish inside only if you stop early; this recipe cooks them to a safe temperature instead. Salt and lamb fat are the flavor. The horizontal spit bastes the stack with its own drippings. A grate can brown the meat, but it will not copy that slow turning stack, which is why the label matters.

How it is made

Season sliced lamb and grill it on a hot grate. This home version is not a horizontal spit.

Authentic touch. Slice the lamb evenly and grill it in a single layer. Say that this is a home grill, not the horizontal spit, when you serve it.

How it is used

With onion, herbs, and grilled tomato. Eat it hot, as slices.

Field notes

  • Cağ kebab in Erzurum is cooked on a horizontal spit of stacked lamb slices.
  • The home recipe is layered sliced lamb on a grill and is not that spit.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb boneless lamb leg, sliced 1/3 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 onion, sliced, for serving

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb slices with salt, pepper, and oil. This home version is grilled slices, not a horizontal spit.
  2. Heat a grill until it is hot. Lay the slices in one layer.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Give the meat a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with onion. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A real horizontal spit if you have one and know how to stack the meat safely.
  • Lamb shoulder slices if leg is not available, still a whole-muscle cut.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Onion
  • Parsley

Lebanese Shish Taouk

Chicken with yogurt, garlic, lemon, and a light char.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Shish taouk is chicken on skewers, a standard of Lebanese grills. A yogurt marinade is common, which means the dish contains dairy and is not vegan. Garlic and lemon sharpen it. The pieces are small so they cook quickly over charcoal. It is poultry, and it has to be cooked through. This is not lamb kafta and not a garlic sauce by itself.

Cultural background

Lebanese restaurants and home cooks grill taouk for a weeknight or a spread. The yogurt makes it dairy. Toum, the garlic sauce, is a separate emulsion and is not in this recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Yogurt clings to chicken and browns in spots, while lemon and garlic stay bright if you do not burn them. Charcoal adds smoke the oven misses. The dairy is part of the flavor, not a garnish, which is why a dairy-free claim would be false for this version. Small cubes cook evenly, which suits breast or thigh if the temperature is checked.

How it is made

Marinate chicken in yogurt, garlic, and lemon, then grill on skewers until the poultry is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Use thigh meat if you can, and wipe off thick yogurt before the grill so it does not carbonize. Check the center of a cube, not the skewer.

How it is used

With salad, pickles, and rice. A garlic sauce on the side is optional and, if it contains egg, is a separate allergen.

Field notes

  • Lebanese shish taouk is chicken on skewers, often in a yogurt marinade.
  • Yogurt means the dish is dairy and not vegan.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb boneless chicken thigh, cut in cubes
  • 1/2 cup plain yogurt
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 lemon, juiced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 4 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, garlic, lemon juice, salt, and oil. Add the chicken and refrigerate 30 minutes.
  2. Thread onto skewers, letting extra yogurt drip off. Heat the grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest cube reaches 165°F (74°C), not touching bone if any bone remains.
  4. Rest a few minutes. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Breast meat, watched closely so it does not dry after it reaches temperature.
  • A dairy-free oil marinade, which would be a different recipe and is not this one.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • Rice
  • Pickles
  • Tomato salad
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Lebanese Kafta

Minced lamb with parsley, onion, and a grilled crust.

25 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Lebanese kafta is minced lamb with parsley and onion, pressed on skewers and grilled. The herbs are mixed into the meat, which makes it different from a plain koobideh and from Adana's hot pepper kebab. It is everyday grill food from the coast inland. The meat is ground and cooked through. Allspice is a frequent addition and is included here in a small amount.

Cultural background

Home cooks and grill restaurants across Lebanon make kafta. It is not chicken taouk and not a raw kibbeh. Parsley and onion are part of the mince, not only a salad on the side.

Why it tastes this way

Parsley and onion lighten lamb fat, and the grill dries the surface so the herbs do not taste steamed. A little allspice is a Levantine pantry note. Charcoal smoke ties it to the other skewers on a mixed grill without making it the same as those skewers. The green flecks are how you know the mix.

How it is made

Mix minced lamb with parsley and onion, mold it onto skewers, and grill until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the onion dry so the mince does not fall off the skewer. Keep the parsley fine, or it will char in strands.

How it is used

With salad, pickles, and rice. Hummus on the side would add chickpeas and often tahini, which is sesame, so it is not in this recipe.

Field notes

  • Lebanese kafta mixes parsley and onion into minced lamb before it is grilled.
  • It is a different mince from Iranian koobideh and from Adana kebab.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb
  • 1 small onion, grated and squeezed dry
  • 1 cup parsley, finely chopped
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon allspice
  • 4 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the lamb, onion, parsley, salt, and allspice until the meat holds together.
  2. Mold onto skewers in a long shape. Heat the grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Serve with salad. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A mix of beef and lamb, still ground.
  • Patties grilled without skewers, cooked to the same temperature.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Tomato salad
  • Pickles
  • Rice
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Baghdad Masgouf

Carp with salt, tamarind, olive oil, and the smoke of a fire beside the fish.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Masgouf is a Tigris fish cook: carp, split open and staked or set beside an open fire in Baghdad, then finished with a brush of sauce. Riverside restaurants made it famous, and families cook it when they can get the fish. If carp is hard to buy, a firm white fish may be eaten as a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf. This recipe is written for carp.

Cultural background

Baghdad cooks along the Tigris are the reference. The dish is carp and fire, not a fried fish and not a stew. A different species does not inherit the name.

Why it tastes this way

Carp is rich, and standing it beside the fire, rather than over a hard flame, renders some of that fat slowly and smokes the skin. Tamarind and salt cut the oil. Olive oil at the end shines it. The river fish and the slow side-fire are the taste, which is why a random white fillet in a pan is a different dinner.

How it is made

Split a carp open, salt it, and cook it beside an open fire until it flakes, then brush it with tamarind and oil.

Authentic touch. Keep the carp beside the fire, skin toward the heat, and do not rush it. If you must use another fish, say so and do not call the plate masgouf.

How it is used

With onion and tomato. The fish is the plate.

Field notes

  • Masgouf is Baghdad's grilled carp, a Tigris fish cook set beside the fire.
  • A firm white fish can be a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole carp, about 3 lb, split open and scaled
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon tamarind paste
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 1 onion, sliced

Steps

  1. Pat the carp dry and salt it. Mix tamarind, olive oil, and turmeric into a brush.
  2. Build an open fire. Set the carp skin-side toward the heat, beside the flames rather than in them, on a rack or stakes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes. Brush with the tamarind mixture near the end.
  4. Serve with onion. This recipe is for carp. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A covered grill with the fish on a rack away from direct flame, still using carp.
  • A little turmeric in the brush, which some cooks add.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • Onion
  • Tomato
  • Lemon
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Iranian Koobideh

Minced lamb and onion, salted, with a smoky grilled crust.

30 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Koobideh is an Iranian minced-lamb kebab pressed on a wide skewer. The name is widely linked to the verb for pounding, because the mince is worked until it stays on the metal. Onion and salt are the core seasoning. It is restaurant and home food in Tehran and across Iran. It is not the saffron chicken kebab, and it is not Lebanese kafta with a handful of parsley. The meat is ground and cooked through.

Cultural background

Iranian kebab houses grill koobideh next to barg and joojeh. This page is only the mince. It does not use a chile-heavy Adana mix.

Why it tastes this way

Grated onion kept in the mince makes the kebab moist and slightly sweet, and charcoal dries the outside so it does not taste boiled. Salt is enough because the lamb is the flavor. Working the meat changes the texture: it holds, and it bites smoothly. That texture is as important as any spice.

How it is made

Knead minced lamb with onion and salt, form it on wide skewers, and grill until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Grate the onion and squeeze out a little liquid so the kebab is moist but not soggy. Press it onto a wide skewer with wet hands.

How it is used

With rice, grilled tomato, and raw onion. Sumac is a common sprinkle.

Field notes

  • Koobideh is minced lamb worked until it holds on a wide skewer.
  • The name is widely linked to pounding, which describes how the meat is prepared.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb
  • 1 small onion, grated
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • A pinch of baking soda
  • 4 wide flat metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the lamb, onion, salt, pepper, and baking soda until the paste is sticky. Chill 20 minutes.
  2. Press onto wide skewers. Heat charcoal until it is hot.
  3. Grill, turning often, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Serve with rice and tomato. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A blend of beef and lamb, still ground.
  • A pinch of turmeric, which some home cooks add.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Rice
  • Grilled tomato
  • Raw onion
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Iranian Joojeh Kabab

Saffron chicken with lemon and onion, charred at the edges.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Joojeh kabab is saffron chicken on skewers, a standard Iranian grill beside the minced lamb. Saffron is the point, bloomed and rubbed into the meat, not a thread scattered on the plate after cooking. Lemon and onion are the usual partners. The dish is poultry and is cooked through. This version stays dairy-free. A dairy marinade would be a different recipe.

Cultural background

Iranian kebab cooks grill joojeh for people who want chicken rather than lamb. Without saffron it is just grilled chicken, so the spice is not optional on this page.

Why it tastes this way

Saffron's floral, slightly bitter note clings to chicken fat and smells stronger once the surface browns. Lemon keeps it sharp. Onion sweetens the marinade. Charcoal adds smoke that a pan cannot. The color is a side effect of the flavor, not the reason to buy the spice.

How it is made

Marinate chicken in bloomed saffron, lemon, and onion, then grill on skewers until the poultry is cooked.

Authentic touch. Grind the saffron with a pinch of salt and bloom it in warm lemon juice before it touches the chicken. Do not toast it until it tastes like hay.

How it is used

With rice and grilled tomato.

Field notes

  • Joojeh kabab is Iranian saffron chicken on skewers.
  • Saffron is the point of the marinade, not a last-minute garnish.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb boneless chicken thigh, cut in cubes
  • 1/2 teaspoon saffron threads
  • 1 lemon, juiced
  • 1 small onion, grated
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 4 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Grind the saffron with a pinch of the salt and stir it into the lemon juice. Mix with onion, the rest of the salt, and oil. Add the chicken and refrigerate 30 minutes.
  2. Thread onto skewers. Heat the grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest cube reaches 165°F (74°C).
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve with rice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thighs instead of breast, which stay moister after they reach temperature.
  • A dairy marinade, which would be a different recipe from this dairy-free one.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • Rice
  • Grilled tomato
  • Lemon
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Omani Shuwa

Lamb with cumin, coriander, and chili, slow-cooked until the fat softens.

30 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Shuwa is lamb cooked for Eid in an earth oven in Oman. The meat is rubbed with spice, wrapped, and buried with fire until it is very tender. A home oven is not a pit. This page uses a covered pot in an oven and says so. The Omani mix is often cumin, coriander, chili, and sometimes a shop blend. There is no single secret formula. The rub below is a simple published-style mix of those spices.

Cultural background

Omani households prepare shuwa for Eid al-Adha and other gatherings. The earth oven is the traditional pot. The home cook here is not digging a pit. It is not a quick grill.

Why it tastes this way

A long, enclosed cook turns lamb fat and spice into one smell: cumin and coriander stay earthy, and chili warms rather than chars. The pit does this by trapping steam and smoke. A covered oven pot keeps the moisture and loses the buried-fire smoke, which is an honest trade. Shop blends vary, so the taste at two houses will not match, and that is normal.

How it is made

Rub a lamb shoulder with a simple cumin, coriander, and chili mix, wrap it, and cook it covered in a home oven until it is safe and tender.

Authentic touch. Wrap the lamb so the spice stays on the meat, and do not uncover the pot for the first half of the cook. Call it a home oven, not a pit.

How it is used

With rice. It is Eid food for the families who make it, pulled or sliced.

Field notes

  • Omani shuwa is Eid lamb cooked in an earth oven, and a home oven is not that pit.
  • Cumin, coriander, and chili are a common mix, and shop blends vary. There is no single secret formula.

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb shoulder, about 5 lb
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 4 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Mix cumin, coriander, chili, salt, garlic, and oil. Rub the shoulder. This is a simple spice mix, not a secret formula. Wrap the meat in parchment and then foil.
  2. Set it in a covered pot. Heat a home oven to 325°F (165°C). This is a home oven, not an earth pit.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest. For a shoulder you want to pull, continue toward 195°F (90°C).
  4. Rest, then slice or pull. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A shop spice blend from an Omani grocer, used in place of the simple mix and named on the jar.
  • Goat shoulder with the same method, still a whole-muscle roast.

Yemeni Chicken Mandi

Chicken and rice with a hawaij-style spice of cumin, coriander, turmeric, and pepper.

30 min prep · 1 hr 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Mandi is a Yemeni dish of meat and rice cooked in a pit or a taboon, now also made in homes and restaurants far beyond Yemen. The pit is traditional, and a covered oven pot is the home version. This page uses chicken and a simple hawaij-style spice. It does not assign the dish to one community as an owner. Lamb is the other common meat. Chicken, as poultry, is cooked to 165°F.

Cultural background

Yemeni families and the cooks who learned the dish from them make mandi for gatherings. The home pot is labeled. It is not a quick grill and not a single household's secret.

Why it tastes this way

Rice cooked under the meat takes the drippings and the spice, which is why mandi is a pot and not only a roasted bird. Cumin, coriander, turmeric, and black pepper make a straightforward hawaij-style mix; real blends vary by shop and house. A pit adds smoke a home oven only suggests. The chicken stays the protein, and the rice is the rest of the plate.

How it is made

Rub chicken with a simple spice mix, brown it, and cook it with rice in a covered oven pot until the poultry is done.

Authentic touch. Brown the chicken first so the rice has some roasted drippings. Keep the pot covered. Do not claim a pit you did not dig.

How it is used

Chicken over the rice, with a salad. It feeds a group from one pot.

Field notes

  • The pit or taboon is the traditional mandi cook, and a covered oven pot is the home version.
  • This page does not assign mandi to one community.

Ingredients

  • 6 chicken thighs, bone in
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 cups long-grain rice, rinsed
  • 3 cups water
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Mix cumin, coriander, turmeric, pepper, and salt. Rub half on the chicken. This is a simple hawaij-style mix, not a claim of one correct blend.
  2. Brown the chicken in oil in an oven-safe pot. Add onion, rice, the rest of the spice, and water. Set the chicken on the rice and cover the pot. A covered oven pot is the home version; the pit is traditional.
  3. Bake at 375°F (190°C) until the thickest thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), not touching bone, and the rice is tender.
  4. Rest 10 minutes covered. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lamb shoulder instead of chicken, cooked as a whole muscle to at least 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest, and longer if you want it tender.
  • A shop hawaij, checked for its own allergens, in place of the simple mix.

Egyptian Kofta

Minced beef and lamb with parsley, onion, and a grilled crust.

25 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Egyptian kofta is grilled minced beef or lamb, mixed with parsley, onion, and spices, and cooked over charcoal in Cairo and elsewhere. It is a different plate from Moroccan kefta brochettes, which have their own spice and skewer habit. The Egyptian mix here leans on parsley, cumin, and coriander. The meat is ground and cooked through. It is street and home food, not a festival invention.

Cultural background

Egyptian households and grill shops cook kofta as ordinary meat. North Africa is the region for this page. It is not raw kibbeh and not a meatball in tomato sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Parsley and onion cut the fat of a beef-and-lamb mince, and cumin and coriander toast on the grill. Charcoal gives a bitter-brown edge that pan frying only approximates. The blend is familiar across Egyptian grills and still not the same as a Moroccan brochette heavy on paprika. Ground meat needs a cooked center, which sets the texture.

How it is made

Mix minced beef and lamb with herbs and spices, form sausages or patties, and grill them until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Grate and squeeze the onion. If the mix is wet, the kofta will slump through the grate.

How it is used

With salad, tahini sauce if you eat sesame, and rice. Tahini is not in this recipe, so sesame is not an allergen here.

Field notes

  • Egyptian kofta is grilled minced beef or lamb with parsley and onion.
  • It is a different plate from Moroccan kefta brochettes.

Ingredients

  • 1 lb ground beef
  • 1/2 lb ground lamb
  • 1 small onion, grated and squeezed dry
  • 1 cup parsley, finely chopped
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the beef, lamb, onion, parsley, cumin, coriander, and salt until the meat holds together.
  2. Form eight short sausages. Heat a grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Serve with salad. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All beef or all lamb, still ground.
  • On skewers, which is a shape change, not a different spice mix.
