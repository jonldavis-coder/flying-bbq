Central Texas Salt-and-Pepper Brisket

Beefy and peppery, with a dark bark and a slice that is smoky rather than sweet.

30 min prep · 12 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

In meat-market towns such as Lockhart, German- and Czech-speaking butchers smoked beef, including the tough chest cut that became brisket, over post oak. African American pitmasters worked those fires and ran their own pits, so the craft is both a Central European butcher tradition and a Black Texas one. Salt and coarse black pepper became the seasoning that defines the style. Sauce, if a market offers it, is optional and is not the point.

Cultural background

This is sliced Central Texas brisket, not East Texas chopped beef in tomato sauce and not a Kansas City burnt end. The cook is a long indirect smoke.

Why it tastes this way

Post oak burns milder than mesquite, so the smoke stays in the background while salt and coarse pepper make the bark. A long cook renders the fat cap and softens collagen that a steak temperature would leave chewy. The dry Central Texas air and an offset pit are why the surface can darken without steaming.

How it is made

Season a whole brisket with salt and coarse pepper only, then smoke it indirectly over post oak until the flat is tender.

Authentic touch. Leave the fat cap about a quarter inch thick, and do not wrap the brisket until the bark is the color you want.

How it is used

Sliced across the grain, with the point and flat both on the board. Sauce stays on the side if it is there at all. White bread, raw onion, and pickles are the usual companions.

Field notes

  • Post oak is the wood Central Texas pitmasters usually name for this kind of brisket.
  • Lockhart is one of the meat-market towns associated with salt-and-pepper beef.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole beef brisket, about 12 pounds, fat cap trimmed to about 1/4 inch
  • 1/4 cup kosher salt
  • 1/4 cup coarse black pepper
  • Post oak chunks, or other oak if post oak is unavailable
  • Water in a spray bottle

Steps

  1. Mix the salt and pepper and coat the brisket on all sides. Refrigerate it if you season it ahead, and do not leave it on the counter.
  2. Heat an offset smoker or a covered grill for indirect cooking to about 250°F (121°C). Burn post oak, or another oak. Keep the fire clean rather than smoldering wet chips.
  3. Smoke the brisket fat cap up until the thickest part of the flat reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance. Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; brisket is taken higher so it will slice instead of bounce. The stall, often around 150–170°F (65–77°C), is evaporative cooling.
  4. Rest the brisket at least 30 minutes before slicing across the grain. A 3-minute rest is the safety minimum; a large brisket needs longer. Serve sauce on the side only if you want it.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A butcher paper wrap during the stall keeps the bark drier than foil.
  • The point can be cubed later for burnt ends, which is a Kansas City plate rather than this one.

  • pickles

Central Texas Beef Plate Ribs

Deep beef flavor, black pepper, and oak smoke on a heavy bone.

20 min prep · 8 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Beef plate ribs are the heavy short ribs from the lower chest, smoked in the same Central Texas meat-market style as brisket. Salt and coarse pepper are the seasoning. They are a beef plate, not pork spareribs, and they take the same kind of low, indirect fire.

Cultural background

These are Central Texas beef ribs. They are not Memphis pork ribs and not a quick grill of thin short ribs.

Why it tastes this way

The plate is a well-used muscle with a thick fat cap, so a long oak smoke renders it the way brisket renders. Pepper browns into the bark. Post oak or another oak keeps the smoke from covering the beef.

How it is made

Salt and pepper a beef plate and smoke it indirectly until the meat relaxes on the bone.

Authentic touch. Buy a three-bone plate if you can, and trim only loose fat. A single thin rib will be done long before a plate section.

How it is used

Cut into individual bones and set on butcher paper. Sauce is optional and off to the side.

Field notes

  • A beef plate rib is a different cut from a pork sparerib, with a much heavier bone.
  • Central Texas seasoning for these ribs is salt and coarse pepper, the same idea as brisket.

Ingredients

  • 1 rack beef plate ribs, about 5 to 7 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper
  • Oak or post oak wood for the fire

Steps

  1. Trim loose surface fat. Coat the ribs with salt and pepper.
  2. Set a smoker or covered grill for indirect heat around 250°F (121°C) and burn oak.
  3. Smoke until the meat has pulled back along the bones and the thickest part is about 195–203°F (90–95°C). Beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest; these ribs are cooked longer so the connective tissue softens.
  4. Rest at least 10 minutes, then cut between the bones. Keep any sauce off the meat unless someone asks for it.

Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest. For tender ribs, cook until the meat has pulled back from the ends of the bones, often around 195–203°F (90–95°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Oak is the usual wood; hickory is a stronger substitute if oak is scarce.

Pairings and uses

  • Beef
  • white bread
  • pickles
  • raw onion
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Central Texas Smoked Sausage

Coarse beef and pork, black pepper, and a clean oak smoke.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Sausage sat in the same Central Texas meat markets that smoked brisket. German- and Czech-speaking butchers brought coarse smoked links, and the pits kept that case going. This recipe starts from fresh ground beef and pork. It is not a curing recipe, and it does not use pink curing salt.

Cultural background

A fresh sausage cooked the day it is mixed, in the peppery Central Texas style. It is not a dried summer sausage and not an East Texas hot link in sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Coarse grind and black pepper are the butcher-shop signature. Oak smoke seasons the surface without a sweet glaze. The beef keeps the link meaty beside the pork fat.

How it is made

Mix a fresh beef-and-pork sausage, then smoke it only until it reaches a safe internal temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep the mixture cold while you mix it, form thick ropes, and cook them the same day. Do not hold raw sausage at room temperature.

How it is used

Sliced on butcher paper beside brisket, or in a piece with mustard if you want it. Mustard would add a mustard allergen that this mix does not have.

Field notes

  • Elgin is one of the Central Texas towns known for market sausage as well as smoked beef.
  • This link is cooked from fresh meat to 160°F. It is not dry-cured.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds ground beef
  • 1 pound ground pork
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon coarse black pepper
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • Oak wood for the smoker

Steps

  1. Keep the meat cold. Mix beef, pork, salt, pepper, and garlic just until the seasoning is spread. Do not add curing salt.
  2. Form thick ropes or fat patties. Refrigerate them while the fire heats, and cook them the same day.
  3. Smoke or cook over indirect heat at about 250°F (121°C) until the center of each link reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest a few minutes and slice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All-beef sausage is a fair butcher's choice if you would rather not use pork.

Pairings and uses

  • brisket
  • saltine crackers
  • raw onion

East Texas Chopped Beef

Smoked beef chopped into a thinner tomato sauce, tangy and a little sweet.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

East Texas chopped beef is a sauced plate, different from Central Texas brisket, where pepper and oak are the whole story and sauce is optional. In the Piney Woods the beef is often chopped and mixed with a thinner tomato sauce. This version keeps that distinction: smoke the beef, then dress it.

Cultural background

Chopped beef in a loose tomato sauce for sandwiches or plates. It is not a Lockhart slice and not a thick Kansas City burnt-end glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Hickory or oak smoke gives the beef a deeper edge, and the sauce loosens the chop so it eats like a sandwich filling. Tomato and vinegar cut the fat. The sauce is thinner than a molasses-heavy city sauce, which is the East Texas point.

How it is made

Smoke a brisket or beef roast, chop it, and moisten it with a thin tomato-vinegar sauce.

Authentic touch. Chop the beef after it rests, then fold in only enough sauce to moisten it. The meat should not swim.

How it is used

On a bun or on a plate with the extra sauce in a cup. It is a chopped sandwich as often as a sliced platter.

Field notes

  • East Texas chopped beef is sauced on purpose, which separates it from salt-and-pepper Central Texas brisket.
  • The sauce in this plate is kept thin so the chopped beef stays loose rather than candy-thick.

Ingredients

  • 1 beef brisket flat or chuck roast, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 8 hamburger buns

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the beef. Smoke it indirectly over hickory or oak at about 250°F (121°C).
  2. Cook until it is probe-tender, about 195–205°F (90–96°C). The roast is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; chopped barbecue beef is cooked longer so it will break apart.
  3. Simmer tomato, vinegar, brown sugar, and onion until the onion softens, about 15 minutes. Taste for salt.
  4. Rest the beef, chop it, and fold in enough sauce to moisten. Serve on buns, which contain wheat, so this plate is not gluten-free.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A chuck roast can stand in for brisket if that is the cut you can buy.

Pairings and uses

  • Beef
  • Sandwiches
  • hamburger buns
  • onion
  • pickles
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

West Texas Mesquite Beef

Seared beef with the sharp, sweet smoke of mesquite.

15 min prep · 1 hr 15 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

West Texas beef over mesquite is a direct-heat cook. Mesquite is the scrub wood of the dry country, and it burns hot. This is not the long offset smoke of a Lockhart brisket, and it should not be stretched into an all-day cook or the outside will bitter before the center is done.

Cultural background

A sirloin roast grilled over mesquite coals. It is a hot-fire beef plate, not Central Texas barbecue and not a smoked sausage.

Why it tastes this way

Mesquite coals run hotter and sweeter-bitter than oak, so the crust picks up a sharper smoke in a short cook. Direct heat browns the surface the way an open West Texas fire does. Salt and pepper are enough because the wood is already loud.

How it is made

Grill a salted beef roast over mesquite coals with the lid managed so the fire stays hot but not flaming.

Authentic touch. Wait until the mesquite has burned down to coals. Flaming mesquite will scorch the roast and taste harsh.

How it is used

Sliced after a rest, with the juices. It can sit beside beans, but it is not chopped into sauce.

Field notes

  • Mesquite burns hot, which is why this West Texas cook is direct heat rather than a long offset smoke.
  • A sirloin roast at this temperature stays sliceable, unlike a brisket taken to tenderness.

Ingredients

  • 1 beef sirloin roast, about 3 pounds
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 2 teaspoons coarse black pepper
  • Mesquite wood or mesquite charcoal, burned down to coals

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the roast and let it sit at room temperature no more than a short while before cooking.
  2. Burn mesquite to coals. Spread the coals for direct grilling and keep a cooler edge.
  3. Grill the roast, turning as the crust browns, until the center reaches 145°F (63°C).
  4. Rest at least 3 minutes before slicing. Do not cook this roast to brisket tenderness or it will dry out.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thick sirloin steaks use the same coals and cook in less time than a roast.

Pairings and uses

  • Beef
  • charred onion
  • salsa
  • beans

South Texas Barbacoa de Cabeza

Soft, beefy cheek with onion, cumin, and dried chile, meant to be shredded.

30 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Mexican barbacoa is a pit cook, and in much of central Mexico the meat was wrapped with maguey leaves over heated stones. South Texas barbacoa de cabeza is the beef-head version of that pit tradition, closely tied to Mexican and Mexican American cooking along the border. This recipe is a covered braise of beef cheek, the home version of that tenderness, not a pit. It does not claim that a single Indigenous nation invented the modern cheek recipe.

Cultural background

A home covered braise for beef cheek in the South Texas barbacoa de cabeza tradition. It is not a Lockhart brisket and not a whole-head pit cook.

Why it tastes this way

Cheek is full of collagen, so a covered pot turns it silky instead of searing it like a steak. Cumin, dried chile, and onion are market seasonings of northern Mexico and South Texas. The maguey-and-pit aroma of a real earth oven is not something a home braise copies.

How it is made

Braise seasoned beef cheeks in a covered pot or oven until they shred, as a home version of pit barbacoa.

Authentic touch. If beef cheek is unavailable, use chuck and say so. Chuck is a workable home stand-in, not the same cut as cachete.

How it is used

Chopped or shredded, with onion, cilantro, and salsa, often in a warm tortilla. The tortillas are corn in this recipe.

Field notes

  • Barbacoa de cabeza means head barbecue, and the cheek is the part most home cooks can actually buy.
  • The Spanish word barbacoa comes from a Taíno word for a stick framework, which is a word history, not a single recipe for beef cheek.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds beef cheeks, or chuck roast if cheeks are unavailable
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon ground cumin
  • 2 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 2 cups water

Steps

  1. Toast the guajillos in a dry pan until fragrant, then soak them in hot water until soft and blend them with a little of that water, the cumin, and garlic.
  2. Salt the cheeks, coat them with the chile puree, and put them in a heavy pot with onion, bay, and water. This covered braise is the home version, not a maguey-lined pit.
  3. Cover and cook in a 325°F (163°C) oven or over low heat until the cheek shreds and the center is about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Beef offal is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; cheek needs the higher temperature for tenderness.
  4. Rest 10 minutes, shred, and taste for salt. Discard the bay leaf.

Cook beef offal to at least 145°F (63°C) with a rest. Cook poultry offal, including chicken hearts and livers, to 165°F (74°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chuck roast, cooked the same covered way, is the workable substitute when cheek is not sold nearby.

  • corn tortillas
  • cilantro

Eastern North Carolina Whole Hog

Chopped pork, sharp with vinegar and red pepper, both lean and fatty in each bite.

40 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings from a home shoulder and belly

History

Eastern North Carolina barbecue is whole-hog cooking, chopped and dressed with vinegar and pepper, with no tomato in the sauce. The recipe here is a home version that uses a pork shoulder and a strip of pork belly, labeled as such because most kitchens cannot cook a whole hog. Enslaved pitmasters did this labor, and Black pitmasters carried it into the public trade, while the vinegar habit fits English and Scots-Irish settlers. No single inventor owns the plate.

Cultural background

Chopped eastern Carolina hog in a vinegar-pepper sauce. The written cook is shoulder plus belly, not a whole animal, and it is not Lexington shoulder.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar cuts hog fat in a humid climate, and cayenne and black pepper were the heat on hand. Mixing a little belly into shoulder copies the way a whole hog eats, with fat against lean. The sauce is the seasoning, not a glaze.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and a piece of belly, chop them together, and dress the chop with vinegar and pepper.

Authentic touch. Chop the pork while it is still warm and dress it with sauce. Do not leave the meat in dry chunks.

How it is used

Piled on a tray or a bun with more sauce on the table. Coleslaw beside it is the vinegar kind, not a mayonnaise slaw.

Field notes

  • Eastern North Carolina sauce is vinegar and pepper, without tomato, which is how it differs from Lexington dip.
  • A whole hog was plantation and public-pit work. This page uses shoulder and belly so a home cook can make the same chopped plate.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 7 pounds
  • 1 pound pork belly, skin removed
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt the shoulder and belly. Heat a smoker for indirect cooking at about 250°F (121°C) with hickory or oak.
  2. Stir vinegar, red pepper, black pepper, and the sauce salt. This sauce has no tomato.
  3. Smoke the meats until the shoulder reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and shreds. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; chopped hog is cooked hotter so the collagen softens. The belly should be tender and hot throughout.
  4. Rest the meats, chop shoulder and belly together, and fold in sauce until the chop is moist. Serve the rest of the sauce on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Shoulder alone is acceptable if belly is hard to find; the chop will be a little leaner.

Pairings and uses

  • hush puppies

Lexington Pork Shoulder

Smoked pork shoulder chopped and dressed with a vinegar dip that has a little tomato.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Lexington, in the North Carolina Piedmont, cooks pork shoulder rather than a whole hog, and the dip is vinegar reddened with a little tomato or ketchup. That is a different plate from eastern Carolina whole hog, which leaves the tomato out. Pitmasters in the Piedmont, many of them Black, made this the town style. A single inventor is not something this recipe claims.

Cultural background

Piedmont shoulder with Lexington dip. It is not eastern vinegar-only hog and not a thick Memphis or Kansas City sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder fat stands up to vinegar, and a small amount of tomato rounds the dip without turning it into a sweet glaze. Hickory smoke is common in this part of the state. The red slaw beside it uses the same dip, so the plate tastes like one idea.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and chop or pull it, then dress it with a thin vinegar dip that contains a little ketchup.

Authentic touch. Use only enough ketchup to blush the vinegar. If the dip tastes like tomato sauce, it has left Lexington.

How it is used

Chopped on a plate or a tray, with red slaw and more dip. It is often a sandwich, but the bun is optional.

Field notes

  • Lexington dip contains a little tomato. Eastern North Carolina sauce does not.
  • The Piedmont plate is pork shoulder, not the whole hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the dip
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder. Smoke it indirectly over hickory at about 250°F (121°C).
  2. Stir vinegar, ketchup, red pepper, sauce salt, and water into a thin dip. It should look blush-red, not thick.
  3. Cook the shoulder to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), until it pulls. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; this shoulder goes further for tenderness.
  4. Rest, chop, and moisten with dip. Serve extra dip on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pulled in large pieces is fine; many Lexington plates are chopped finer.

South Carolina Midlands Mustard Pork

Smoked pork shoulder with a tangy, yellow mustard sauce.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

The South Carolina Midlands are known for pork shoulder dressed with a mustard sauce. That style is widely linked to German settlement in the region. The link is an association, not a proven single origin, and this page does not name an inventor. It is a different sauce from eastern Carolina vinegar and from Lexington's tomato dip.

Cultural background

Midlands pork with mustard sauce. It is not a Carolina vinegar hog and not a hash, which is the stew served beside some South Carolina pits.

Why it tastes this way

Yellow mustard brings acid and a sharp edge that cuts shoulder fat, and a little sugar keeps it from tasting raw. Smoke from the pit sits under the sauce because the sauce goes on after the shoulder is tender. The yellow color is the regional signal on the plate.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder, then dress the chopped meat with a mustard, vinegar, and sugar sauce.

Authentic touch. Sauce the chopped pork, and also send a bowl of sauce to the table. Do not paint it on so early that the sugars burn.

How it is used

Chopped, with extra mustard sauce, often beside rice and hash or a simple slaw.

Field notes

  • Midlands mustard sauce is widely linked to German settlement, which is not the same as a documented single inventor.
  • The sauce is yellow mustard, so this plate is a different Carolina barbecue from the vinegar hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 cup yellow mustard
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
