Appalachian Smoke

Hickory shoulder, sorghum chops, a bought country-ham steak, and the pots that sit beside them.

Appalachian stops in this book are the ones that can be tied to a real smoke or a real pot: hickory shoulder, sorghum-glazed chops, ramp-season trout, a purchased country-ham steak, vinegar chicken, a holiday turkey, chow-chow, soup beans, and skillet cornbread.

Scots-Irish and English settlers brought pork and vinegar habits into the mountains. German-speaking families brought sweet-sour pickling. Black Appalachian cooks worked the same farms and kitchens. Cherokee and other Native nations of these mountains had their own fire and corn long before those settlements. This book does not hand them a modern pulled-pork recipe.

Country ham here is a cured product you buy. The page cooks a steak of it. It does not teach home curing.

Appalachian Hickory Pork Shoulder

Pork shoulder with a clean hickory smoke, salt, and black pepper.

25 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

In the mountains of east Tennessee, West Virginia, and western North Carolina, hickory is a common smoke wood for a pork shoulder. This plate is that mountain smoke, salted and peppered, not a single-town sauce style. Scots-Irish and English settlers, Black Appalachian cooks, and Cherokee and other Native nations of the mountains do not all share one recipe, so this page claims only the hickory shoulder a home cook can document.

Cultural background

A hickory-smoked pork shoulder from the southern Appalachians. It is not eastern Carolina whole hog and not a sorghum glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Hickory is a hardwood of these ridges, and its smoke is stronger than a fruit wood. Salt and pepper let that smoke stay the main flavor. A long cook softens the shoulder the same way a lowland pit does, but the wood is the mountain part.

How it is made

Salt and pepper a pork shoulder and smoke it over hickory until it pulls.

Authentic touch. Use hickory if you can name the wood. Oak is the honest substitute. Do not soak the chunks until they smolder bitter.

How it is used

Pulled or chopped, with the juices. A vinegar splash is optional and is not required for this plate.

Field notes

  • Hickory grows through the southern Appalachians and is the smoke wood this shoulder uses.
  • This is a mountain pork shoulder, separate from the vinegar whole-hog plate of eastern North Carolina.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • Hickory wood, or oak if hickory is unavailable

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder and let it stand in the refrigerator while the fire starts.
  2. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) over hickory.
  3. Cook to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), until a probe slides in. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; a pulled shoulder continues higher so the collagen softens.
  4. Rest, then pull or chop. Keep the juices.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of the vinegar-pepper sauce from eastern Carolina can be served on the side without changing the smoke.

Appalachian Sorghum Pork Chops

Seared pork chops with a late, tangy sorghum glaze.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Sorghum syrup is a mountain sweetener pressed from sweet sorghum, a different plant from sugarcane. Hill kitchens used it where cane molasses was a trade good rather than a local mill product. These chops are a home grill plate glazed with that syrup. The glaze goes on late so the sugars do not burn.

Cultural background

Grilled pork chops finished with sorghum. This is mountain home cooking, not a pit shoulder and not a honey glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Sorghum tastes sharper and greener than cane molasses, so a thin glaze seasons the chop without turning it into candy. Direct heat browns the fat. Late glazing is what keeps the syrup from scorching on a hot grate.

How it is made

Grill thick pork chops and brush on a sorghum and vinegar glaze only at the end.

Authentic touch. Brush the sorghum on in the last minute or two. If it blackens, the fire was too hot or the glaze went on too soon.

How it is used

Whole chops, with the glaze shiny but not burnt. Greens or cornbread can sit beside them.

Field notes

  • Sorghum syrup is pressed from sweet sorghum, not from sugarcane.
  • The glaze goes on late because sorghum sugar burns on a hot grill.

Ingredients

  • 4 bone-in pork chops, about 1 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons sorghum syrup
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the chops. Heat a grill for direct cooking with a cooler edge.
  2. Stir sorghum and vinegar. Keep the glaze off the chops while they sear.
  3. Grill until the center of each chop reaches 145°F (63°C). Rest at least 3 minutes.
  4. Brush with glaze over the cooler edge for about a minute, then serve. Do not let the syrup flame.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cane molasses is a different sweetener. Use it only if sorghum is unavailable, and say you substituted.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • skillet cornbread
  • collard greens
  • soup beans
  • Gluten-free

Ramp-Season Smoked Trout

Smoked trout with the garlic-onion smell of spring ramps.

20 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Ramps are Allium tricoccum, a wild leek of the eastern mountains, dug in spring. This plate is home cooking: trout with ramps, smoked or grilled. It is not a Cherokee ceremony, and it is not a claim on anyone's seasonal rite. Harvest ramps only where it is legal, and do not pull a whole patch.

Cultural background

A spring home plate of trout and ramps. Cherokee and other mountain nations have their own foods; this recipe does not speak for them.

Why it tastes this way

Ramps are sharper than planted onion and milder than raw garlic, so a few leaves season an oily trout without a sauce. Smoke clings to the fish skin. Spring is the only honest season for the plant, which is why the plate is not a year-round pantry dish.

How it is made

Hot-smoke or grill trout and toss it with ramps that have been wilted in a little oil.

Authentic touch. Use a few ramps, not a sack. If you cannot harvest legally, use a small amount of cultivated leek and do not call it ramps.

How it is used

Flaked warm, with the greens. Bread is optional and would add wheat.

Field notes

  • Ramps are a wild allium, Allium tricoccum, and digging them is illegal in some parks and on some private land.
  • This is a home cook of trout and ramps, not a Cherokee ceremony.

Ingredients

  • 4 small trout, dressed, or 1 1/2 pounds trout fillets
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 6 ramps, cleaned, or 1 small leek if ramps cannot be harvested legally
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Confirm the ramps were harvested legally. Rinse them well and slice the bulbs and leaves.
  2. Salt and pepper the trout. Wilt the ramps in oil in a pan until soft, then take them off the heat.
  3. Hot-smoke or grill the trout over indirect or gentle direct heat until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh flakes.
  4. Spoon the ramps over the fish and serve. This is home cooking, not a ceremony.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hot grill instead of a smoker works if you still cook the fish to temperature.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • cornbread
  • lemon
  • boiled potatoes
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Country Ham Steak

Salty, dry-cured ham with a browned edge.

15 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Country ham is a salt-cured, aged ham sold by producers in Virginia, Kentucky, Tennessee, North Carolina, and nearby states. The cure is the product you buy. This page does not give a curing recipe, a nitrate schedule, or aging times. It only cooks a steak cut from that ham on a griddle.

Cultural background

A purchased country-ham steak, fried. The cure happened before you bought it. This is not a fresh pork chop.

Why it tastes this way

Salt and time concentrate the pork, so a thin steak tastes intense after a short fry. A little surface browning is the cook's part. The mountain and upper-South climate of traditional hams was cool storage, which is the producer's work, not a step in this skillet.

How it is made

Fry a purchased country-ham steak on a griddle or in a skillet until it is heated through.

Authentic touch. Rinse or soak the steak briefly in cold water if it is too salty for you. That is a cook's rinse, not a cure. Pat it dry before it hits the pan.

How it is used

Sliced or whole, often with biscuits or eggs at breakfast. Biscuits and some egg dishes would add wheat and egg, which this steak recipe does not include.

Field notes

  • Country ham is salt-cured before you buy it. This page does not give a cure.
  • A steak from that ham is a fry, not a long smoke.

Ingredients

  • 4 purchased country-ham steaks, about 1/2 inch thick
  • Cold water, for a brief rinse
  • 1 teaspoon neutral oil, if the steaks are very lean
  • Black pepper to taste

Steps

  1. Buy the ham already cured from a producer. Do not try to cure one from these instructions.
  2. Rinse the steaks or soak them 10 minutes in cold water, then pat them dry. This only pulls a little surface salt.
  3. Heat a plancha or skillet and cook the steaks until the center reaches 145°F (63°C).
  4. Rest at least 3 minutes. Pepper them if you want. They are already salty.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Red-eye gravy, made by loosening the pan with black coffee, is a common companion and is still not a cure.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • biscuits
  • grits
  • eggs
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Appalachian Vinegar Chicken

Grilled half chickens, sharp with vinegar and black pepper.

20 min prep · 55 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Half chickens with a vinegar mop are a mountain cookout habit in the southern Appalachians, kin to the region's vinegar-and-pepper seasoning for pork but cooked as poultry. This mop has no egg and no mayonnaise. It is not Cornell chicken and not Alabama white sauce.

Cultural background

Grilled half chickens basted with vinegar, salt, and pepper. A mountain plate, not a campus baste and not a Decatur sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and pepper cut chicken fat the way they cut hog fat in Carolina pits, and the mountains had vinegar on hand as a preserve and a seasoning. Direct and indirect heat together brown the skin without burning the mop. There is no sugar to scorch.

How it is made

Grill half chickens over coals and baste with a vinegar, salt, and pepper mop.

Authentic touch. Mop over a slightly cooler part of the grill. Vinegar on a roaring flame mostly evaporates and does not season the meat.

How it is used

Half a chicken on the plate, with more mop if you want it sharper.

Field notes

  • This mop is vinegar, salt, and pepper, with no egg and no mayonnaise.
  • It is an Appalachian cookout chicken, distinct from Cornell's egg baste and Alabama's white sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 chickens, halved, about 4 pounds each
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 cup water
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the mop
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper

Steps

  1. Salt the chicken halves. Stir vinegar, water, pepper, mop salt, and red pepper.
  2. Heat a grill with a hot side and a cooler side.
  3. Cook the chickens mostly on the cooler side, mopping now and then, until the thickest part of the thigh, not touching bone, reaches 165°F (74°C).
  4. Rest a few minutes. Do not serve the chicken rare, and discard mop that sat against raw chicken unless you boil it.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch more cayenne makes the mop hotter. The base stays vinegar.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • skillet cornbread
  • soup beans
  • cucumber
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Hickory-Smoked Turkey

Turkey with a mild hickory smoke, salt, and pepper.

30 min prep · 3 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

A hickory-smoked turkey, breast or whole bird, is a holiday roast in the southern Appalachians and across the inland South. Hickory is the local hardwood. This recipe uses a bone-in breast so a home smoker can finish it in an afternoon. It is not a cured turkey and not a pork shoulder.

Cultural background

Holiday turkey smoked over hickory. Poultry must reach 165°F. The mountain and Southern wood is the point, not a sweet glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Turkey is lean, so hickory smoke does the seasoning that a fatty pork shoulder would get from its own drippings. Salt and pepper in the rub keep the skin savory. A moderate pit, not a raging grill, cooks the breast before the smoke turns harsh.

How it is made

Salt a turkey breast and smoke it over hickory until the meat is at a safe poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Take the temperature in the thickest breast, not touching bone. A pop-up timer is a worse guide than a thermometer.

How it is used

Sliced, warm or cold, as a holiday meat. Gravy is optional and not in this ingredient list.

Field notes

  • Hickory is the smoke wood for this holiday turkey in the southern mountains and the inland South.
  • A breast is the practical home cut; a whole bird uses the same final temperature in the thickest part.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in turkey breast, about 6 pounds
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • Hickory wood for the smoker

Steps

  1. Dry the turkey breast and rub it with salt, pepper, and paprika. Refrigerate it while the smoker heats.
  2. Smoke over indirect hickory heat at about 275°F (135°C).
  3. Cook until the thickest part of the breast, not touching bone, reaches 165°F (74°C).
  4. Rest at least 10 minutes before slicing. Do not serve turkey rare. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole turkey takes longer. Measure the breast and the thigh, and do not stop until both are at temperature.

  • green beans
  • cranberry sauce

Skillet Cornbread

Crusty cornbread, savory from buttermilk and salt, not cakey-sweet.

15 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Skillet cornbread is an everyday bread in the southern Appalachians and across the South, baked in a preheated pan so the edge fries a little in fat. This one uses cornmeal, egg, buttermilk, and butter, and a baking powder labeled gluten-free, with no wheat flour and no sugar. It is a bread for beans and greens, not a dessert.

Cultural background

A savory skillet bread. Dairy and egg are in it. No wheat flour is added, and the baking powder is the gluten-free kind, so the written recipe is gluten-free. It is not hush puppies and not a sweet cake.

Why it tastes this way

A hot greased skillet browns the batter at the edge, which is the crust mountain tables expect. Buttermilk and egg moisten the cornmeal. Corn, not wheat, is the grain, which fits a region that grew corn for bread. Beside salty soup beans, the bread is the mild contrast.

How it is made

Pour cornmeal batter into a hot buttered skillet and bake it until the center is set.

Authentic touch. Heat the skillet with the butter in it until the butter is hot before the batter goes in. A cold pan makes a pale, steamy round.

How it is used

Warm, with soup beans, greens, or a barbecue plate. It is not sweetened.

Field notes

  • This skillet cornbread has no wheat flour and no sugar.
  • A preheated skillet is what browns the crust before the center sets.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups cornmeal
  • 1 teaspoon baking powder labeled gluten-free
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 egg
  • 1 1/2 cups buttermilk
  • 2 tablespoons butter

Steps

  1. Heat the oven to 425°F (218°C). Put the butter in a 9-inch cast-iron skillet and set the skillet in the oven until the butter melts and the pan is hot.
  2. Stir cornmeal, baking powder, and salt. Beat in the egg and buttermilk. There is no wheat flour.
  3. Pour the batter into the hot skillet and bake until the center is set and the top is browned, about 20 to 25 minutes.
  4. Cool a few minutes before cutting. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of sugar is a sweeter Southern habit. This mountain-leaning pan leaves it out.

Appalachian Chow-Chow

Tangy chopped cabbage, green tomato, and onion in a mustard-seed brine.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 12 · about 6 cups

History

Chow-chow is a chopped vegetable relish kept in mountain and Southern refrigerators and spooned beside beans and greens. This recipe is a refrigerator relish, not a shelf-stable canning recipe, and it gives no processing time for jars. Mustard seed is in the brine, so mustard is an allergen. Use it within a week.

Cultural background

A cold chopped relish for the Appalachian table. Not canned. Not a fermented crock left at room temperature. Cabbage, green tomato, and onion are the body.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar, sugar, and mustard seed pickle the vegetables just enough to stay crisp, which gives a sharp spoonful against rich soup beans or pork. End-of-summer gardens in the mountains are why the relish mixes green tomato with cabbage instead of being a single-vegetable pickle. The refrigerator, not a canner, is what makes this batch safe to describe.

How it is made

Salt chopped vegetables, rinse them, cover them with a hot vinegar brine, cool them, and refrigerate.

Authentic touch. Cool the relish and refrigerate it the same day. Do not seal it in jars for a shelf. This page is not a canning manual.

How it is used

Cold, by the spoon, beside soup beans, cornbread, or pork. It is a condiment.

Field notes

  • This chow-chow is a refrigerator relish, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.
  • Mustard seed in the brine means the relish contains mustard.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups chopped green cabbage
  • 2 cups chopped green tomato
  • 1 cup chopped onion
  • 1 cup chopped green bell pepper
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon mustard seed
  • 1 teaspoon celery seed

Steps

  1. Toss the vegetables with the salt and let them stand 30 minutes. Rinse and drain them well.
  2. Simmer vinegar, sugar, mustard seed, and celery seed until the sugar dissolves and the brine is hot, at a simmer around 212°F (100°C).
  3. Pour the hot brine over the vegetables. Cool the bowl. This is not a canning recipe. Do not process jars for shelf storage, and do not give these vegetables a canning time.
  4. Refrigerate and use within a week, holding it below 40°F (4°C). Mustard seed is an allergen.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter chile can be chopped in. It does not change the refrigerator rule.

Appalachian Soup Beans

Creamy pinto beans in a simple broth with onion.

15 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Soup beans are pinto beans simmered into a brothy pot, an everyday Appalachian meal with cornbread. A ham hock is a common seasoning, and this version does not use one. It is a vegetarian pot of beans, onion, garlic, and salt. The beans are boiled until they are completely tender.

Cultural background

A meatless pot of Appalachian soup beans. No ham hock. Serve them with their broth. They are a meal as often as they are a side.

Why it tastes this way

Pintos break down just enough to thicken their own broth, which is the soup in soup beans. Onion cooked the whole time is the savor that a ham hock would otherwise dominate. Cornbread beside the bowl is the mountain habit, because the beans were the daily protein and the bread was the daily grain.

How it is made

Boil dried pinto beans with onion until they are completely tender and still soupy. No pork.

Authentic touch. Leave them brothy. Soup beans are not refried and not drained dry. Spoon off broth with the beans.

How it is used

In bowls, with cornbread and often chow-chow. The cornbread is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • A ham hock is common in soup beans. This pot does not use one.
  • The beans are served in their broth, which is why they are called soup beans.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound dried pinto beans, picked over and rinsed
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 8 cups water

Steps

  1. Rinse the beans and put them in a pot with onion, garlic, and water. Do not add a ham hock.
  2. Bring them to a full boil at 212°F (100°C), boil for several minutes, then simmer.
  3. Cook until the beans are completely tender and the pot is still brothy, usually 1 1/2 to 2 1/2 hours. Add water if they get dry. Do not hold dried beans warm from raw without boiling them.
  4. Salt them once they are soft. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Boil dried beans until they are completely tender. Kidney beans must be boiled hard, not held warm from raw. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dried red pepper in the pot adds heat without adding meat.
