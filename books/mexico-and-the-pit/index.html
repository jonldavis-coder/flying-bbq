Yucatán Cochinita Pibil

Sour orange, achiote, and slow pork, dyed red and soft enough to shred.

40 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

The pib is a Maya pit: a hole of heated stones used to cook food in the ground. Pork and sour orange arrived in Yucatán after Spanish contact. Achiote, the seed of Bixa orellana, is native to the tropical Americas and is what turns the pork red. A covered pan in a home oven can borrow the seasoning, and it should be labeled as not a pib.

Cultural background

Cochinita pibil is young pig, or a pork shoulder in a home kitchen, wrapped and cooked until it shreds. It is Yucatecan fiesta food and also a weekday taco filling. The oven version is a substitute for the pit, not the pit itself.

Why it tastes this way

Achiote is earthy and slightly peppery, and sour orange cuts the fat of the shoulder. In a pib the meat steams against hot stone and banana leaf instead of searing. That covered heat is why the pork tastes dyed and tangy rather than like a grilled chop. The home oven can echo the steam, without the wood and the ground.

How it is made

Marinate a pork shoulder in achiote and sour orange, wrap it, and cook it covered until it shreds. A home oven is not a pib.

Authentic touch. Grind the achiote with the garlic and juice so the paste stains the meat, and do not skip the rest after it leaves the heat.

How it is used

Shred it and eat it with warm corn tortillas, pickled red onion, and a habanero salsa on the side if you want more heat.

Field notes

  • Achiote comes from Bixa orellana, a plant native to the tropical Americas.
  • Sour orange and pork reached Yucatán after Spanish contact; the pib is older than those ingredients.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds boneless pork shoulder
  • 1/4 cup ground achiote (annatto) seed
  • 1/2 cup sour orange juice, or 1/4 cup orange juice plus 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 8 garlic cloves, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 large banana leaves, passed over a flame until flexible, or parchment if you have no leaves

Steps

  1. Blend the achiote, sour orange juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and pepper into a paste. Coat the pork. The meat is still raw and is not ready to eat. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Wrap the coated pork in banana leaf or parchment and set it in a heavy pot with a tight lid. Refrigerate it if it sits more than 2 hours. Do not leave the paste at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Heat a home oven to 325°F (165°C). This oven is not a pib. Cook the wrapped pork until the center is at least 145°F (63°C). For cochinita that shreds, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then shred it. The center should already have reached the temperature in the cooking step. Serve warm, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add a little habanero to the recado; this version keeps the chile in a side salsa.
  • Banana leaves are the usual wrapper when they are sold. Parchment works in a home oven and is not a leaf.

Poc Chuc

Grilled pork with a sharp sour-orange marinade and a little char.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Poc chuc is Yucatán grilled pork, sliced and turned over a hot fire after a sour-orange marinade. Sour orange is a citrus that reached the peninsula after Spanish contact. The cook stays on the grill, in pieces, which is why it is not cochinita from a pib. Pickled onion is the usual bright relish beside it.

Cultural background

This is everyday Yucatecan pork, not a buried pit roast and not Mexico City al pastor. A shoulder steak or a thin whole-muscle slice is the home cut. It is grilled, rested, and sliced.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange seasons the surface and keeps the pork from tasting flat. A hot grill browns the marinade instead of steaming it. Wood or charcoal adds a light smoke that a covered pib would hide. The result is tangy and charred, in slices rather than shreds.

How it is made

Marinate thin pork in sour orange, garlic, and oregano, then grill it over a hot fire to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Grill it hot and do not let the sour-orange sugar burn black. A clean grate and a short cook keep the citrus taste.

How it is used

Serve the slices with corn tortillas, pickled red onion, and a simple tomato salsa.

Field notes

  • Poc chuc is grilled, so the pork stays in slices rather than becoming cochinita shreds.
  • Pickled red onion is the relish most often set beside Yucatecan grilled pork.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds thin pork shoulder steaks
  • 1/2 cup sour orange juice, or 1/4 cup orange juice plus 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 small red onion, sliced, for a quick pickle with 1/2 cup vinegar and 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil. Coat the pork and refrigerate it for 30 minutes. The pork is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Stir the sliced onion with vinegar and salt. That relish is not a canned preserve. Keep it cold and use it within a few days. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the pork over a hot fire until the thickest steak reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested pork. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Eat it the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A thin pork loin chop works if shoulder steaks are not sold, and it still needs 145°F (63°C).
  • Some tables add a whole grilled onion beside the meat.

Mexico City Al Pastor

Chile-stained pork with pineapple, crisp at the edge and juicy where the slice is thin.

40 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

In the twentieth century, Lebanese shawarma in central Mexico became the vertical trompo of al pastor. Taquerías in Mexico City stacked pork, stained with achiote or guajillo, and set pineapple on the spit. The home cook who grills thin slices on a grate or a sheet is not running a vertical spit unless a spit is actually in the kitchen. The seasoning can still be the same.

Cultural background

Al pastor is a Mexico City taco of marinated pork and pineapple. It is not Yucatecan cochinita, and it is not Lebanese shawarma served as lamb. The written steps are the home grill, labeled as such.

Why it tastes this way

Guajillo and achiote give the pork a dried-chile color and a mild heat, and pineapple adds acid and sugar at the edge of the slice. The trompo bastes the meat in its own fat as it turns. A home grill still browns thin pork quickly, which is the part of the taste you can reach without the spit.

How it is made

Marinate thin pork in guajillo and achiote, then roast it on a vertical spit if you have one. Otherwise grill or sheet-cook the slices and call that a home version, not a trompo.

Authentic touch. Slice the pork thin before it cooks. Thick chunks will not taste like pastor even if the chile paste is right.

How it is used

Chop the cooked pork with a little grilled pineapple and eat it in small corn tortillas with onion and cilantro.

Field notes

  • The vertical trompo is a Levantine shawarma spit adapted in central Mexico in the twentieth century.
  • Pineapple on the pastor spit is a Mexican addition to that spit method.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds boneless pork shoulder, sliced about 1/4 inch thick
  • 4 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 tablespoons ground achiote (annatto)
  • 1/2 cup pineapple juice
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 cup fresh pineapple chunks
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Toast the guajillos in a dry pan until fragrant, cover them with hot water for 15 minutes, and blend them with achiote, pineapple juice, garlic, cumin, and salt. Coat the raw pork. It is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. If you have a vertical spit, stack the pork, oil the outside, and roast it, shaving slices as the face browns. If you do not, this is not a vertical spit: grill the slices or lay them on a hot sheet. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook every slice until the thickest piece reads 145°F (63°C). Grill the pineapple until it browns. Rest the pork 3 minutes.
  4. Chop the rested pork. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Eat it in tortillas the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Achiote alone, without guajillo, is a common paste. This recipe uses both.
  • If you own a vertical spit, stack the slices and cook them there. That is the only version that is a trompo.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • corn tortillas
  • cilantro and onion
  • lime
  • salsa verde

Sonoran Carne Asada

Beef grilled over mesquite, salted, with a flour tortilla as the wrap rather than a dough in the meat.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Carne asada in Sonora is a northern Mexican grill: beef over mesquite, eaten in flour tortillas. Wheat tortillas belong to the north as a pairing. They are not mixed into the steak. The same table is part of life in southern Arizona, which is why this stop also carries the United States. It is not the Salvadoran plate of the same Spanish name.

Cultural background

Sonoran carne asada is a ranch and backyard steak, often thin, often mesquite. The meat itself is beef and salt. Beans, salsa, and grilled onions sit beside it. This page does not put wheat in the rub.

Why it tastes this way

Mesquite coals are hot and a little sweet, and they mark beef faster than a mild hardwood. Northern Sonora is cattle and desert country, so the meal is steak and a wheat tortilla rather than a long chile stew. The lime and salt on the meat stay sharp because the cook is short.

How it is made

Salt a thin steak and grill it hot over mesquite coals to a whole-muscle temperature, then rest it.

Authentic touch. Burn mesquite down to coals before the steak goes on. Flames from fresh logs will taste like soot, not like Sonoran mesquite.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with flour tortillas, grilled spring onions, and a fresh salsa. The tortilla is a pairing.

Field notes

  • Mesquite is the usual grilling wood of Sonora.
  • Flour tortillas are the northern wrap; the steak in this recipe has no wheat in it.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds thin beef steak, such as ranchera or skirt
  • 2 teaspoons coarse salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 limes, cut into wedges
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • Mesquite wood or charcoal, burned to coals

Steps

  1. Oil the steak lightly and season it with salt and pepper. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. There is no flour in this step. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Burn mesquite or charcoal to a bed of coals. A flaming log is not the grilling stage. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the steak until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice across the grain. The steak should have reached 145°F (63°C) and rested. Squeeze lime at the table. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A gas grill with a mesquite chunk on the coals is a home stand-in, and it is not a mesquite fire.
  • Ranchera, flank, or skirt all appear on this grill. Cook each one as a whole cut.

Monterrey Cabrito

Young goat, salted, with a browned skin and a mild, meaty center.

30 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Cabrito in Monterrey is kid goat, cooked on a spit or opened over an open fire. Nuevo León made the dish a restaurant and family standard in the twentieth century, in a city already tied to northern ranching. The animal is young, which is what the word means. A full-grown goat is a different cook.

Cultural background

This is Monterrey goat, not a lamb barbacoa and not a pork pibil. Restaurants often open the kid flat beside glowing coals. A home cook with a leg or a small shoulder should say that the cut is not a whole kid.

Why it tastes this way

Kid goat is leaner than a pork shoulder, so the taste is mild and a little sweet if the fire is gentle. Mesquite or oak coals in this dry northern city brown the skin without a sweet glaze. Salt is the seasoning that lets the meat stay the point of the plate.

How it is made

Salt a kid, or a home-size leg labeled as not the whole animal, and roast it on a spit or beside coals to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep the fire beside the meat or under a steady bed of coals. A roaring flame dries a lean kid before the center is done.

How it is used

Cut it into pieces and eat it with flour or corn tortillas, salsa, and beans. The tortillas are pairings.

Field notes

  • Cabrito means a young goat, not a full-grown animal.
  • In Monterrey it is cooked on a spit or opened flat beside the fire.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in kid goat leg, about 4 pounds, labeled as a home cut and not a whole kid
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • 4 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Wood or charcoal for a spit or an open grill

Steps

  1. Rub the leg with oil, salt, pepper, and garlic. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it until the fire is ready. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Spit the leg or set it beside a bed of coals, not in open flame. This is the Monterrey fire, scaled to one leg. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part, away from bone, reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Carve the rested leg. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A bone-in leg is the workable home cut. Call it a leg, not a whole cabrito.
  • Some asadores use only salt. A little garlic is a home addition and should stay light.

  • beans
  • tortillas

Norteño Discada

A mixed pan of beef, pork, bacon, chile, and onion, browned on a wide steel disc.

30 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Discada is a norteño mixed grill cooked on a discarded plow disc, the disco. It belongs to ranch and camping cooks across Chihuahua, Coahuila, Durango, and neighboring northern states, and it spread as a backyard party pan in the twentieth century. Beef, pork, and often bacon share the disc. A large skillet is the home stand-in, and it is not a plow disc.

Cultural background

This is one pan with more than one kind of meat, so the temperatures are not all the same. Whole strips of beef and pork are one target. Fresh sausage or ground meat in the same disc is another. The cook is northern Mexico, not an asado from the southern cone.

Why it tastes this way

The disc is thin steel over a hot fire, so meat browns in its own fat the way a plancha does. Bacon fat carries onion and green chile across the beef and pork. The flavor is a camp fry, wide and savory, from a tool that started as farm equipment rather than from a sauce.

How it is made

Brown bacon, sausage, and strips of beef and pork with onion and chile on a plow disc, or in a heavy skillet labeled as not a disco.

Authentic touch. Cook the ground meat or sausage to its own temperature before you lean on the look of the steak strips. They finish at different points.

How it is used

Eat it from the disc with tortillas, salsa, and beans. Keep the pan hot and the cooked meat above the raw scraps.

Field notes

  • The pan is an agricultural plow disc, which is why the cook is called discada.
  • It is a norteño camp dish shared across several northern states, not a single city recipe.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound beef steak, in strips
  • 1 pound pork shoulder, in strips
  • 1/2 pound fresh raw chorizo, casings split, or 1/2 pound ground beef
  • 4 ounces sliced bacon
  • 1 large onion, sliced
  • 2 jalapeños, sliced
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • A plow disc set over a fire, or a large heavy skillet if you have no disc

Steps

  1. Set the disc or skillet over a hot fire. If this is a skillet, it is not a plow disc. Start the bacon until it is crisp and steaming hot, at least 145°F (63°C). The other meat is still raw.
  2. Add the fresh chorizo or ground beef and cook it, breaking it up, until it reaches 160°F (71°C). Do not stop at a browned outside.
  3. Add the beef strips, pork strips, onion, jalapeño, garlic, and salt. Cook until the whole cuts read 145°F (63°C). Rest those strips 3 minutes. Keep the ground meat at or above 160°F (71°C).
  4. Taste a vegetable, not a raw piece. The beef and pork strips should have reached 145°F (63°C) and rested, and the sausage or ground meat should have reached 160°F (71°C). Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat and fresh sausage to 160°F (71°C). Cook whole strips of beef and pork to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest them 3 minutes. Cook bacon until it is crisp and hot.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A wok or a large skillet replaces the disc at home. Say that it is not a disco.
  • Some cooks add hot dog pieces. If you do, heat them until they are steaming, and still hold the fresh sausage to 160°F (71°C).

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • Feasts
  • tortillas
  • salsa
  • beans
  • avocado

Oaxacan Tasajo

Thin salted beef, grilled quickly, chewy at the edge and beefy in the middle.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tasajo is thin salted beef sold in Oaxaca markets and grilled to order. Salting and drying were how butchers kept beef before refrigeration, and the grilled slice became a market breakfast and a fonda plate. This home recipe does not ask you to cure meat in the sun. Buy market tasajo if you can, or salt a thin steak briefly in the refrigerator.

Cultural background

Oaxacan tasajo is beef, not pork cecina from the same market stall, though the two are often grilled side by side. It is a hot, fast grill. It is not a long smoke.

Why it tastes this way

Salt concentrates the beef, and the thin cut takes a hard sear before the inside dries out. The Central Valleys grill is charcoal, close to the meat, so the surface browns while the slice stays small. Onion and salsa are the fresh contrast buyers expect at the stand.

How it is made

Grill thin salted beef hot and fast to a whole-muscle temperature. Do not sun-dry meat at home.

Authentic touch. Keep the slice thin. A thick steak salted for an hour is only a seasoned steak, not market tasajo.

How it is used

Eat it with corn tortillas, grilled spring onion, salsa, and a radish. Cecina and chorizo on the same grill are separate meats.

Field notes

  • Tasajo is beef that Oaxaca markets sell already salted and thin for the grill.
  • This recipe does not include home curing or sun-drying.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds beef skirt or flank, sliced 1/4 inch thick
  • 2 teaspoons salt, for a short refrigerator seasoning, or 1 pound purchased tasajo
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 spring onions
  • Lime wedges

Steps

  1. If you are not using market tasajo, salt the thin beef and refrigerate it for 1 hour, then pat it dry. That is seasoning, not a cure. Do not dry it in the sun. The meat is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Oil the slices lightly. Grill the spring onions beside them. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the beef until the thickest slice reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. The rested slices should have reached 145°F (63°C). Eat them hot with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • If you buy prepared tasajo, rinse the surface salt if it looks heavy, then grill it.
  • A skirt steak sliced thin is the home stand-in, and it should be named as a stand-in.

Yucatán Tikin Xic

Fish stained with achiote and sour orange, gently charred, sometimes steamed a little by a leaf.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tikin xic is a Yucatecan fish cook: achiote and sour orange on a fillet or a split fish, sometimes wrapped in banana leaf and grilled. Achiote is a native American seed. Sour orange arrived after Spanish contact. The leaf is a wrapper cooks use when they have it. This page does not turn the grill into a ceremony.

Cultural background

The dish is fish, in the same seasoning family as Yucatecan pork, with a different animal and a shorter cook. A home kitchen without banana leaves can use parchment and should say so.

Why it tastes this way

Achiote colors the fish and adds a peppery earth taste, and sour orange keeps it bright. If a banana leaf is on the grill, the fish steams a little as well as browns. Without the leaf, the same paste tastes more directly of the fire. Either way the fish, not the wrapper, is the center.

How it is made

Coat fish with achiote and sour orange and grill it, in a leaf if you have one, until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Oil the leaf or the parchment so the paste does not stick and tear the cooked fish.

How it is used

Eat it with corn tortillas, pickled onion, and a tomato salsa.

Field notes

  • Achiote and sour orange are the Yucatecan seasoning pair used here on fish.
  • A banana leaf wraps the fish when cooks have one; it is not itself the seasoning.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds firm white fish fillets, such as snapper or grouper
  • 2 tablespoons ground achiote (annatto)
  • 1/4 cup sour orange juice, or 2 tablespoons orange juice plus 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 banana leaf, or parchment paper

Steps

  1. Mix achiote, juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Wrap the fish in a banana leaf, or in parchment if you have no leaf. Parchment is not a banana leaf. Refrigerate it if it waits more than 2 hours. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill the packet over a medium-hot fire, turning once, until the center of the thickest fillet reads 145°F (63°C) and the fish is opaque and flakes.
  4. Open the packet carefully. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Eat it the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Mixiote in Maguey or Parchment

Lamb steamed in a chile paste, soft, with a concentrated guajillo taste.

40 min prep · 3 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Mixiote is meat wrapped for a pit or a steamer in the central highlands, especially Hidalgo, the State of Mexico, Puebla, and Tlaxcala. The older wrapper is the thin cuticle of a maguey leaf. Maguey leaves are often hard to buy, and cutting them can harm the plant, so many home cooks use parchment. Parchment is a substitute and it is not the same as maguey. A home packet is also not a pit.

Cultural background

The usual meat is lamb or mutton, though chicken and rabbit appear in the same kind of packet. This page is lamb. The chile paste is the flavor you can keep even when the wrapper changes. Say which wrapper you used.

Why it tastes this way

Guajillo and a covered packet concentrate chile and lamb juices instead of grilling them off. Maguey has a light vegetal smell that parchment does not have. The highland pit adds wood and stone. A home oven still tastes of chile and lamb, and it should not be described as the maguey pit.

How it is made

Wrap chile-rubbed lamb in maguey if you have it, or in parchment labeled as a substitute, and cook it covered until the lamb is at a safe whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Tie the packet so the juices stay in. Mixiote that boils dry in an open pan is a different, harder dish.

How it is used

Open the packet at the table and eat the lamb with corn tortillas, salsa, and nopales or beans.

Field notes

  • The traditional wrapper is the cuticle of a maguey leaf, used in the central highlands.
  • Parchment keeps steam in, and it is not maguey.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds boneless lamb shoulder, in large pieces
  • 4 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 tablespoons cider vinegar
  • Parchment paper and kitchen string, unless you have food-safe maguey wrappers

Steps

  1. Toast and soak the guajillos, then blend them with garlic, cumin, oregano, salt, and vinegar. Coat the lamb. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Wrap the lamb in parchment and tie the packet. If you do not have maguey, say so: parchment is a substitute and it is not the same. A home oven is not a pit. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Set the packet in a covered pot with a cup of water, or cover it tightly. Cook at 325°F (165°C) until the thickest piece reads at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes. For pullable shoulder, continue toward 195°F (90°C).
  4. Open the rested packet. The lamb should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicken in the same paste must reach 165°F (74°C). This written recipe is lamb.
  • Avocado leaves scent some highland packets. Leave them out if you cannot tell culinary leaves from ornamental ones.

Arrachera a la Parrilla

Skirt steak, garlicky and lime-sharp, sliced across a loose grain.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Arrachera is the skirt, a diaphragm muscle, and grilling it is a widespread Mexican practice rather than one town's patent. On a ranch grill it is often only salt, garlic, and lime over a hot fire. This page is that straightforward parrilla steak. It is not Sonoran carne asada with its mesquite and flour-tortilla table, and it is not a trompo.

Cultural background

The name on this page is Arrachera a la Parrilla. The cut is whole muscle. Slice it across the grain after it rests, or the texture stays stringy even when the temperature is right.

Why it tastes this way

Skirt is loose and beefy, so a very hot grate browns it before the inside overcooks. Lime and garlic sit on the surface because the cook is short. The flavor is the steak and the fire, not a long marinade of dried chiles.

How it is made

Grill skirt steak hot with salt, garlic, and lime, then rest and slice it across the grain.

Authentic touch. Find the direction of the grain before you grill, then slice the other way after the rest.

How it is used

Serve the slices with corn tortillas, a tomato and onion salsa, and lime.

Field notes

  • Arrachera is skirt steak, a diaphragm muscle, and it is sliced across the grain.
  • It is a hot grill, not a long smoke and not a vertical spit.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds beef skirt steak
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 limes

Steps

  1. Rub the skirt with oil, garlic, salt, and pepper. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill until it is very hot and the grate is clean. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the skirt until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice across the grain. The steak should have reached 145°F (63°C) and rested. Add lime at the table. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Flank can stand in if skirt is not sold. Say which cut is on the plate, and cook it as a whole steak.
  • A hotter salsa can be served beside it without changing the mild seasoning on the meat.

Mexican Elote

Grilled corn with chile, lime, and salt.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 ears

History

Elote is ear corn grilled over coals in Mexico and dressed at the cart. Many street servings add mayonnaise and cotija cheese. Mayonnaise would bring egg, and cheese or crema would bring dairy. This recipe is the chile-and-lime version only, so the written ear is vegan. The corn is the dish.

Cultural background

There is no meat, egg, or dairy in the ingredients. A guajillo chile keeps the heat mild. People who want the creamy street version should know it is a different set of allergens and not this page.

Why it tastes this way

Grilling dries the kernels and sweetens them, and lime and salt make that sweetness sharper. Mild chile adds color and a little warmth without a sauce. Char from the coals is the smoke. The ear tastes of corn first, which is the point of leaving the creamy dressings off.

How it is made

Grill corn and dress it with lime, salt, and mild chile. Do not add mayonnaise or cheese.

Authentic touch. Grill the ears in the husk for a few minutes if you want them juicier, then husk them and char the kernels directly.

How it is used

Eat the ears hot, with extra lime. It is a side or a street snack.

Field notes

  • Elote is corn grilled over coals and dressed at the cart.
  • This version is chile and lime. It does not include mayonnaise or cheese.

Ingredients

  • 4 ears of corn, husks pulled back or removed
  • 1 lime, cut into wedges
  • 1 teaspoon ground guajillo or other mild chile powder
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Oil the ears and salt them lightly. There is no meat, mayonnaise, or cheese in this recipe.
  2. Grill the corn over hot coals, turning, until the kernels are browned in spots.
  3. Squeeze on lime and dust the ears with chile and salt.
  4. Eat them hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This version stays vegan because the creamy street dressings are not on it.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A creamy street elote with mayonnaise and cotija is common, and it would add egg and dairy. It is not this recipe.
  • Chile de árbol instead of guajillo makes the same corn hotter.
