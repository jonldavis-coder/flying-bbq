Jamaican Jerk Chicken

Chicken with allspice, thyme, and Scotch bonnet, smoky if the fire has pimento wood.

35 min prep · 55 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Jerk is tied to Maroon communities in Jamaica, people of African descent who escaped slavery and cooked seasoned meat over pimento wood in the hills. Allspice is the berry of that pimento tree, native to the Caribbean region, and Scotch bonnet is a New World chile. Chicken is the piece most cooks grill now. The older pork shoulder is a separate, longer cook.

Cultural background

The paste is scallion, thyme, allspice, garlic, ginger, and Scotch bonnet. Pimento wood is the traditional smoke. A charcoal grill without that wood is a home fire, and it is not the same smoke. The chicken still has to reach a poultry temperature.

Why it tastes this way

Pimento wood is the allspice tree, so the smoke repeats the spice ground into the paste. Thyme and scallion are green and sharp, and Scotch bonnet brings a high, fruity heat. Chicken fat carries those flavors across a shorter cook than a pork shoulder can stand.

How it is made

Rub chicken with a jerk paste and grill it over coals until the thickest meat reaches a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Burn pimento wood down to coals if you can get it. If you cannot, say the grill is not a pimento fire, and keep the allspice in the paste.

How it is used

Eat it with festival dumplings or bread, roasted breadfruit, and a cold drink. The heat is in the pepper.

Field notes

  • Scotch bonnet is a Caribbean chile, Capsicum chinense.
  • Pimento is the Jamaican name for the allspice tree, and its wood is the traditional jerk smoke.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, cut into 8 pieces
  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme, leaves stripped
  • 1 tablespoon ground allspice
  • 1 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh ginger
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice

Steps

  1. Blend scallions, thyme, allspice, Scotch bonnet, garlic, ginger, salt, oil, and lime juice into a paste. Wear gloves with the pepper. Coat the chicken. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Refrigerate the coated chicken if it sits. Do not leave the garlic paste at room temperature. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  3. Grill over coals, ideally with pimento wood if you have it, turning often, until the thickest piece reads 165°F (74°C) away from the bone. Juices should run clear.
  4. Rest the chicken a few minutes. Every piece should have reached 165°F (74°C). Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Seed the Scotch bonnet for a hot paste. Leaving the seeds in pushes it hotter.
  • A covered charcoal grill is the home tool. It is not a pimento-wood pit unless that wood is on the fire.

Pairings and uses

  • festival dumplings
  • roast breadfruit
  • rice and peas

Jamaican Jerk Pork Shoulder

Pork shoulder with a heavy allspice and Scotch bonnet paste, cooked until you can slice or chop it.

40 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Pork is the older jerk meat. Maroon cooks in the Jamaican hills used pimento wood and a hot, aromatic paste on pig, long before chicken became the more common restaurant order. A shoulder needs hours, not the short grill of chicken pieces, which is why this is a separate recipe. Scotch bonnet is a New World chile, and allspice is a Caribbean tree.

Cultural background

This shoulder is seasoned more heavily than the chicken, and it stays on a covered fire until the center is safe and, if you want it tender enough to chop, much hotter than the sliceable minimum. It is not the chicken recipe with a different label.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder fat melts slowly and carries allspice, thyme, and Scotch bonnet into the meat. A long fire, especially one with pimento wood, dries the surface into a bark while the inside stays moist. Chicken cannot sit that long without going dry, so the pork tastes deeper and more perfumed.

How it is made

Coat a pork shoulder in a thick jerk paste and cook it covered for several hours, to a pork temperature that matches how you will serve it.

Authentic touch. Use a full measure of allspice. The berry should be obvious in the pork, not a faint dusting.

How it is used

Chop or slice it and eat it with breadfruit, festival, or rice and peas.

Field notes

  • Jerk was a pork cook before chicken became the more common restaurant order.
  • A shoulder needs a longer fire than chicken pieces, which is why the two recipes are separate.

Ingredients

  • 5 pounds boneless pork shoulder
  • 8 scallions, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons ground allspice
  • 6 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 2 Scotch bonnets, seeded
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh ginger
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 tablespoons cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend scallions, allspice, thyme, Scotch bonnets, garlic, ginger, brown sugar, salt, vinegar, and oil into a thick paste. Coat the pork. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not leave the paste out. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Set up a covered grill or oven for a long, moderate cook, about 300°F (150°C). This is hours, not the chicken grill.
  3. Cook until the center of the shoulder is at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes if you will slice it. If you want it choppable, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), then rest it.
  4. Slice or chop the rested pork. It should already have reached the temperature you chose in the cooking step. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • For sliced pork, stop at the whole-muscle temperature and a rest. For chopped jerk, keep going until the shoulder softens.
  • No pimento wood: cook it on a covered charcoal grill and say the smoke is not pimento.

  • festival dumplings

Cuban Lechón Asado

Skin-on pork with garlic and sour orange, roasted until the skin crackles.

45 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Pigs came to Cuba with Spanish colonization, and enslaved and free Africans cooked a large share of the island's pork, which is the criollo background of lechón asado. A whole pig is the feast; a skin-on shoulder is the home lechón and should be labeled as that. The English word barbecue passed through Spanish barbacoa, from a Taíno word for a framework of sticks, a Caribbean path into English rather than a single recipe. Cuban lechón is not that Taíno framework, and it is not Haitian boukannen.

Cultural background

This page is the spit or the covered roast of a whole pig, scaled at home to a skin-on shoulder. The seasoning is Cuban mojo. The oven shoulder that follows on its own page is a different, wetter cook.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange and a large amount of garlic season the fat, and a long fire dries the skin until it blisters. The meat underneath stays porky and sour rather than sweet. Trade brought the citrus and the pig; the island's cooks, including African Cuban cooks, set the amounts. A home shoulder can taste of that mojo even when it is not a whole animal.

How it is made

Season a skin-on pork shoulder, the home lechón, with mojo and roast it on a spit or uncovered until the center is safe and the skin is crisp.

Authentic touch. Dry the skin and salt it. Wet skin will not crackle, no matter how strong the mojo is.

How it is used

Chop the meat and crackling together and eat it with white rice, black beans, and yuca.

Field notes

  • A whole pig is the feast lechón; a skin-on shoulder is the home size and should be called that.
  • The word barbecue reached English through Spanish barbacoa, from a Taíno word for a stick framework.

Ingredients

  • 6 pounds skin-on pork shoulder, the home lechón, not a whole pig
  • 1 cup sour orange juice, or 1/2 cup orange juice plus 1/2 cup lime juice
  • 10 garlic cloves, mashed
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the juice, garlic, oregano, cumin, salt, pepper, and oil. Rub it under the skin and over the meat. The pork is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not store this garlic mojo at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Dry the skin. Spit the shoulder or set it uncovered on a rack. This is the home lechón, not a whole pig. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Roast it, starting moderate and finishing hotter if you want the skin to blister, until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes. For pullable meat, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) before the rest.
  4. Chop the rested pork, skin included if it crackled. The center should already have reached the temperature you cooked to. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A covered grill can stand in for a spit. Say so if you are not actually turning the meat.
  • Sour orange is the acid. Orange juice plus lime is the substitute when sour oranges are not sold.

Pairings and uses

  • Feasts
  • Holidays
  • white rice
  • black beans
  • yuca
  • pickled onion

Cuban Mojo Pork Shoulder

A covered pork shoulder soaked with garlic, cumin, and sour orange.

30 min prep · 3 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Mojo is the Cuban marinade of sour orange, garlic, oregano, and cumin. At home it is often poured over a pork shoulder and roasted in a covered pan, which is a different meal from the spit-roasted whole pig. The pig and the citrus both belong to the island's history after Spanish colonization, and African Cuban cooks shaped how those flavors were used. This page is the home shoulder only.

Cultural background

Do not use this recipe as a stand-in for the whole-pig lechón. The pan is covered for part of the cook, the skin is not the point, and the sauce stays spoonable. It is still Cuban, and it is still pork.

Why it tastes this way

A covered pan keeps the sour orange and garlic against the shoulder instead of drying them off on a spit. Cumin and oregano, pantry spices of the Spanish and Canary trade into the Caribbean, round the acid. The pork tastes braised and bright, not like crackling.

How it is made

Roast a pork shoulder in mojo in a covered home pan until it reaches a pork-shoulder temperature.

Authentic touch. Mash the garlic with salt to a paste before it meets the juice. Floating garlic bits will not season a five-pound shoulder.

How it is used

Slice or shred it and spoon the pan juices over rice and beans. This is not the chopped crackling of a lechón feast.

Field notes

  • This is an oven shoulder for a home kitchen, not the spit-roasted whole pig.
  • Sour orange is the acid; orange juice plus lime is the stand-in when sour oranges are not sold.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds boneless pork shoulder
  • 3/4 cup sour orange juice, or 6 tablespoons orange juice plus 6 tablespoons lime juice
  • 8 garlic cloves, mashed with 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 1 onion, sliced

Steps

  1. Stir juice, garlic, oregano, cumin, pepper, and oil. Pour it over the pork and onion in a covered pot. The pork is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not leave the mojo at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Heat an oven to 325°F (165°C). This is a covered home roast, not a spit and not a whole pig.
  3. Cook, covered, until the center is at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes to slice. For shredded pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), then rest.
  4. Slice or shred the rested pork and spoon on the juices. The center should already have reached the temperature you chose. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Uncover the pan at the end if you want a little browning. It will still not be a crackling lechón.
  • A picnic shoulder with the bone in needs the same center temperature, checked away from the bone.

Pairings and uses

  • white rice
  • black beans
  • sweet plantains

Puerto Rican Lechón Asado

Roast pork seasoned with garlic, oregano, and achiote, with a salted skin.

40 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Puerto Rican lechón asado is a whole pig on a spit, sold at lechoneras and cooked for holidays. The seasoning is adobo, garlic, salt, pepper, and oregano, and many cooks also paint the skin with achiote. That is not the Cuban mojo page: cumin and sour orange are not the center here. Guavate, in the hills of Cayey, became famous for a road of these pits. A home shoulder is the workable size and should be labeled as a shoulder.

Cultural background

Adobo on this page is a garlic and oregano rub you mix yourself, not a packet with unnamed additives. Achiote oil colors the skin. The cook is a spit or a stand-in roast, and the meat is pork.

Why it tastes this way

Garlic, black pepper, and oregano sit in the fat, and achiote stains it a deep orange without tasting like a chile. A long spit dries the skin in the open air of the hills. The flavor is savory and porky, closer to a salted roast than to a sour Cuban mojo.

How it is made

Rub a skin-on pork shoulder with Puerto Rican adobo and achiote oil, then spit-roast or oven-roast it to a pork-shoulder temperature.

Authentic touch. Warm the annatto seeds in oil and strain them. Use that colored oil the same day. Do not store it on the counter.

How it is used

Chop meat and skin together and eat it with arroz con gandules or plain rice and a salad.

Field notes

  • Guavate, above Cayey, is known for a road of lechoneras.
  • Achiote colors this pork. The seasoning is not Cuban mojo.

Ingredients

  • 6 pounds skin-on pork shoulder, a home lechón
  • 8 garlic cloves, mashed
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 2 teaspoons black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons wine vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons annatto seeds
  • 1/4 cup neutral oil

Steps

  1. Warm the annatto seeds in the oil until the oil is red, then strain them out. Mix that oil with garlic, oregano, salt, pepper, and vinegar. Use it the same day. Do not leave it at room temperature. The pork is still raw. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Rub the mixture under and over the skin. Refrigerate it if it waits. This is a shoulder, not a whole pig, unless you are actually cooking a whole pig. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Spit-roast it, or roast it uncovered on a rack, until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes. For meat you can pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) before the rest.
  4. Chop the rested pork. The center should already have reached the temperature you cooked to. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks skip achiote and use only adobo. The pork is still this dish.
  • A home oven, uncovered, replaces a spit. Say that it is not a roadside pit.

Pairings and uses

  • Feasts
  • Holidays
  • arroz con gandules
  • avocado salad

Puerto Rican Pinchos

Grilled pork cubes with garlic, oregano, and a light sweet-sharp glaze.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Pinchos are pork skewers sold from small grills along Puerto Rican roads and beaches. They use the island's adobo tastes, garlic and oregano, and often a light glaze. They are a street portion, not the whole pig of a lechonera. The cubes are whole muscle, so they are cooked as pork pieces rather than as ground meat.

Cultural background

This is a skewer for a hot grill. The glaze is brown sugar, garlic, and vinegar, mixed at home. It is not a bottled barbecue sauce, and it is not the long roast of lechón.

Why it tastes this way

Small cubes brown on all sides, so you get more crust than a roast shoulder. Garlic and oregano match the rest of the island's pork, and a little sugar in the glaze darkens on the coal without turning the meat into candy. Sea air and a quick grill are the street setting, and the seasoning still works in a backyard.

How it is made

Skewer garlic-seasoned pork cubes and grill them, brushing on a small glaze near the end.

Authentic touch. Cut the cubes the same size so they finish together. A thick end next to a thin one will mislead a single thermometer reading.

How it is used

Eat the skewers on a piece of bread if you want the street sandwich, or without bread. Bread is a pairing and adds wheat.

Field notes

  • Pinchos are street skewers, sold from grills along roads and beaches.
  • They are pork cubes, not the whole animal of a lechonera.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds pork shoulder, in 1 1/2 inch cubes
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon tomato paste
  • Metal or soaked bamboo skewers

Steps

  1. Toss the pork with garlic, oregano, salt, and oil. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Thread it onto skewers. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Stir brown sugar, vinegar, and tomato paste into a small glaze. Keep it cold until the grill is ready. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the skewers, turning, until the center of the thickest cube reads 145°F (63°C). Brush on the glaze in the last few minutes. Rest the skewers 3 minutes.
  4. The cubes should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Eat them hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of guava paste in the glaze is a common street version. This recipe uses brown sugar instead.
  • Chicken pinchos would need 165°F (74°C). These are pork.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food

  • a pickle
  • grilled pineapple

Dominican Wasakaka Chicken

Roasted or grilled chicken with a fresh garlic and herb sauce.

25 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Wasakaka is a Dominican sauce of garlic, herbs, and lime or sour orange, spooned over grilled or roasted chicken. It belongs to the Dominican table, including the pollo a la brasa and pollo al carbón that families buy and cook. The sauce is not Argentine chimichurri and not Cuban mojo, even though garlic and acid appear in all three. The bird is poultry and is cooked through.

Cultural background

The chicken can be grilled or roasted. The wasakaka is mixed the day you eat it and kept cold. Oregano and cilantro, or parsley, are the herbs. This page uses cilantro.

Why it tastes this way

Chicken skin browns over direct heat, and the raw garlic sauce stays sharp because it never goes on the fire for long. Lime or sour orange is the Caribbean acid, and oregano is the dried herb of the Spanish-speaking islands. Together they make the plate taste fresh rather than glazed.

How it is made

Grill or roast chicken to a poultry temperature and dress it with wasakaka mixed the same day.

Authentic touch. Salt the sauce enough to season the chicken. A pale, unsalted wasakaka will taste like wet garlic.

How it is used

Serve with tostones, white rice, and beans. The sauce is spooned at the table.

Field notes

  • Wasakaka is a Dominican garlic and herb sauce for grilled or roasted chicken.
  • It is a Dominican sauce, not chimichurri and not Cuban mojo.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, cut into pieces
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 6 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil, for the sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Oil and salt the chicken. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Stir garlic, cilantro, oregano, lime juice, oil, and salt into wasakaka. Refrigerate it and use it the same day. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  3. Grill or roast the chicken until the thickest piece reads 165°F (74°C) away from the bone.
  4. Rest it briefly. Every piece should have reached 165°F (74°C). Spoon on the wasakaka. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sour orange replaces lime when you can buy it.
  • An oven roast reaches the same poultry temperature if you do not have a grill.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • tostones
  • white rice
  • beans

Haitian Boukannen

Grilled pork with garlic, thyme, and a hot pepper, edged with smoke.

30 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Boukannen is Haitian grilled or smoked pork, usually built on epis, the garlic, thyme, and pepper seasoning of the kitchen. The French word boucan and the Spanish word barbacoa are related Caribbean words for a wooden frame used over fire, and both passed into later languages from that region. Related words are not the same dish: a boucan is not a Cuban lechón, and it is not a Mexican barbacoa. This page is the Haitian pork cook.

Cultural background

Epis is blended and used the day you cook, then kept cold. The pork here is a whole-muscle steak or thick slice, grilled, not a fried griot. Smoke is welcome. A long cure is not part of the recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Thyme, garlic, and Scotch bonnet are the taste of epis, and an open fire dries the surface of the pork. The related words boucan and barbacoa point at a Caribbean way of setting meat over a frame, but the flavor on the plate is Haitian seasoning, not a generic smoke. The pepper is hot, and the thyme stays visible.

How it is made

Season pork with epis and grill or lightly smoke it to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Cook epis pork soon after you season it, or refrigerate it. Garlic and oil should not sit out while the fire burns down.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with plantains, pikliz if you already have a jar, and rice.

Field notes

  • French boucan and Spanish barbacoa are related Caribbean words, and they are not the same dish.
  • Haitian boukannen is grilled or smoked pork, seasoned in a Haitian kitchen.

  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 1 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 1/4 cup sour orange juice or lime juice
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

  2. Burn wood or charcoal to coals. The pork can sit beside a little smoke or directly over the grate. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the thickest steak reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested pork. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Trinidadian Grilled Kingfish

Grilled kingfish or mahi with culantro, garlic, and a hot pepper.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Trinidad and Tobago, kingfish is a common grill, and the marinade is built on chadon beni, the local name for culantro, with garlic and thyme. Chadon beni is Eryngium foetidum, a different herb from cilantro, with a stronger leaf. The fish may be kingfish or mahi when that is what the market has. Both are cooked as fish, to the same temperature.

Cultural background

Green seasoning is an everyday Trinidadian blend, not a single bottled brand. This one is culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, and lime. The fish is the meal. It is not a meat dish with a fish name.

Why it tastes this way

Culantro is more pungent than cilantro, so a short marinade still tastes green and garlicky after the grill. Lime keeps the oil from feeling heavy. A hot grate marks the steaks, and the pepper, a Caribbean chile, sits in the fat of the fish rather than in a separate sauce.

How it is made

Marinate kingfish or mahi in culantro and garlic, then grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. If you only have cilantro, use more of it and say it is not chadon beni. The two herbs are related and not interchangeable in strength.

How it is used

Eat it with pepper sauce, a slice of avocado, and coconut-scented rice if that is on the table.

Field notes

  • Chadon beni is culantro, Eryngium foetidum, which Trinidadian cooks use by the bunch.
  • It is a different herb from cilantro, though the two are related.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds kingfish steaks, or mahi-mahi if kingfish is not sold
  • 1 cup chopped chadon beni (culantro), or a larger handful of cilantro labeled as a substitute
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 1/2 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 1 lime
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, lime juice, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. If you used cilantro, tell people it is not chadon beni. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Refrigerate the fish if it sits more than a few minutes. Do not leave the garlic marinade out. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill over a hot fire until the thickest steak reads 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve it hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

  • Mahi-mahi replaces kingfish when kingfish is not sold. Name the fish you actually cooked.
  • Leave the seeds out of the pepper if you want the plate less hot.

Barbadian Grilled Flying Fish

A light, herb-seasoned fish with lime, whether it is flying fish or a named substitute.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Flying fish is the fish most associated with Barbadian cooking, often fried or steamed with cou-cou and also grilled at seaside stalls. Oistins is one of the places people go for that fish. When flying fish is not sold where you live, a mild white fish such as tilapia is a substitute and has to be named as one. Do not call the substitute flying fish.

Cultural background

The seasoning is thyme, garlic, onion, lime, and a mild pepper. The fish is small and quick on a hot grill. Cou-cou, a cornmeal side, is a pairing and is not cooked in this recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Flying fish is mild and a little sweet, so thyme and lime do the seasoning instead of a heavy sauce. A short grill keeps it from drying. A substitute white fish can take the same herbs, and it will taste like that fish, which is why the name on the plate has to stay honest.

How it is made

Season flying fish, or a stated mild white fish, and grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Dry the fillets well. Small fish stick and tear if they go onto a wet grate.

How it is used

Eat it with lime, a little pepper sauce, and cou-cou or rice if you have it.

Field notes

  • Flying fish is the fish most associated with Barbadian cooking.
  • When it is not sold where you live, a mild white fish is a substitute and should be named as one.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds flying fish fillets, or 2 pounds mild white fish such as tilapia if flying fish is unavailable
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 small onion, grated
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 lime
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  3. Grill the fillets until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C) and the fish is opaque and flakes. Small fillets may be done in only a few minutes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Bahamian Grilled Spiny Lobster

Spiny lobster grilled with lime, garlic, and a little pepper.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

The lobster of the Bahamas is spiny lobster, a warm-water animal without the big claws of a cold-water lobster. Grilling a split tail is a common cook on the islands. This version is lime, garlic, and a little goat pepper or other mild heat. It is not the Antiguan grill, which is built on butter and thyme.

Cultural background

Spiny lobster tails are shellfish. They are done when the flesh is opaque and pearly and has reached the shellfish temperature. A clawed lobster is a different animal and not what this page describes.

Why it tastes this way

Lime and garlic stay bright on a lean, sweet shellfish, and a short grill over coals adds a little browning without a dairy sauce. The Bahamian pepper is floral and hot, used in a small amount so the lobster remains the taste. Butter and thyme would push the plate toward the Antiguan version, so they are left off here.

How it is made

Grill split spiny lobster tails with lime and garlic until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Split the tail and grill it shell side down first so the meat cooks in the shell instead of drying on the open grate.

How it is used

Eat it with lime, peas and rice, and a simple slaw.

Field notes

  • Bahamian lobster is spiny lobster, which does not have the big claws of a cold-water lobster.
  • This lime and garlic grill is a different plate from the Antiguan butter and thyme lobster.

Ingredients

  • 4 spiny lobster tails, about 8 ounces each, split lengthwise
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 limes
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 1/2 mild hot pepper such as a Bahamian goat pepper, seeded and minced

Steps

  1. Loosen the meat from the shell but leave it in place. Rub it with oil, garlic, salt, lime juice, and pepper. It is raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  2. Heat a grill to medium-high. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  3. Grill shell side down, then turn, until the flesh is opaque and pearly and the center reads 145°F (63°C).
  4. The lobster should already be opaque and at 145°F (63°C). Serve with more lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Saint Lucian Coal Pot Chicken

Charcoal chicken with garlic, thyme, and a local pepper, cooked over a small clay stove.

25 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

A coal pot is a small clay charcoal stove used in Saint Lucia and across the eastern Caribbean. Chicken cooked on one is a home grill: seasoned, set over the coals in the pot's crown, and turned until it is done. This page is that Saint Lucian chicken. It is not a smokehouse bird and not a jerk pit, even though thyme and hot pepper appear in all of them.

Cultural background

The seasoning is garlic, thyme, chive, lime, and a minced hot pepper. The coal pot is the fire. A small charcoal grill is the home substitute if you do not own a clay pot, and it should be described as a substitute.

Why it tastes this way

A coal pot burns a short bed of charcoal close to the meat, so the skin browns and the thyme and garlic stay on the surface. Lime keeps the seasoning sharp. The taste is a backyard charcoal chicken, smaller and more direct than a long smoke.

How it is made

Season chicken and grill it over a coal pot, or a clearly labeled substitute grill, to a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Wait until the charcoal in the coal pot is covered in ash. Cooking over smoking, unlit coals tastes like fuel.

How it is used

Eat it with boiled green fig and saltfish if that is the day's starch, or with rice and a green salad.

Field notes

  • A coal pot is a small clay charcoal stove, not a smoking barrel.
  • Coal pots are used across the eastern Caribbean; this page is the Saint Lucian chicken.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, cut into pieces
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 2 tablespoons chopped chives
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 lime
  • 1 small hot pepper, seeded and minced
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Rub the chicken with garlic, thyme, chives, salt, lime juice, pepper, and oil. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Light charcoal in a coal pot and wait until it is ashed over. If you use another grill, say it is not a coal pot. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  3. Grill the chicken, turning, until the thickest piece reads 165°F (74°C) away from the bone.
  4. Rest it briefly. Every piece should have reached 165°F (74°C). Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Poulet Boucané

Smoked chicken with garlic, thyme, and a West Indian pepper.

30 min prep · 2 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Poulet boucané is smoked chicken in the Creole cooking of Martinique and Guadeloupe. Boucané means smoked, and the word sits in the same Caribbean family as French boucan, a frame for drying and smoking meat. The chicken itself is a later Creole plate, seasoned with garlic, thyme, and piment. This page pins the map on Martinique and counts Guadeloupe as the other island where the same name is used.

Cultural background

This is a smokehouse or covered-smoker bird, not the open coal-pot grill of Saint Lucia and not Jamaican jerk. The smoke is part of the name. A home kettle with a small wood chunk is a substitute and should be called a home smoker, not a boucan.

Why it tastes this way

Smoke settles on seasoned skin, and thyme and garlic stay recognizable underneath it. The pepper is a Caribbean chile, used in a measured amount so the thyme still shows. Because the bird cooks longer than a quick grill, the flavor is rounder and less sharp than coal-pot chicken.

How it is made

Season a chicken and smoke it until the thickest meat reaches a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep the smoke thin and the temperature moderate. Bitter, heavy smoke will cover the thyme.

How it is used

Serve it with rice, red beans, and a little pickled chile.

Field notes

  • On a Martinique or Guadeloupe menu, boucané means the meat has been smoked.
  • The same name is used on both islands. This pin is Martinique, and Guadeloupe is the other country listed.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 West Indian hot pepper, seeded and minced
  • 1 small onion, grated
  • Wood chunks for a smoker, such as a mild fruit wood

Steps

  1. Rub the chicken with garlic, thyme, salt, oil, pepper, and onion. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not leave the paste out. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Heat a smoker or a covered grill set up for indirect heat to about 300°F (150°C), with a thin stream of smoke. A home kettle is not a traditional boucan.
  3. Cook until the thickest part of the thigh and the breast each read 165°F (74°C), not touching bone.
  4. Rest the chicken briefly. It should have reached 165°F (74°C). Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A cut-up chicken smokes faster than a whole bird. Check every piece.
  • No smoker: a covered grill with a small wood chunk is a home substitute. Say so.

Antiguan Grilled Lobster

Lobster grilled with butter, thyme, and a local hot pepper.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Grilled lobster in Antigua and Barbuda is a butter, thyme, and hot-pepper cook, different from the lime and garlic spiny lobster of the Bahamas. The animal is usually a warm-water lobster tail. Butter carries the thyme and the pepper onto the meat as it browns. This page keeps those three seasonings and does not copy the Bahamian plate.

Cultural background

The written recipe uses butter, so it is not dairy-free. The pepper is minced into the butter rather than served only as a side sauce. The flesh is shellfish and is cooked until opaque.

Why it tastes this way

Butter browns at the edge of the shell and holds thyme against the sweet meat. A local hot pepper, the kind grown in the eastern Caribbean, adds heat without the citrus-first taste of the Bahamian grill. The result is richer and more herbal.

How it is made

Grill lobster tails with thyme butter and a minced hot pepper until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Melt the butter with the thyme before it goes on the lobster so the herb actually flavors the fat.

How it is used

Serve with more thyme butter, bread if you want it, and a green salad. Bread adds wheat and is a pairing, not part of the lobster.

Field notes

  • Antiguan grilled lobster is seasoned with butter, thyme, and a local hot pepper.
  • It is a different seasoning from the Bahamian lime and garlic lobster.

Ingredients

  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 small Antiguan or other hot pepper, seeded and minced
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

  4. The lobster should already be opaque and at 145°F (63°C). Serve with the remaining warm butter. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Jamaican Roast Breadfruit

Soft, roasted breadfruit with a browned skin and a mild, starchy center.

10 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Breadfruit is a tree fruit from the Pacific. It was brought to the Caribbean in the late eighteenth century, including on the British voyage that stocked Jamaican gardens, as food for enslaved people on plantations. Jamaican cooks made the fruit their own. Roasting it on coals is one ordinary way to cook it. This is a cook of the fruit, not a meat dish.

Cultural background

There is no meat, fish, or shellfish in the recipe. The fruit is set on coals until the stem end loosens and the center is soft. It is a side beside jerk or a plate by itself with salt and lime.

Why it tastes this way

The coals dry and char the skin while the starchy interior steams. That contrast is the flavor: a little smoke, a soft crumb, and almost no seasoning. The fruit traveled from the Pacific into Caribbean yards, and the roast tastes of that adopted staple rather than of a sauce.

How it is made

Roast a whole breadfruit on coals until the center is soft. This cooks the fruit and nothing else.

Authentic touch. Turn the fruit a few times so one side does not carbonize before the center softens. The stem pulls when it is ready.

How it is used

Split it, scoop out the center core, and eat the flesh with salt and lime. It can sit beside jerk, and it is still not meat.

Field notes

  • Breadfruit was carried from the Pacific to the Caribbean in the late eighteenth century.
  • Roasting it on coals cooks the fruit. There is no meat in the dish.

Ingredients

  • 1 ripe but firm breadfruit, about 3 pounds
  • Coals from a wood or charcoal fire
  • 1 teaspoon salt, at the table
  • 1 lime, cut into wedges

Steps

  1. Leave the breadfruit whole. There is no meat to season. Cut a small cross at the stem so steam can leave.
  2. Set it directly on ashed-over coals, not in an open flame.
  3. Turn it until the skin is well browned all over and a skewer into the center meets soft flesh, about an hour. The stem end should pull.
  4. Cool it until you can handle it, peel it, and cut away the central core. Season with salt and lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This remains a fruit, not a meat dish.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A peeled wedge in a pan over the fire is easier to turn. It is the same fruit.
  • Butter at the table is common and would add dairy. This written recipe has none.
