Central Texas Salt-and-Pepper Brisket

Beefy and peppery, with a dark bark and a slice that is smoky rather than sweet.

30 min prep · 12 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

In meat-market towns such as Lockhart, German- and Czech-speaking butchers smoked beef, including the tough chest cut that became brisket, over post oak. African American pitmasters worked those fires and ran their own pits, so the craft is both a Central European butcher tradition and a Black Texas one. Salt and coarse black pepper became the seasoning that defines the style. Sauce, if a market offers it, is optional and is not the point.

Cultural background

This is sliced Central Texas brisket, not East Texas chopped beef in tomato sauce and not a Kansas City burnt end. The cook is a long indirect smoke.

Why it tastes this way

Post oak burns milder than mesquite, so the smoke stays in the background while salt and coarse pepper make the bark. A long cook renders the fat cap and softens collagen that a steak temperature would leave chewy. The dry Central Texas air and an offset pit are why the surface can darken without steaming.

How it is made

Season a whole brisket with salt and coarse pepper only, then smoke it indirectly over post oak until the flat is tender.

Authentic touch. Leave the fat cap about a quarter inch thick, and do not wrap the brisket until the bark is the color you want.

How it is used

Sliced across the grain, with the point and flat both on the board. Sauce stays on the side if it is there at all. White bread, raw onion, and pickles are the usual companions.

Field notes

  • Post oak is the wood Central Texas pitmasters usually name for this kind of brisket.
  • Lockhart is one of the meat-market towns associated with salt-and-pepper beef.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole beef brisket, about 12 pounds, fat cap trimmed to about 1/4 inch
  • 1/4 cup kosher salt
  • 1/4 cup coarse black pepper
  • Post oak chunks, or other oak if post oak is unavailable
  • Water in a spray bottle

Steps

  1. Mix the salt and pepper and coat the brisket on all sides. Refrigerate it if you season it ahead, and do not leave it on the counter.
  2. Heat an offset smoker or a covered grill for indirect cooking to about 250°F (121°C). Burn post oak, or another oak. Keep the fire clean rather than smoldering wet chips.
  3. Smoke the brisket fat cap up until the thickest part of the flat reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance. Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; brisket is taken higher so it will slice instead of bounce. The stall, often around 150–170°F (65–77°C), is evaporative cooling.
  4. Rest the brisket at least 30 minutes before slicing across the grain. A 3-minute rest is the safety minimum; a large brisket needs longer. Serve sauce on the side only if you want it.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A butcher paper wrap during the stall keeps the bark drier than foil.
  • The point can be cubed later for burnt ends, which is a Kansas City plate rather than this one.

Eastern North Carolina Whole Hog

Chopped pork, sharp with vinegar and red pepper, both lean and fatty in each bite.

40 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings from a home shoulder and belly

History

Eastern North Carolina barbecue is whole-hog cooking, chopped and dressed with vinegar and pepper, with no tomato in the sauce. The recipe here is a home version that uses a pork shoulder and a strip of pork belly, labeled as such because most kitchens cannot cook a whole hog. Enslaved pitmasters did this labor, and Black pitmasters carried it into the public trade, while the vinegar habit fits English and Scots-Irish settlers. No single inventor owns the plate.

Cultural background

Chopped eastern Carolina hog in a vinegar-pepper sauce. The written cook is shoulder plus belly, not a whole animal, and it is not Lexington shoulder.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar cuts hog fat in a humid climate, and cayenne and black pepper were the heat on hand. Mixing a little belly into shoulder copies the way a whole hog eats, with fat against lean. The sauce is the seasoning, not a glaze.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and a piece of belly, chop them together, and dress the chop with vinegar and pepper.

Authentic touch. Chop the pork while it is still warm and dress it with sauce. Do not leave the meat in dry chunks.

How it is used

Piled on a tray or a bun with more sauce on the table. Coleslaw beside it is the vinegar kind, not a mayonnaise slaw.

Field notes

  • Eastern North Carolina sauce is vinegar and pepper, without tomato, which is how it differs from Lexington dip.
  • A whole hog was plantation and public-pit work. This page uses shoulder and belly so a home cook can make the same chopped plate.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 7 pounds
  • 1 pound pork belly, skin removed
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt the shoulder and belly. Heat a smoker for indirect cooking at about 250°F (121°C) with hickory or oak.
  2. Stir vinegar, red pepper, black pepper, and the sauce salt. This sauce has no tomato.
  3. Smoke the meats until the shoulder reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and shreds. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; chopped hog is cooked hotter so the collagen softens. The belly should be tender and hot throughout.
  4. Rest the meats, chop shoulder and belly together, and fold in sauce until the chop is moist. Serve the rest of the sauce on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Shoulder alone is acceptable if belly is hard to find; the chop will be a little leaner.

Pairings and uses

  • Feasts
  • vinegar slaw
  • hush puppies
  • white bread
  • Gluten-free

Lexington Pork Shoulder

Smoked pork shoulder chopped and dressed with a vinegar dip that has a little tomato.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Lexington, in the North Carolina Piedmont, cooks pork shoulder rather than a whole hog, and the dip is vinegar reddened with a little tomato or ketchup. That is a different plate from eastern Carolina whole hog, which leaves the tomato out. Pitmasters in the Piedmont, many of them Black, made this the town style. A single inventor is not something this recipe claims.

Cultural background

Piedmont shoulder with Lexington dip. It is not eastern vinegar-only hog and not a thick Memphis or Kansas City sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder fat stands up to vinegar, and a small amount of tomato rounds the dip without turning it into a sweet glaze. Hickory smoke is common in this part of the state. The red slaw beside it uses the same dip, so the plate tastes like one idea.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and chop or pull it, then dress it with a thin vinegar dip that contains a little ketchup.

Authentic touch. Use only enough ketchup to blush the vinegar. If the dip tastes like tomato sauce, it has left Lexington.

How it is used

Chopped on a plate or a tray, with red slaw and more dip. It is often a sandwich, but the bun is optional.

Field notes

  • Lexington dip contains a little tomato. Eastern North Carolina sauce does not.
  • The Piedmont plate is pork shoulder, not the whole hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the dip
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder. Smoke it indirectly over hickory at about 250°F (121°C).
  2. Stir vinegar, ketchup, red pepper, sauce salt, and water into a thin dip. It should look blush-red, not thick.
  3. Cook the shoulder to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), until it pulls. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; this shoulder goes further for tenderness.
  4. Rest, chop, and moisten with dip. Serve extra dip on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pulled in large pieces is fine; many Lexington plates are chopped finer.

Pairings and uses

  • hush puppies
  • dip

South Carolina Midlands Mustard Pork

Smoked pork shoulder with a tangy, yellow mustard sauce.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

The South Carolina Midlands are known for pork shoulder dressed with a mustard sauce. That style is widely linked to German settlement in the region. The link is an association, not a proven single origin, and this page does not name an inventor. It is a different sauce from eastern Carolina vinegar and from Lexington's tomato dip.

Cultural background

Midlands pork with mustard sauce. It is not a Carolina vinegar hog and not a hash, which is the stew served beside some South Carolina pits.

Why it tastes this way

Yellow mustard brings acid and a sharp edge that cuts shoulder fat, and a little sugar keeps it from tasting raw. Smoke from the pit sits under the sauce because the sauce goes on after the shoulder is tender. The yellow color is the regional signal on the plate.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder, then dress the chopped meat with a mustard, vinegar, and sugar sauce.

Authentic touch. Sauce the chopped pork, and also send a bowl of sauce to the table. Do not paint it on so early that the sugars burn.

How it is used

Chopped, with extra mustard sauce, often beside rice and hash or a simple slaw.

Field notes

  • Midlands mustard sauce is widely linked to German settlement, which is not the same as a documented single inventor.
  • The sauce is yellow mustard, so this plate is a different Carolina barbecue from the vinegar hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 cup yellow mustard
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper, for the sauce
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder and smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) until it reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and pulls apart. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; shoulder for chopping goes hotter.
  2. Stir mustard, vinegar, brown sugar, pepper, and salt into a sauce. Warm it only until the sugar dissolves.
  3. Rest the shoulder, chop or pull it, and fold in sauce.
  4. Serve the remaining sauce on the side. The mustard is an allergen.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spicier sauce can take more cayenne. The mustard stays the base.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • rice
  • barbecue hash
  • sliced onion
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Memphis Dry-Rubbed Ribs

Pork ribs with a paprika, sugar, and pepper crust and no sauce on the meat.

25 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Memphis barbecue is pork, and one of its defining plates is a rib coated in a dry rub and served without sauce on the meat. Black pitmasters and Black neighborhoods built much of the city's public barbecue. A wet shoulder is a different Memphis plate. This one stays dry.

Cultural background

Dry-rubbed pork spare ribs. Sauce is not brushed on these ribs. A sauced shoulder is the wet Memphis entry, not this one.

Why it tastes this way

Paprika, salt, pepper, and a little sugar form a bark in hickory smoke. Leaving the sauce off lets that crust stay crisp instead of steaming. The sweetness is in the rub, not in a molasses glaze.

How it is made

Rub pork ribs and smoke them indirectly, with no sauce on the meat.

Authentic touch. If someone wants sauce, put it in a cup. Brushing it on this rib turns the plate into something else.

How it is used

Whole bones on paper, with pickles and white bread. Any sauce is strictly on the side.

Field notes

  • A Memphis dry rib is defined by the rub on the meat and the absence of sauce on top.
  • Memphis wet shoulder is a separate plate that uses a thin tomato-vinegar sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 racks pork spareribs, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons paprika
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 teaspoons garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoons onion powder
  • Hickory wood for the fire

Steps

  1. Pull the membrane from the bone side if it is still attached. Mix the rub and coat both sides.
  2. Set a smoker for indirect heat around 250°F (121°C) with hickory.
  3. Cook until the meat has drawn back from the bone ends and the thickest meat is about 195–203°F (90–95°C). Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest; tenderness comes later.
  4. Rest briefly and serve with no sauce on the ribs.

Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest. For tender ribs, cook until the meat has pulled back from the ends of the bones, often around 195–203°F (90–95°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Baby back ribs cook faster than spareribs. Use a thermometer rather than the clock.

Kansas City Burnt Ends

Cubes of brisket point, tender, peppery, and sweet-smoky at the edges.

40 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Kansas City sat on rail lines that moved cattle and cooks, and its barbecue became known for a sweet, smoky plate. Henry Perry, a Black cook from the Memphis area, is the early figure local history credits with selling barbecue in the city in the early twentieth century. Burnt ends are cubes from the fatty point of the brisket, returned to the pit. This page credits Perry as that early local figure without claiming he invented every later sauce.

Cultural background

Point-muscle cubes finished sweet and smoky. They are a Kansas City plate, not a plain Central Texas slice, though both start with brisket.

Why it tastes this way

The point is fattier than the flat, so cubes stay moist while the edges caramelize. A sweet glaze meets pepper and smoke because the city sauce tradition runs sweeter than Texas salt and pepper. The second cook is what makes an end, not a single slice of brisket.

How it is made

Smoke a brisket point, cube it, glaze it, and return it to the pit until the edges are sticky.

Authentic touch. Use the point, not the lean flat. Flat cubes dry out before they become burnt ends.

How it is used

In a pile, with bread and pickles. They are also eaten on a sandwich.

Field notes

  • Burnt ends are cut from the brisket point, the fattier of the two muscles.
  • Local Kansas City history credits Henry Perry as an early barbecue seller there, without a claim that one cook invented the later sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 brisket point, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper
  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • Oak or hickory for the fire

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the point. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) until it is about 190°F (88°C) inside and the bark is set.
  2. Stir ketchup, molasses, brown sugar, vinegar, and paprika into a glaze. This glaze uses molasses, not honey, and it leaves out Worcestershire so the plate stays free of that fish and wheat ingredient.
  3. Cube the point, toss with glaze, and return the cubes to the pit until they are sticky and the center of a cube is about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; burnt ends are cooked hotter for tenderness.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole brisket can be sliced for the flat while the point is cubed for ends.

  • sweet sauce

Alabama White-Sauce Chicken

Smoked chicken with a peppery, tangy mayonnaise sauce.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Alabama white sauce is a documented restaurant invention: Big Bob Gibson in Decatur, in the 1920s, served smoked chicken with a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce. It is not a folk sauce of unclear age. The chicken is still a smoked bird; the white sauce is what makes the plate recognizable.

Cultural background

Smoked chicken dressed with mayonnaise white sauce from Decatur. It is not a vinegar mop and not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries pepper, acid, and a little sugar onto skin that has taken hickory smoke. The sauce is cool and sharp against the rich skin. Because it is mayonnaise, it is an egg sauce and it should stay cold until it is served.

How it is made

Smoke chicken indirectly and serve it with a cold mayonnaise, vinegar, and pepper sauce.

Authentic touch. Smoke the chicken until the skin is set, then sauce it. Do not boil the mayonnaise sauce or it will break.

How it is used

Jointed, with sauce over the meat or on the side, often with pickles and white bread.

Field notes

  • Big Bob Gibson's restaurant in Decatur is the documented 1920s source of Alabama white sauce.
  • The sauce is mayonnaise and vinegar, so it contains egg and is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, halved
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 cup mayonnaise made from egg, oil, and vinegar
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper, for the sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt, for the sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the chicken halves. Heat a smoker for indirect cooking at about 275°F (135°C).
  2. Whisk mayonnaise, vinegar, pepper, cayenne, salt, and sugar. Refrigerate the sauce. Do not leave mayonnaise in the sun.
  3. Smoke the chicken until the thickest part of the thigh, not touching bone, reaches 165°F (74°C).
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve with the cold white sauce. Do not serve the chicken rare.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Half chickens cook more evenly on a small grill than a whole bird crowded into the center.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • white bread
  • pickles
  • potato salad
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Owensboro Mutton

Smoky mutton or lamb shoulder with a dark, peppery Worcestershire dip.

30 min prep · 7 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Owensboro, Kentucky, made mutton its barbecue, closely tied to Catholic parish picnics that cooked sheep for crowds. The dark table sauce is called black dip, heavy on Worcestershire and black pepper. This is western Kentucky's plate, not a pork shoulder with a Carolina sauce. Burgoo is the stew that often sat beside the same pits.

Cultural background

A home covered braise for beef cheek in the South Texas barbacoa de cabeza tradition. It is not a Lockhart brisket and not a whole-head pit cook.

Chopped or shredded, with onion, cilantro, and salsa, often in a warm tortilla. The tortillas are corn in this recipe.

Salt a pork shoulder and cook it covered, with a little water, until it shreds. This is not an imu.

History

Cabrito in Monterrey is kid goat, cooked on a spit or opened over an open fire. Nuevo León made the dish a restaurant and family standard in the twentieth century, in a city already tied to northern ranching. The animal is young, which is what the word means. A full-grown goat is a different cook.

Cultural background

This is Monterrey goat, not a lamb barbacoa and not a pork pibil. Restaurants often open the kid flat beside glowing coals. A home cook with a leg or a small shoulder should say that the cut is not a whole kid.

Why it tastes this way

Kid goat is leaner than a pork shoulder, so the taste is mild and a little sweet if the fire is gentle. Mesquite or oak coals in this dry northern city brown the skin without a sweet glaze. Salt is the seasoning that lets the meat stay the point of the plate.

How it is made

Salt a kid, or a home-size leg labeled as not the whole animal, and roast it on a spit or beside coals to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep the fire beside the meat or under a steady bed of coals. A roaring flame dries a lean kid before the center is done.

How it is used

Cut it into pieces and eat it with flour or corn tortillas, salsa, and beans. The tortillas are pairings.

Field notes

  • Cabrito means a young goat, not a full-grown animal.
  • In Monterrey it is cooked on a spit or opened flat beside the fire.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in kid goat leg, about 4 pounds, labeled as a home cut and not a whole kid
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • 4 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Wood or charcoal for a spit or an open grill

Steps

  1. Rub the leg with oil, salt, pepper, and garlic. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it until the fire is ready. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Spit the leg or set it beside a bed of coals, not in open flame. This is the Monterrey fire, scaled to one leg. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part, away from bone, reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Carve the rested leg. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Oaxacan Tasajo

Thin salted beef, grilled quickly, chewy at the edge and beefy in the middle.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tasajo is thin salted beef sold in Oaxaca markets and grilled to order. Salting and drying were how butchers kept beef before refrigeration, and the grilled slice became a market breakfast and a fonda plate. This home recipe does not ask you to cure meat in the sun. Buy market tasajo if you can, or salt a thin steak briefly in the refrigerator.

Cultural background

Oaxacan tasajo is beef, not pork cecina from the same market stall, though the two are often grilled side by side. It is a hot, fast grill. It is not a long smoke.

Why it tastes this way

Salt concentrates the beef, and the thin cut takes a hard sear before the inside dries out. The Central Valleys grill is charcoal, close to the meat, so the surface browns while the slice stays small. Onion and salsa are the fresh contrast buyers expect at the stand.

How it is made

Grill thin salted beef hot and fast to a whole-muscle temperature. Do not sun-dry meat at home.

Authentic touch. Keep the slice thin. A thick steak salted for an hour is only a seasoned steak, not market tasajo.

How it is used

Eat it with corn tortillas, grilled spring onion, salsa, and a radish. Cecina and chorizo on the same grill are separate meats.

Field notes

  • Tasajo is beef that Oaxaca markets sell already salted and thin for the grill.
  • This recipe does not include home curing or sun-drying.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds beef skirt or flank, sliced 1/4 inch thick
  • 2 teaspoons salt, for a short refrigerator seasoning, or 1 pound purchased tasajo
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 spring onions
  • Lime wedges

Steps

  1. If you are not using market tasajo, salt the thin beef and refrigerate it for 1 hour, then pat it dry. That is seasoning, not a cure. Do not dry it in the sun. The meat is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Oil the slices lightly. Grill the spring onions beside them. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the beef until the thickest slice reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. The rested slices should have reached 145°F (63°C). Eat them hot with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Yucatán Tikin Xic

Fish stained with achiote and sour orange, gently charred, sometimes steamed a little by a leaf.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tikin xic is a Yucatecan fish cook: achiote and sour orange on a fillet or a split fish, sometimes wrapped in banana leaf and grilled. Achiote is a native American seed. Sour orange arrived after Spanish contact. The leaf is a wrapper cooks use when they have it. This page does not turn the grill into a ceremony.

Cultural background

The dish is fish, in the same seasoning family as Yucatecan pork, with a different animal and a shorter cook. A home kitchen without banana leaves can use parchment and should say so.

Why it tastes this way

Achiote colors the fish and adds a peppery earth taste, and sour orange keeps it bright. If a banana leaf is on the grill, the fish steams a little as well as browns. Without the leaf, the same paste tastes more directly of the fire. Either way the fish, not the wrapper, is the center.

How it is made

Coat fish with achiote and sour orange and grill it, in a leaf if you have one, until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Oil the leaf or the parchment so the paste does not stick and tear the cooked fish.

How it is used

Eat it with corn tortillas, pickled onion, and a tomato salsa.

Field notes

  • Achiote and sour orange are the Yucatecan seasoning pair used here on fish.
  • A banana leaf wraps the fish when cooks have one; it is not itself the seasoning.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds firm white fish fillets, such as snapper or grouper
  • 2 tablespoons ground achiote (annatto)
  • 1/4 cup sour orange juice, or 2 tablespoons orange juice plus 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 banana leaf, or parchment paper

Steps

  1. Mix achiote, juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Wrap the fish in a banana leaf, or in parchment if you have no leaf. Parchment is not a banana leaf. Refrigerate it if it waits more than 2 hours. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill the packet over a medium-hot fire, turning once, until the center of the thickest fillet reads 145°F (63°C) and the fish is opaque and flakes.
  4. Open the packet carefully. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Eat it the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grouper, snapper, or another firm white fish all work. Cook each to the fish temperature.
  • No banana leaf: use parchment, and say the packet is not the leaf.

Jamaican Jerk Chicken

Chicken with allspice, thyme, and Scotch bonnet, smoky if the fire has pimento wood.

35 min prep · 55 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Jerk is tied to Maroon communities in Jamaica, people of African descent who escaped slavery and cooked seasoned meat over pimento wood in the hills. Allspice is the berry of that pimento tree, native to the Caribbean region, and Scotch bonnet is a New World chile. Chicken is the piece most cooks grill now. The older pork shoulder is a separate, longer cook.

Cultural background

The paste is scallion, thyme, allspice, garlic, ginger, and Scotch bonnet. Pimento wood is the traditional smoke. A charcoal grill without that wood is a home fire, and it is not the same smoke. The chicken still has to reach a poultry temperature.

Why it tastes this way

Pimento wood is the allspice tree, so the smoke repeats the spice ground into the paste. Thyme and scallion are green and sharp, and Scotch bonnet brings a high, fruity heat. Chicken fat carries those flavors across a shorter cook than a pork shoulder can stand.

How it is made

Rub chicken with a jerk paste and grill it over coals until the thickest meat reaches a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Burn pimento wood down to coals if you can get it. If you cannot, say the grill is not a pimento fire, and keep the allspice in the paste.

How it is used

Eat it with festival dumplings or bread, roasted breadfruit, and a cold drink. The heat is in the pepper.

Field notes

  • Scotch bonnet is a Caribbean chile, Capsicum chinense.
  • Pimento is the Jamaican name for the allspice tree, and its wood is the traditional jerk smoke.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, cut into 8 pieces
  • 6 scallions, chopped
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme, leaves stripped
  • 1 tablespoon ground allspice
  • 1 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh ginger
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice

Steps

  1. Blend scallions, thyme, allspice, Scotch bonnet, garlic, ginger, salt, oil, and lime juice into a paste. Wear gloves with the pepper. Coat the chicken. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Refrigerate the coated chicken if it sits. Do not leave the garlic paste at room temperature. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  3. Grill over coals, ideally with pimento wood if you have it, turning often, until the thickest piece reads 165°F (74°C) away from the bone. Juices should run clear.
  4. Rest the chicken a few minutes. Every piece should have reached 165°F (74°C). Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Seed the Scotch bonnet for a hot paste. Leaving the seeds in pushes it hotter.
  • A covered charcoal grill is the home tool. It is not a pimento-wood pit unless that wood is on the fire.

Jamaican Jerk Pork Shoulder

Pork shoulder with a heavy allspice and Scotch bonnet paste, cooked until you can slice or chop it.

History

Pork is the older jerk meat. Maroon cooks in the Jamaican hills used pimento wood and a hot, aromatic paste on pig, long before chicken became the more common restaurant order. A shoulder needs hours, not the short grill of chicken pieces, which is why this is a separate recipe. Scotch bonnet is a New World chile, and allspice is a Caribbean tree.

Cultural background

This shoulder is seasoned more heavily than the chicken, and it stays on a covered fire until the center is safe and, if you want it tender enough to chop, much hotter than the sliceable minimum. It is not the chicken recipe with a different label.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder fat melts slowly and carries allspice, thyme, and Scotch bonnet into the meat. A long fire, especially one with pimento wood, dries the surface into a bark while the inside stays moist. Chicken cannot sit that long without going dry, so the pork tastes deeper and more perfumed.

How it is made

Coat a pork shoulder in a thick jerk paste and cook it covered for several hours, to a pork temperature that matches how you will serve it.

Authentic touch. Use a full measure of allspice. The berry should be obvious in the pork, not a faint dusting.

How it is used

Chop or slice it and eat it with breadfruit, festival, or rice and peas.

Field notes

  • Jerk was a pork cook before chicken became the more common restaurant order.
  • A shoulder needs a longer fire than chicken pieces, which is why the two recipes are separate.

Ingredients

  • 5 pounds boneless pork shoulder
  • 8 scallions, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons ground allspice
  • 6 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 2 Scotch bonnets, seeded
  • 6 garlic cloves
  • 1 tablespoon fresh ginger
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 tablespoons cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend scallions, allspice, thyme, Scotch bonnets, garlic, ginger, brown sugar, salt, vinegar, and oil into a thick paste. Coat the pork. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not leave the paste out. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

  3. Cook until the center of the shoulder is at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes if you will slice it. If you want it choppable, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), then rest it.
  4. Slice or chop the rested pork. It should already have reached the temperature you chose in the cooking step. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Cuban Lechón Asado

Skin-on pork with garlic and sour orange, roasted until the skin crackles.

45 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Pigs came to Cuba with Spanish colonization, and enslaved and free Africans cooked a large share of the island's pork, which is the criollo background of lechón asado. A whole pig is the feast; a skin-on shoulder is the home lechón and should be labeled as that. The English word barbecue passed through Spanish barbacoa, from a Taíno word for a framework of sticks, a Caribbean path into English rather than a single recipe. Cuban lechón is not that Taíno framework, and it is not Haitian boukannen.

Cultural background

This page is the spit or the covered roast of a whole pig, scaled at home to a skin-on shoulder. The seasoning is Cuban mojo. The oven shoulder that follows on its own page is a different, wetter cook.

Why it tastes this way

Sour orange and a large amount of garlic season the fat, and a long fire dries the skin until it blisters. The meat underneath stays porky and sour rather than sweet. Trade brought the citrus and the pig; the island's cooks, including African Cuban cooks, set the amounts. A home shoulder can taste of that mojo even when it is not a whole animal.

How it is made

Season a skin-on pork shoulder, the home lechón, with mojo and roast it on a spit or uncovered until the center is safe and the skin is crisp.

Authentic touch. Dry the skin and salt it. Wet skin will not crackle, no matter how strong the mojo is.

How it is used

Chop the meat and crackling together and eat it with white rice, black beans, and yuca.

Field notes

  • A whole pig is the feast lechón; a skin-on shoulder is the home size and should be called that.
  • The word barbecue reached English through Spanish barbacoa, from a Taíno word for a stick framework.

Ingredients

  • 6 pounds skin-on pork shoulder, the home lechón, not a whole pig
  • 1 cup sour orange juice, or 1/2 cup orange juice plus 1/2 cup lime juice
  • 10 garlic cloves, mashed
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the juice, garlic, oregano, cumin, salt, pepper, and oil. Rub it under the skin and over the meat. The pork is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not store this garlic mojo at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Dry the skin. Spit the shoulder or set it uncovered on a rack. This is the home lechón, not a whole pig. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Roast it, starting moderate and finishing hotter if you want the skin to blister, until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes. For pullable meat, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) before the rest.
  4. Chop the rested pork, skin included if it crackled. The center should already have reached the temperature you cooked to. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A covered grill can stand in for a spit. Say so if you are not actually turning the meat.
  • Sour orange is the acid. Orange juice plus lime is the substitute when sour oranges are not sold.

Thyme, garlic, and Scotch bonnet are the taste of epis, and an open fire dries the surface of the pork. The related words boucan and barbacoa point at a Caribbean way of setting meat over a frame, but the flavor on the plate is Haitian seasoning, not a generic smoke. The pepper is hot, and the thyme stays visible.

How it is made

Season pork with epis and grill or lightly smoke it to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch. Cook epis pork soon after you season it, or refrigerate it. Garlic and oil should not sit out while the fire burns down.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with plantains, pikliz if you already have a jar, and rice.

Trinidadian Grilled Kingfish

Grilled kingfish or mahi with culantro, garlic, and a hot pepper.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Trinidad and Tobago, kingfish is a common grill, and the marinade is built on chadon beni, the local name for culantro, with garlic and thyme. Chadon beni is Eryngium foetidum, a different herb from cilantro, with a stronger leaf. The fish may be kingfish or mahi when that is what the market has. Both are cooked as fish, to the same temperature.

Cultural background

Green seasoning is an everyday Trinidadian blend, not a single bottled brand. This one is culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, and lime. The fish is the meal. It is not a meat dish with a fish name.

Why it tastes this way

Culantro is more pungent than cilantro, so a short marinade still tastes green and garlicky after the grill. Lime keeps the oil from feeling heavy. A hot grate marks the steaks, and the pepper, a Caribbean chile, sits in the fat of the fish rather than in a separate sauce.

How it is made

Marinate kingfish or mahi in culantro and garlic, then grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. If you only have cilantro, use more of it and say it is not chadon beni. The two herbs are related and not interchangeable in strength.

How it is used

Eat it with pepper sauce, a slice of avocado, and coconut-scented rice if that is on the table.

Field notes

  • Chadon beni is culantro, Eryngium foetidum, which Trinidadian cooks use by the bunch.
  • It is a different herb from cilantro, though the two are related.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds kingfish steaks, or mahi-mahi if kingfish is not sold
  • 1 cup chopped chadon beni (culantro), or a larger handful of cilantro labeled as a substitute
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 1/2 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 1 lime
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, lime juice, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. If you used cilantro, tell people it is not chadon beni. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Refrigerate the fish if it sits more than a few minutes. Do not leave the garlic marinade out. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill over a hot fire until the thickest steak reads 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve it hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mahi-mahi replaces kingfish when kingfish is not sold. Name the fish you actually cooked.
  • Leave the seeds out of the pepper if you want the plate less hot.

Guatemalan Churrasco with Chirmol

Grilled beef with a roasted tomato and onion chirmol.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Guatemala, churrasco is a grilled steak, and chirmol is the roasted tomato, onion, and cilantro relish that comes with it. The Spanish word also names the Argentine mixed grill, which is a different meal in a different country. This page stays with the Guatemalan plate. Corn tortillas and a simple salad are the usual company.

Cultural background

Chirmol is chopped or lightly roasted, fresh, and seasoned with lime. It is not Argentine chimichurri and not Honduran chimol, though both are herb and onion relishes. The steak is a whole cut of beef.

Why it tastes this way

A hot grill gives the beef a browned crust, and the chirmol stays raw-tasting and sharp from lime and cilantro. Roasting the tomatoes first makes them sweeter than a fully raw salsa. The contrast is the point: charred meat, bright relish, warm tortillas.

How it is made

Grill a steak to a whole-muscle temperature and serve it with a roasted tomato chirmol.

Authentic touch. Char the tomatoes and onion before you chop them. A fully raw sauce is closer to another country's chimol than to this chirmol.

How it is used

Eat the steak with chirmol, corn tortillas, and a little cabbage or lettuce.

Field notes

  • Chirmol is a fresh roasted tomato and onion relish, not a bottled steak sauce.
  • Guatemalan churrasco is a Central American plate, not the Argentine mixed grill that shares the Spanish word.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds beef steak, about 1 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 3 ripe tomatoes
  • 1/2 white onion
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 lime
  • 1 jalapeño, seeded and minced

Steps

  1. Salt and oil the steak. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Char the tomatoes and onion on the grill, then chop them with cilantro, jalapeño, lime juice, and a pinch of salt. Keep this chirmol cold until you eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the steak until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested steak. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Spoon on the chirmol. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks leave the chirmol fully chopped and raw. This one roasts the tomato and onion first.
  • A thin ranch steak or a thicker sirloin both work if the center reaches the same temperature.

Argentine Asado

Salted beef cooked over wood coals, with chimichurri as a table sauce.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

An Argentine asado is a mixed grill on a parrilla, wood burned down to coals, with salt as the seasoning. Cattle ranching on the pampas made beef the center of the meal. Vacío, a flank cut, and tira de asado, cross-cut beef short ribs, are two cuts the fire is built to hold, and each has its own page here. Chimichurri is the green sauce beside the meat, not a glaze cooked onto it. Indigenous nations of the plains had their own fires; this parrilla is the later criollo cattle cook.

Cultural background

This page is the mixed fire, not a single steak and not a Uruguayan cross. The meat is whole beef. Chimichurri is a pairing, with its own recipe in this file, so it is not repeated as an ingredient here.

Why it tastes this way

Hard woods of the region burn to a steady coal, and the beef cooks in dry heat with little more than salt. Fat bastes the cut as it sits. The flavor is beef and wood, and the sharp parsley sauce is there to cut it afterward. A rushed gas grill without coals will brown the meat and miss the wood.

How it is made

Grill vacío and tira de asado over wood coals with coarse salt, to a whole-beef temperature, and serve chimichurri beside them.

Authentic touch. Salt early, then leave the meat alone. Moving it every minute tears the crust the coals are trying to make.

How it is used

Eat the cuts as they are ready, with chimichurri, bread, and a simple salad. Bread is a pairing and adds wheat.

Field notes

  • Vacío and tira de asado are two cuts a parrilla is built to cook, and each has its own page.
  • Chimichurri is a table sauce. The meat on this fire is salted, not buried in the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef vacío or flank
  • 2 pounds tira de asado, beef short ribs cut across the bone
  • 2 tablespoons coarse salt
  • Wood such as a hard regional firewood, burned to coals

Steps

  1. Salt both cuts. They are still raw and are not ready to eat. Let the salt sit while the wood burns to coals. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Spread the coals under a grill grate. This is a parrilla fire, not a closed smoker. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the thickest part of each cut reads 145°F (63°C). Rest the meat 3 minutes. The rib may stay on longer if you want it looser, which will be past 145°F (63°C).
  4. Slice the rested beef. Both cuts should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Serve chimichurri on the side. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some parrillas serve beef pinker than this guidance. This recipe uses 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest.
  • A kettle grill with wood chunks is a home fire. Say so if it is not a parrilla grate over coals.

Flank grilled over coals, salted, sliced thin across the grain.

History

Vacío is a flank cut on the Argentine parrilla, thinner than a roast and quicker than a rack of ribs. It is its own page because the cut cooks differently from tira de asado and from a mixed grill. The seasoning is coarse salt. Chimichurri stays on the side. Mataderos in Buenos Aires is one neighborhood where Sunday fires still draw a crowd.

Cultural background

This is flank, whole muscle, not short ribs and not a sausage. Slice it across the grain after the rest. It is still an asado cut, cooked on the same kind of wood fire.

Why it tastes this way

Flank is beefy and a little loose, so it picks up coal smoke quickly and stays interesting when sliced thin. Salt is enough because the cut has its own flavor. A long sauce cook would hide the thing the parrilla is trying to show.

How it is made

Salt a flank and grill it over coals to a whole-beef temperature, then slice it across the grain.

Authentic touch. Grill it in one piece and slice after the rest. Precut strips dry out before the center is done.

How it is used

Serve the slices with chimichurri and bread. The bread is a pairing.

Field notes

  • Vacío is a flank cut, thin enough to grill more quickly than a thick rib.
  • It is not short ribs and not a ground sausage.

Ingredients

  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Wood or charcoal burned to coals
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  2. Grill it over coals, turning when the first side is browned. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested flank across the grain. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Choripán

Grilled fresh chorizo in bread, with a sharp green sauce.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 sandwiches

History

Choripán is a grilled fresh chorizo split into bread, a street sandwich of the Río de la Plata asado. The sausage is ground pork, so it has to be cooked through the way other ground meat is. The bread is wheat. Chimichurri or salsa criolla goes on the crumb. It is not a dried Spanish chorizo eaten cold.

Cultural background

Buy a fresh, raw chorizo meant for the grill, often called chorizo parrillero. A fully cooked smoked sausage is a different product. The roll is part of the dish, which is why this page includes wheat.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat, paprika, and garlic inside the sausage drip onto the coals and flavor the crust. The bread catches that fat. A vinegar sauce cuts it. The sandwich tastes of a quick street grill, not of a long cured sausage.

How it is made

Grill fresh chorizo to a ground-meat temperature and serve it in bread.

Authentic touch. Split the sausage lengthwise once the outside is set, so the center can brown and so you can check the temperature in the middle.

How it is used

Put the split sausage in a roll with chimichurri. Eat it as a sandwich.

Field notes

  • The sausage is a fresh chorizo, so it is cooked through like other ground meat.
  • The bread is part of the dish, which is why this one is a sandwich.

Ingredients

  • 6 fresh raw pork chorizos, about 4 ounces each
  • 6 crusty wheat rolls
  • 1/2 cup chimichurri, from the chimichurri recipe or a parsley, garlic, vinegar, and oil sauce
  • A hot grill

Steps

  1. Prick the sausages once if the casings are very tight. They are raw ground pork and are not ready to eat. Ground meat is not ready to eat until it reaches 160°F (71°C).
  2. Grill them over coals or a hot grate, turning, until the center of each sausage reads 160°F (71°C). Split them to check the middle if you need to.
  3. Toast the cut rolls on the grill for a minute. The bread is wheat. Ground meat is not ready to eat until it reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Every sausage should have reached 160°F (71°C) before it goes in the bread. Add chimichurri. Refrigerate leftover meat within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A Uruguayan choripán is the same idea. This page is written from the Argentine street grill.
  • Salsa criolla instead of chimichurri is a common choice on the same sausage.

Brazilian Picanha

Rump cap grilled with its fat cap on, seasoned only with coarse salt.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Picanha is the rump cap of the beef, grilled with the fat cap left on and with coarse salt as the seasoning. It is a defining cut of Brazilian churrasco, strongly associated with the south and now cooked across the country. It is not carne de sol, the salted beef of the Northeast. Some restaurants serve it pinker than the temperature on this page. The guidance here is 145°F (63°C) and a rest.

Cultural background

The fat cap is not trimmed off. It bastes the meat. A long spice paste is not what makes the cut picanha. Steaks or a C-shaped skewer are both used. This page uses a skewer and allows a hot grill.

Why it tastes this way

The fat cap melts over the lean cap of the rump, so each slice tastes richer than a lean steak from the same animal. Coarse salt stays on the surface and crackles. Southern Brazilian churrasco is a wood or charcoal fire, and that dry heat browns the fat instead of steaming it.

How it is made

Salt the fat-on rump cap and grill it on a skewer, fat toward the heat, to a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Score the fat cap in a crosshatch, without cutting into the meat, so the salt has somewhere to sit and the fat can render.

How it is used

Slice it against the grain and eat it with farofa, a vinaigrette relish, and rice. Those sides have their own pages.

Field notes

  • The cut is the rump cap, and the fat cap stays on.
  • Coarse salt is the seasoning. A long spice paste is not what makes it picanha.

Ingredients

  • 2 1/2 pounds beef picanha, rump cap, fat cap left on
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Long metal skewers, or a hot grill if you name it as the home version
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Score the fat cap and salt the meat. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Fold the piece onto skewers with the fat outward, or lay steaks fat side down on a hot grill and say that is the home version. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested meat across the grain. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

  • Thick steaks on a hot grill, fat cap down first, are a home version of the skewer. Say if you are not using a skewer.

Brazilian Costela

Beef ribs cooked a long time beside wood, salty and deeply browned.

20 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Costela is beef rib cooked for hours beside a wood fire in southern Brazil. Gaucho cooks often take it until the meat is tender, which is past 145°F (63°C). That longer cook is a choice of texture. The ribs do not need to reach a brisket temperature to be costela. The safety line for the whole cut is still 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest.

Cultural background

This is beef rib, whole muscle, not pork rib and not picanha. Salt is the seasoning. The fire is open, watched, and slower than a steak. A home oven can finish the same cut and should be called an oven if that is what you used.

Why it tastes this way

A long stay beside wood dries the surface and softens the collagen without a sweet sauce. The fat cap and the bones baste the meat. Southern camps use whatever hard wood is at hand, and the flavor is beef and light smoke rather than a spice crust.

How it is made

Salt a rack of beef ribs and cook it beside an open wood fire, or in a moderate oven, past the whole-muscle safety line if you want it tender.

Authentic touch. Start with the bone side toward the gentler heat. A roaring flame will char the outside long before the inside eases.

How it is used

Slice between the bones and eat it with farofa, vinaigrette, and rice.

Field notes

  • Costela is beef rib, cooked long beside a wood fire in southern Brazil.
  • Many gauchos cook it past 145°F until it is tender. A brisket temperature is not required.

Ingredients

  • 5 pounds beef ribs, in a rack
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • Wood for an open fire, or an oven if you are cooking at home

  4. Slice the rested ribs. They should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Peruvian Anticuchos

Beef heart skewers with vinegar, garlic, and a dried red chile.

History

Anticuchos are beef-heart skewers. Enslaved Africans in coastal Peru, and the Afro-Peruvian cooks who followed them, turned this offal into a skilled street food, and Lima still grills it at night. Aji panca, a dried Peruvian red chile, is the usual pepper. Another dried red chile can be used if you say it is a substitute and not aji panca. The heart is whole muscle and is cooked to at least 145°F (63°C).

Cultural background

This is offal, treated as a regular cook. Vinegar, garlic, cumin, and the dried chile are the marinade. The skewers are street food, eaten with potato and aji.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and aji panca taste sharp and dried-chile sweet, which is how Afro-Peruvian cooks seasoned beef heart for the grill. Charcoal browns the outside while the heart stays a whole muscle. The flavor is that marinade and that fire, tied to a history of labor, not to a garnish.

How it is made

Marinate beef heart in aji panca, vinegar, and garlic, then grill it on skewers to an offal temperature.

Authentic touch. Cut the heart across the grain into even cubes so the skewers finish together and stay tender enough to chew.

How it is used

Eat the skewers with boiled potato and a spoon of the same chile sauce.

Field notes

  • Anticuchos are beef-heart skewers associated with Afro-Peruvian cooking.
  • Aji panca is a dried Peruvian red chile. Another dried red chile is a substitute and not the same pepper.

  • 3 tablespoons aji panca paste, or 2 tablespoons ground dried red chile named as a substitute

  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • Metal skewers

  3. Grill over hot coals, turning, until the center of a cube reads at least 145°F (63°C). Rest the skewers 3 minutes.
  4. The heart should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Serve it hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Peruvian Pollo a la Brasa

Rotisserie chicken with garlic, cumin, and a mild dried red chile.

30 min prep · 1 hr 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Rotisserie chicken became a Lima restaurant food in the mid-twentieth century, and pollo a la brasa is now an ordinary meal across the city. This page does not treat a single founder story as settled fact. The bird is seasoned with garlic, cumin, and aji panca and cooked as poultry to 165°F (74°C). A home oven bird is not a rotisserie unless it actually turns on a spit.

Cultural background

The written marinade has no soy sauce and no beer, so the recipe stays free of those allergens. Restaurant blends vary. The temperature does not. Serve it with fries and a green sauce if you like, as pairings.

Why it tastes this way

Turning the bird in dry heat crisps the skin while cumin and garlic stay in the fat. Aji panca is mild and fruity rather than sharp. Lima's version became popular because it was a whole chicken, seasoned and ready, not because of one secret ingredient. The taste is that spice and that browned skin.

How it is made

Marinate a chicken and roast it on a spit, or in an oven labeled as not a rotisserie, to a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Truss the bird so the heat is even, and check both breast and thigh. One reading is not the whole chicken.

How it is used

Cut it into pieces and eat it with salad, potatoes, and aji verde if you have it.

Field notes

  • Rotisserie chicken became a Lima restaurant food in the mid-twentieth century.
  • This page does not name a single founder as settled fact.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons aji panca paste, or 1 tablespoon ground dried red chile named as a substitute
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon white vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix garlic, chile, cumin, salt, pepper, vinegar, and oil. Rub the chicken under and over the skin. It is raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Spit-roast it if you have a rotisserie. If you do not, roast it on a rack in a 400°F (200°C) oven and say it is not a rotisserie.
  3. Cook until the thickest part of the breast and the thigh each read 165°F (74°C), not touching bone.

30 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 wide skewers

History

Adana kebab is a hand-minced spicy lamb kebab from the city of Adana, pressed onto wide flat skewers. Hot red pepper and lamb fat are part of the mix, not optional decorations. It is a distinct kebab from the milder Urfa version. The meat is ground, so it is cooked through. This home mix uses a knife or a coarse grind and does not claim a guild certificate.

Cultural background

Adana's kebab cooks and the home cooks who follow them make this spicy skewer. It is not Urfa kebab, not chicken shish, and not a stew. The heat is the city's signature.

Why it tastes this way

Fat from the lamb bastes the mince as it sits over charcoal, and hot pepper blooms in that fat. Hand mincing leaves a coarser bite than a fine supermarket grind. A wide skewer keeps the soft meat from falling apart. The result is smoky, rich, and hot, which is why it is not interchangeable with a mild cousin.

How it is made

Knead spicy minced lamb onto wide skewers and grill over hot coals until the center is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Work the mince until it sticks to a flat skewer. Keep a little fat in the mix or the kebab will dry before it browns.

How it is used

With sliced onion, parsley, and grilled tomato. Sumac can be sprinkled at the table.

Field notes

  • Adana kebab is hand-minced lamb with hot pepper, formed on a wide skewer.
  • It is a different kebab from Urfa, which is the milder cousin.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb, coarse
  • 3 oz lamb fat, finely chopped
  • 2 tablespoons hot red pepper flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 4 wide flat metal skewers

Steps

  1. Knead the lamb, fat, pepper flakes, salt, and black pepper until the paste is sticky. Chill 15 minutes.
  2. Wet your hands and press the meat along the wide skewers in a long sausage shape.
  3. Grill over hot coals, turning carefully, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C). Ground lamb is not served pink.
  4. Rest a minute and serve with onion. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A coarse grinder if you cannot hand-mince, with the same pepper and fat.
  • A less fiery amount of pepper for children at the table, still not the Urfa recipe.

Erzurum Cag Kebab

Sliced lamb, salted and stacked, with a grilled edge and a juicy center.

30 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Cağ kebab is associated with Erzurum and a horizontal spit: slices of lamb are stacked on the spit and turned beside the fire. That apparatus is the local method. The home version here is layered sliced lamb grilled on a grate. It is labeled as that, and it is not a horizontal spit. The meat is a whole-muscle cut, salted, not a mince. Do not rename a pan of slices as the Erzurum spit.

Cultural background

Erzurum's spit cooks are the reference, and this page is honest about the home substitute. It is not Adana mince and not doner shaved from a vertical stack.

Why it tastes this way

Thin slices of lamb brown quickly and stay pinkish inside only if you stop early; this recipe cooks them to a safe temperature instead. Salt and lamb fat are the flavor. The horizontal spit bastes the stack with its own drippings. A grate can brown the meat, but it will not copy that slow turning stack, which is why the label matters.

How it is made

Season sliced lamb and grill it on a hot grate. This home version is not a horizontal spit.

Authentic touch. Slice the lamb evenly and grill it in a single layer. Say that this is a home grill, not the horizontal spit, when you serve it.

How it is used

With onion, herbs, and grilled tomato. Eat it hot, as slices.

Field notes

  • Cağ kebab in Erzurum is cooked on a horizontal spit of stacked lamb slices.
  • The home recipe is layered sliced lamb on a grill and is not that spit.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb boneless lamb leg, sliced 1/3 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 onion, sliced, for serving

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb slices with salt, pepper, and oil. This home version is grilled slices, not a horizontal spit.
  2. Heat a grill until it is hot. Lay the slices in one layer.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Give the meat a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with onion. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Lebanese Shish Taouk

Chicken with yogurt, garlic, lemon, and a light char.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Shish taouk is chicken on skewers, a standard of Lebanese grills. A yogurt marinade is common, which means the dish contains dairy and is not vegan. Garlic and lemon sharpen it. The pieces are small so they cook quickly over charcoal. It is poultry, and it has to be cooked through. This is not lamb kafta and not a garlic sauce by itself.

Cultural background

Lebanese restaurants and home cooks grill taouk for a weeknight or a spread. The yogurt makes it dairy. Toum, the garlic sauce, is a separate emulsion and is not in this recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Yogurt clings to chicken and browns in spots, while lemon and garlic stay bright if you do not burn them. Charcoal adds smoke the oven misses. The dairy is part of the flavor, not a garnish, which is why a dairy-free claim would be false for this version. Small cubes cook evenly, which suits breast or thigh if the temperature is checked.

How it is made

Marinate chicken in yogurt, garlic, and lemon, then grill on skewers until the poultry is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Use thigh meat if you can, and wipe off thick yogurt before the grill so it does not carbonize. Check the center of a cube, not the skewer.

How it is used

With salad, pickles, and rice. A garlic sauce on the side is optional and, if it contains egg, is a separate allergen.

Field notes

  • Lebanese shish taouk is chicken on skewers, often in a yogurt marinade.
  • Yogurt means the dish is dairy and not vegan.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb boneless chicken thigh, cut in cubes
  • 1/2 cup plain yogurt
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 lemon, juiced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 4 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, garlic, lemon juice, salt, and oil. Add the chicken and refrigerate 30 minutes.
  2. Thread onto skewers, letting extra yogurt drip off. Heat the grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest cube reaches 165°F (74°C), not touching bone if any bone remains.
  4. Rest a few minutes. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Breast meat, watched closely so it does not dry after it reaches temperature.
  • A dairy-free oil marinade, which would be a different recipe and is not this one.

Baghdad Masgouf

Carp with salt, tamarind, olive oil, and the smoke of a fire beside the fish.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Masgouf is a Tigris fish cook: carp, split open and staked or set beside an open fire in Baghdad, then finished with a brush of sauce. Riverside restaurants made it famous, and families cook it when they can get the fish. If carp is hard to buy, a firm white fish may be eaten as a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf. This recipe is written for carp.

Cultural background

Baghdad cooks along the Tigris are the reference. The dish is carp and fire, not a fried fish and not a stew. A different species does not inherit the name.

Why it tastes this way

Carp is rich, and standing it beside the fire, rather than over a hard flame, renders some of that fat slowly and smokes the skin. Tamarind and salt cut the oil. Olive oil at the end shines it. The river fish and the slow side-fire are the taste, which is why a random white fillet in a pan is a different dinner.

How it is made

Split a carp open, salt it, and cook it beside an open fire until it flakes, then brush it with tamarind and oil.

Authentic touch. Keep the carp beside the fire, skin toward the heat, and do not rush it. If you must use another fish, say so and do not call the plate masgouf.

How it is used

With onion and tomato. The fish is the plate.

Field notes

  • Masgouf is Baghdad's grilled carp, a Tigris fish cook set beside the fire.
  • A firm white fish can be a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole carp, about 3 lb, split open and scaled
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon tamarind paste
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 1 onion, sliced

Steps

  1. Pat the carp dry and salt it. Mix tamarind, olive oil, and turmeric into a brush.
  2. Build an open fire. Set the carp skin-side toward the heat, beside the flames rather than in them, on a rack or stakes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes. Brush with the tamarind mixture near the end.
  4. Serve with onion. This recipe is for carp. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A covered grill with the fish on a rack away from direct flame, still using carp.
  • A little turmeric in the brush, which some cooks add.

How it is made

Knead minced lamb with onion and salt, form it on wide skewers, and grill until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Grate the onion and squeeze out a little liquid so the kebab is moist but not soggy. Press it onto a wide skewer with wet hands.

Omani Shuwa

Lamb with cumin, coriander, and chili, slow-cooked until the fat softens.

30 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Shuwa is lamb cooked for Eid in an earth oven in Oman. The meat is rubbed with spice, wrapped, and buried with fire until it is very tender. A home oven is not a pit. This page uses a covered pot in an oven and says so. The Omani mix is often cumin, coriander, chili, and sometimes a shop blend. There is no single secret formula. The rub below is a simple published-style mix of those spices.

Cultural background

Omani households prepare shuwa for Eid al-Adha and other gatherings. The earth oven is the traditional pot. The home cook here is not digging a pit. It is not a quick grill.

Why it tastes this way

A long, enclosed cook turns lamb fat and spice into one smell: cumin and coriander stay earthy, and chili warms rather than chars. The pit does this by trapping steam and smoke. A covered oven pot keeps the moisture and loses the buried-fire smoke, which is an honest trade. Shop blends vary, so the taste at two houses will not match, and that is normal.

How it is made

Rub a lamb shoulder with a simple cumin, coriander, and chili mix, wrap it, and cook it covered in a home oven until it is safe and tender.

Authentic touch. Wrap the lamb so the spice stays on the meat, and do not uncover the pot for the first half of the cook. Call it a home oven, not a pit.

How it is used

With rice. It is Eid food for the families who make it, pulled or sliced.

Field notes

  • Omani shuwa is Eid lamb cooked in an earth oven, and a home oven is not that pit.
  • Cumin, coriander, and chili are a common mix, and shop blends vary. There is no single secret formula.

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb shoulder, about 5 lb
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 4 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Mix cumin, coriander, chili, salt, garlic, and oil. Rub the shoulder. This is a simple spice mix, not a secret formula. Wrap the meat in parchment and then foil.
  2. Set it in a covered pot. Heat a home oven to 325°F (165°C). This is a home oven, not an earth pit.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest. For a shoulder you want to pull, continue toward 195°F (90°C).
  4. Rest, then slice or pull. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cultural background

Yemeni families and the cooks who learned the dish from them make mandi for gatherings. The home pot is labeled. It is not a quick grill and not a single household's secret.

Why it tastes this way

Rice cooked under the meat takes the drippings and the spice, which is why mandi is a pot and not only a roasted bird. Cumin, coriander, turmeric, and black pepper make a straightforward hawaij-style mix; real blends vary by shop and house. A pit adds smoke a home oven only suggests. How it is made

Rub chicken with a simple spice mix, brown it, and cook it with rice in a covered oven pot until the poultry is done.

Authentic touch. Brown the chicken first so the rice has some roasted drippings. Keep the pot covered. Do not claim a pit you did not dig.

How it is used

Chicken over the rice, with a salad. It feeds a group from one pot.

Gai yang is grilled chicken from Isan, in northeastern Thailand, usually a bird opened flat and cooked over charcoal. Coriander root, garlic, and white pepper are pounded into the marinade, and fish sauce provides the salt. The chicken is often eaten with a chili dipping sauce. Across the Mekong, Lao ping kai is a related grill; this page is the Thai bird.

Cultural background

Isan cooks and Bangkok shops that learned from them grill this chicken. Fish sauce means the dish contains fish as well as chicken. There is no dairy and no wheat in the marinade. The chili sauce can sit beside it.

Why it tastes this way

Coriander root is earthier than the leaf, and with garlic and white pepper it makes a fragrant paste that chars on the skin. Charcoal dries the surface while the thigh cooks through. Fish sauce salts the meat more deeply than a sprinkle of salt at the end. The sour chili dip, if you serve one, is there to cut the fat.

How it is made

Marinate a butterflied chicken with a coriander-root paste and grill it over charcoal until the thigh is done.

Authentic touch. Pound the coriander root and garlic. Chopped leaf coriander is a different flavor and will burn.

How it is used

Chop it into pieces and eat it with sticky rice and a chili dip such as nam jim jaew.

Field notes

  • Gai yang is the grilled chicken of Isan, in northeastern Thailand.
  • Coriander root, garlic, and white pepper are the usual pounded marinade.

Cantonese Char Siu

Sweet-salty pork with a lacquered edge, five spice, and a little char.

30 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Char siu means fork-roast. Cantonese cooks hung strips of pork from forks in a roast oven so the fat dripped away and the glaze lacquered the meat. Roast shops in Guangzhou and other Cantonese cities still sell it that way. This recipe uses a rack in a home oven and says so; it is not a restaurant fork oven; soy sauce and hoisin bring soy and wheat.

Cultural background

Cantonese roast shops are the home of this pork. A home oven on a rack borrows the glaze and not the oven. There is pork, soy, wheat, and sesame oil. There is no dairy. The meat is a whole cut, rested, not a pulled shoulder.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar and maltose brown into a lacquer, and five spice and fermented seasoning sit in that sweetness. Hanging the meat in a shop oven dries the surface. A home rack still lets heat reach all sides, which is closer to a fork roast than a pork sitting in a puddle of sauce. The wheat in the soy sauce is part of that Cantonese seasoning, not an accident.

How it is made

Coat pork strips with a soy, hoisin, and five-spice glaze and roast them on a rack in a home oven until the center is done, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Put the pork on a rack over a pan. If it braises in the glaze, the lacquer never forms.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with rice and a few greens.

Field notes

  • Char siu means fork-roast, from the forks that held the pork in a Cantonese oven.
  • A home oven on a rack is a substitute and should be called a home oven.

Ingredients

  • 2.5 pounds boneless pork shoulder, cut into long strips
  • 3 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
  • 1 tablespoon maltose or brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Stir the soy sauce, hoisin, maltose or sugar, five spice, sesame oil, garlic, and neutral oil. Coat the pork and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a home oven to 425°F (220°C). Set the pork on a rack over a pan. This is a home oven, not a Cantonese fork oven. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Roast, turning once, until the center of the thickest strip reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then slice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

  • Honey in the glaze would add honey. This glaze uses sugar and maltose.

How it is made

Skewer small pieces of lamb, grill them hot, and season them with salt, cumin, and chili as they cook, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Sprinkle the cumin and chili in more than one pass while the skewers turn. One heavy dump falls off into the coals.

How it is used

Eat the skewers as soon as they have rested, while the cumin is still fragrant.

Galbi is Korean grilled beef short rib. The meat is often cut thin across the bone, marinated with soy sauce, sugar, garlic, sesame, and grated pear or apple, then grilled. It is a restaurant and home barbecue dish. Cultural background

Korean barbecue tables grill galbi over charcoal or on a gas grill set into the table. The marinade has soy, wheat, and sesame. There is no dairy. Pear tenderizes and sweetens the surface.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, sugar, and pear brown quickly on a thin rib, and sesame oil perfumes the fat. Garlic stays sharp. The bone and the thin cut mean a short grill, so the marinade becomes a crust instead of a boiled sauce. A thick uncut roast would not taste like galbi even with the same bowl.

How it is made

Marinate thin beef short ribs in soy, pear, garlic, and sesame, then grill them and rest them.

Authentic touch. If the ribs are thick, ask the butcher to cut them flanken-style, across the bones. Thick English-cut ribs are a different grill.

How it is used

Cut the meat off the bone if you like and eat it with rice, lettuce, and ssamjang.

Salt pork belly lightly, grill it until it is cooked and rested, and serve it for lettuce wraps.

How it is made

Salt chicken thigh pieces, skewer them, and grill them over charcoal until the center is done.

Eat the skewers hot. A tare can be offered from the separate recipe if you want the sweet glaze.

Calling it a court recipe would erase that history.

How it is made

Grill thin beef and onion over high heat, rest the beef, and dip it in a soy and sesame sauce.

Authentic touch. Cook a few pieces at a time. A crowded table grill steams the beef and misses the char.

How it is used

Eat it with rice and the dip, straight from the grill.

Turmeric and lemongrass perfume the fat, and chili warms the paste that steams inside the meat. A long cook softens the shoulder after it is already safe to eat. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Hanoi Bun Cha

Grilled pork patties and pork belly with rice noodles, herbs, and a lime-fish sauce bowl.

35 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Bun cha is a Hanoi lunch: grilled pork, rice noodles, herbs, and a sweet-sour bowl of fish sauce, lime, and sugar. The pork is often both a minced patty and a slice of belly. Rice noodles do not contain wheat, so the written dish is gluten-free if you do not add a wheat sauce. It is street and shop food in Hanoi, eaten at midday more than as a court recipe.

Cultural background

Hanoi cooks grill the pork over charcoal and set noodles and herbs on the table. The patties are ground pork. The belly is a whole cut. Fish sauce contains fish. There is no wheat in the noodles or the marinade.

Why it tastes this way

Charcoal sweetens the edges of the pork, and the dipping bowl is sour, salty, and a little sweet, which cuts the fat of the belly. Rice noodles stay mild so the grill and the lime show. Herbs add freshness. Wheat noodles would make a different bowl.

How it is made

Season ground pork and pork belly, grill both to their own temperatures, and serve them with rice noodles and a lime-fish sauce bowl.

Authentic touch. Grill the patties over real heat so they brown. If they only poach in a pan of sauce, they are not bun cha.

How it is used

Put noodles in a bowl, add herbs and the warm pork, and spoon on the dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Bun cha is a Hanoi plate of grilled pork and rice noodles.
  • Rice noodles contain no wheat when they are made only from rice.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound ground pork
  • 1 pound pork belly, sliced 1/4 inch thick
  • 3 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 8 ounces dry rice noodles
  • 1 small head of lettuce, leaves separated

Steps

  1. Mix the ground pork with 1 tablespoon fish sauce, half the garlic, and the pepper. Shape thin patties. Rub the belly with the remaining garlic and 1 tablespoon fish sauce. Refrigerate both. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  2. Stir the remaining fish sauce with lime juice, sugar, and 1/4 cup water for the bowl. Boil the rice noodles, drain them, and rinse them cool. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the patties and the belly over a hot fire. The center of each patty must reach 160°F (71°C). The belly must reach at least 145°F (63°C). Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the belly 3 minutes. Serve pork, noodles, and lettuce with the sauce. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some shops serve only the patties. This page includes belly as well.
  • Extra chili in the bowl raises the heat. The written bowl is mild.

Dominican Wasakaka Chicken

Roasted or grilled chicken with a fresh garlic and herb sauce.

25 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Wasakaka is a Dominican sauce of garlic, herbs, and lime or sour orange, spooned over grilled or roasted chicken. It belongs to the Dominican table, including the pollo a la brasa and pollo al carbón that families buy and cook. The sauce is not Argentine chimichurri and not Cuban mojo, even though garlic and acid appear in all three. The bird is poultry and is cooked through.

Cultural background

The chicken can be grilled or roasted. The wasakaka is mixed the day you eat it and kept cold. Oregano and cilantro, or parsley, are the herbs. This page uses cilantro.

Why it tastes this way

Chicken skin browns over direct heat, and the raw garlic sauce stays sharp because it never goes on the fire for long. Lime or sour orange is the Caribbean acid, and oregano is the dried herb of the Spanish-speaking islands. Together they make the plate taste fresh rather than glazed.

How it is made

Grill or roast chicken to a poultry temperature and dress it with wasakaka mixed the same day.

Authentic touch. Salt the sauce enough to season the chicken. A pale, unsalted wasakaka will taste like wet garlic.

How it is used

Serve with tostones, white rice, and beans. The sauce is spooned at the table.

Field notes

  • Wasakaka is a Dominican garlic and herb sauce for grilled or roasted chicken.
  • It is a Dominican sauce, not chimichurri and not Cuban mojo.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, cut into pieces
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 6 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 3 tablespoons neutral oil, for the sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Oil and salt the chicken. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  2. Stir garlic, cilantro, oregano, lime juice, oil, and salt into wasakaka. Refrigerate it and use it the same day. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The poultry is not ready to eat until the thickest part reaches 165°F (74°C).
  3. Grill or roast the chicken until the thickest piece reads 165°F (74°C) away from the bone.
  4. Rest it briefly. Every piece should have reached 165°F (74°C). Spoon on the wasakaka. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sour orange replaces lime when you can buy it.
  • An oven roast reaches the same poultry temperature if you do not have a grill.
