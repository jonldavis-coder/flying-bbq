English Hog Roast

Salted pork, crackling, and wood smoke, with enough fat to baste the meat.

30 min prep · 4 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Turning a pig over a fire is an old English way to feed a gathering. The everyday Sunday roast is usually an oven joint, and the spit is the older fire method. A whole animal needs a long spit, a steady cook, and more meat than most kitchens can hold, so this home version uses a pork shoulder. Salt, time, and a dry skin do the work that a sweet glaze would only cover.

Cultural background

This is crowd food for English celebrations, not a weekly supper. The shoulder stands in for a whole hog when the spit and the animal are out of reach. It is still a roast, carved, not a pulled-pork sandwich recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Pork shoulder carries fat and collagen, and a wood fire browns the skin while the interior stays moist. English cooks have long used that contrast: a crisp outside and a sliceable or shreddable inside. The oven Sunday joint tastes of the kitchen; the spit tastes of the fire that came before it.

How it is made

Salt a pork shoulder and turn it on a spit over hardwood coals, or roast it on a rack over a pan if the spit is small. Carve when the temperature and the skin are both ready.

Authentic touch. Score the skin and salt it ahead of the fire so the crackling dries. Keep the flames under the fat, not licking the skin into soot.

How it is used

Carved onto plates with apple sauce and a sharp salad. Leftover meat is good cold.

Field notes

  • A home Sunday roast in England is usually cooked in an oven, while the spit is the older open-fire method.
  • A whole hog is still a fair and wedding roast; a shoulder is the practical home cut.

Ingredients

  • 1 pork shoulder, about 6 lb, skin on
  • 2 tablespoons coarse salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • Hardwood chunks or charcoal for the fire

Steps

  1. Pat the shoulder dry, score the skin, and rub it with salt, pepper, and oil. Leave it uncovered in the refrigerator for at least a few hours if you can.
  2. Build a hardwood or charcoal fire with a space to one side so the shoulder is not in direct flame. Set it on a spit or on a rack over a drip pan.
  3. Turn it steadily. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For meat you want to pull or chop, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) in the thickest part.
  4. Rest it, then carve. Crackling should blister, not blacken. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole small pig on a proper spit, for a crew that can tend it all day.
  • The same salted shoulder in a hot oven when there is no spit.

Cornish Grilled Mackerel

Oily, smoky mackerel with lemon, salt, and a charred skin.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 whole fish

History

Mackerel has long been a summer fish off Cornwall, landed in harbors such as Newlyn and eaten while the oils are still bright. Grilling whole fish over a hot fire is a coastal habit, not a restaurant invention. The skin protects the flesh, and the fish needs little more than salt. This page keeps the recipe to that grill.

Cultural background

Cornwall is the place, and the fish is Atlantic mackerel. It is a simple shore supper. It is not a smoked kipper and not a sauced fillet.

Why it tastes this way

Mackerel is rich in oil, so a hot grill browns the skin before the flesh dries. Salt and lemon cut that richness. A wood or charcoal fire adds a light smoke that suits an oily fish better than a thick sauce would.

How it is made

Score whole mackerel, salt them, and grill over a hot fire until the skin blisters and the center flakes.

Authentic touch. Grill the fish the day you buy it. Oil the skin and the grate, and turn only once, or the flesh will break.

How it is used

On the bone, with lemon, brown bread, and a green salad. One fish is a usual serving.

Field notes

  • Mackerel is an oily Atlantic fish that runs off Cornwall in the warmer months.
  • Whole fish hold together on a grill better than fillets of the same catch.

Ingredients

  • 4 whole mackerel, gutted, about 12 oz each
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 lemon, cut in wedges
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Heat a grill until it is hot. Pat the fish dry, oil the skin, and season with salt and pepper. Cut two slashes on each side.
  2. Oil the grate. Grill the fish over the coals, turning once, until the skin is blistered.
  3. Cook the fish to 145°F (63°C) in the thickest part, until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve at once with lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A few fennel stalks in the cavity.
  • Cold leftover fish the next day with horseradish, if it was refrigerated promptly.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • Lemon
  • Brown bread
  • Watercress
  • New potatoes
  • Dairy-free

English Mint Sauce

Sharp vinegar, fresh mint, and a little sugar, meant to cut lamb fat.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Mint sauce is the English table sauce of vinegar, mint, and sugar served with lamb. It belongs next to a roast, not on a grill of its own. Garden mint and malt or white vinegar are the usual pantry pieces. The sauce is mixed and left to stand so the mint softens in the acid. It is not a jelly and not a pesto.

Cultural background

This page is only the sauce. The ingredients are mint, vinegar, and sugar. It is not a barbecue rub, and it is not cooked over a fire.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat is rich, and vinegar cuts it. Mint brings a cool, green note that the British kitchen has long put with spring lamb. A pinch of sugar keeps the vinegar from tasting only harsh. The combination is about balance, not sweetness.

How it is made

Stir chopped mint into vinegar with sugar and a pinch of salt. This is a bowl sauce, not a grill.

Authentic touch. Chop the mint fine and stir it into warm vinegar so the leaves soften. Let it stand before you serve it.

How it is used

Cold or cool, in a jug beside roast or grilled lamb. A spoonful on the chop is enough.

Field notes

  • English mint sauce is vinegar, mint, and sugar, and it is served with lamb.
  • The sauce is not a grill and not a mint jelly.

Ingredients

  • 1 packed cup fresh mint leaves
  • 4 tablespoons malt or white wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons boiling water

Steps

  1. Chop the mint finely. Stir the sugar and salt into the boiling water until they dissolve.
  2. Add the vinegar and the mint. This is a sauce, not a grill, so there is no animal-protein thermometer target.
  3. Let the sauce stand at least 20 minutes so the mint softens in the acid.
  4. Taste and add a few drops more vinegar or a pinch more sugar if you want it sharper or rounder. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little hot water to loosen a very sharp mix.
  • White wine vinegar in place of malt vinegar.

Welsh Salt-Marsh Lamb

Grilled lamb chops with a clean, saline edge from salt-marsh grazing, plus salt and a little fat smoke.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

On the Gower and on other Welsh salt marshes, lambs graze pastures that flood with the tide. That grazing is why the meat is sold as salt-marsh lamb, and Gower salt marsh lamb is a protected name for animals raised there. Other estuaries produce related meat. The chops here are grilled simply, because the point is the lamb, not a heavy marinade.

Cultural background

The cooks are Welsh farmers and the households that buy their chops. The page is about salt-marsh lamb from Wales, including the Gower. It is not a generic mixed grill.

Why it tastes this way

Salt-marsh plants and tidal pasture give this lamb a reputation for a clean, slightly saline taste. A hot grill browns the fat cap without covering that flavor. Lemon or a mint sauce can sit beside it, but the meat should stay recognizable.

How it is made

Grill thick lamb chops over a hot fire, turning once, until the fat is browned and the center meets the safe temperature.

Authentic touch. Buy chops from lamb that actually grazed a salt marsh, then salt them just before the fire. Do not soak them in a sweet sauce.

How it is used

With boiled potatoes, a green vegetable, and mint sauce if you want the sharp classic beside it.

Field notes

  • Gower salt marsh lamb is a protected name for lamb raised on the Gower salt marshes.
  • Salt-marsh grazing is also practiced on other Welsh estuaries, not only on one beach.

Ingredients

  • 8 lamb loin chops, about 1 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 lemon, optional, for serving

Steps

  1. Pat the chops dry. Oil them lightly and season with salt and pepper just before cooking.
  2. Heat a grill until the grate is hot. Grill the chops over the coals, turning once.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) in the thickest part and give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with lemon if you want it. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A garlic and rosemary rub instead of salt alone.
  • Chops from another Welsh salt marsh, named for the place they came from.

Home-smoked Haddock in the Arbroath Manner

Hot-smoked haddock, salty and lightly sweet from the wood, still moist inside.

20 min prep · 1 hr cook · Serves 4 · 4 fillets

History

Traditional Arbroath smokies are a protected smokehouse product, tied to a specific way of hot-smoking haddock in Arbroath. This recipe is a home hot-smoke, not a legal copy of the name-protected product. It uses fillets, a kettle, and a short smoke cooked through for the table. Do not sell it or label it as the protected smokie.

Cultural background

The locale is Arbroath, and the fish is haddock. The page is a home method in that direction. It is not a certificate and not a cold-smoked finnan haddie left silky and raw in the center.

Why it tastes this way

Haddock takes smoke without the heavy oil of mackerel. A short hot smoke over hardwood cooks the fish and leaves a pale gold surface. Salt seasons it. The protected town product has its own pits and tied pairs of fish; a backyard kettle can only suggest that flavor.

How it is made

Salt haddock fillets and hot-smoke them in a covered grill until they flake and reach a safe temperature. Call it a home smoke, not the protected name.

Authentic touch. Keep the smoke thin and the heat gentle. If the wood flares, the fish will taste bitter instead of sweet.

How it is used

Hot, with potatoes and a lemon wedge, or flaked into a chowder later. Eat it as cooked fish.

Field notes

  • Arbroath smokies are a protected smokehouse product and are not a name for any home-smoked haddock.
  • A home hot-smoke cooks the fish for the table and is not a legal copy of that product.

Ingredients

  • 4 haddock fillets, about 6 oz each
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon brown sugar
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • A small handful of hardwood chips, soaked 15 minutes

Steps

  1. Mix the salt and sugar and sprinkle them over the fillets. Rest 20 minutes, then rinse lightly and pat dry. Oil the fish.
  2. Set a covered grill for indirect heat. Add a small handful of drained wood chips to the coals so the smoke stays thin.
  3. Hot-smoke the fillets until they reach 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes. This is a home hot-smoke, not the protected smokehouse product.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Whole headed and gutted haddock if you can tend them without breaking the flesh.
  • Oak or beech if those are the woods you have, kept to a light smoke.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • Potatoes
  • Lemon
  • Peas
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Irish Grilled Mackerel

Atlantic mackerel with charred skin, salt, and lemon.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 whole fish

History

Atlantic mackerel is a regular catch off the Irish coast, and grilling it whole is a straightforward way to cook an oily fish. Harbors on the southwest, including Dingle, land it in season. The recipe is salt, fire, and lemon. It does not need a story about a festival to be worth cooking.

Cultural background

These are Irish coastal cooks and the fish they buy locally. The dish is grilled mackerel, not a smoked herring and not a fish cake.

Why it tastes this way

Cold Atlantic water and a short time from boat to fire keep the oils sweet. High heat crisps the skin. Lemon and salt are enough because the fish is already rich. A smoky grate adds the only extra flavor the dish wants.

How it is made

Oil and salt whole mackerel and grill them hot until the flesh flakes.

Authentic touch. Leave the fish whole and turn it once. Mackerel falls apart if you fuss with it.

How it is used

With a green salad and lemon. One fish to a plate.

Field notes

  • Atlantic mackerel is a common seasonal catch in Irish waters.
  • The fish is grilled whole so the skin holds the oily flesh together.

Ingredients

  • 4 whole Atlantic mackerel, gutted
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • Black pepper
  • 1 lemon

Steps

  1. Heat the grill. Pat the mackerel dry, oil the skin, and season with salt and pepper.
  2. Grill over a hot fire, turning once, until the skin blisters.
  3. Cook the fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve with lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A bay leaf in the cavity.
  • Gooseberry sauce on the side if you already have it, which is a separate sharp fruit sauce.

Irish Lamb Chops

Grilled lamb with browned fat, salt, pepper, and a little garlic.

15 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Lamb is everyday meat in Ireland because the grass grows well in a wet climate. Grilled chops are a simple way to cook it at home. This page does not invent a national barbecue festival for them. The seasoning stays light so the lamb tastes like lamb. Rosemary and garlic are common, not required.

Cultural background

Irish households cook these chops without a special costume or a made-up contest. It is weeknight and Sunday food. It is not a smoked leg and not a minced kebab.

Why it tastes this way

Pasture-fed lamb carries a mild, grassy fat that browns over a hot fire. Salt and a short cook keep that taste in the foreground. Smoke from charcoal is a light extra, not a thick bark. The climate that grows the grass is the reason the meat is here at all.

How it is made

Season lamb chops and grill them over a hot fire, turning once.

Authentic touch. Take the chops out of the cold a short while before cooking so they grill evenly. Rest them before you cut.

How it is used

With potatoes, cabbage or a green salad, and mint sauce if you like it sharp.

Field notes

  • Ireland's wet climate grows the grass that feeds most of its lamb.
  • Grilled lamb chops are ordinary food, and this page does not invent a barbecue festival for them.

Ingredients

  • 8 lamb chops, about 1 inch thick
  • 2 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Rub the chops with garlic, oil, salt, and pepper.
  2. Grill over a hot fire, turning once, until the fat edge is browned.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with potatoes. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A lemon and olive-oil marinade for an hour.
  • Loin chops or thicker barn chops from the shoulder, with the time adjusted.

  • Boiled potatoes
