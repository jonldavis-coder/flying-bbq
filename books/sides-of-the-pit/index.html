Appalachian Chow-Chow

Tangy chopped cabbage, green tomato, and onion in a mustard-seed brine.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 12 · about 6 cups

History

Chow-chow is a chopped vegetable relish kept in mountain and Southern refrigerators and spooned beside beans and greens. This recipe is a refrigerator relish, not a shelf-stable canning recipe, and it gives no processing time for jars. Mustard seed is in the brine, so mustard is an allergen. Use it within a week.

Cultural background

A cold chopped relish for the Appalachian table. Not canned. Not a fermented crock left at room temperature. Cabbage, green tomato, and onion are the body.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar, sugar, and mustard seed pickle the vegetables just enough to stay crisp, which gives a sharp spoonful against rich soup beans or pork. End-of-summer gardens in the mountains are why the relish mixes green tomato with cabbage instead of being a single-vegetable pickle. The refrigerator, not a canner, is what makes this batch safe to describe.

How it is made

Salt chopped vegetables, rinse them, cover them with a hot vinegar brine, cool them, and refrigerate.

Authentic touch. Cool the relish and refrigerate it the same day. Do not seal it in jars for a shelf. This page is not a canning manual.

How it is used

Cold, by the spoon, beside soup beans, cornbread, or pork. It is a condiment.

Field notes

  • This chow-chow is a refrigerator relish, not a shelf-stable canning recipe.
  • Mustard seed in the brine means the relish contains mustard.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups chopped green cabbage
  • 2 cups chopped green tomato
  • 1 cup chopped onion
  • 1 cup chopped green bell pepper
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup sugar
  • 1 tablespoon mustard seed
  • 1 teaspoon celery seed

Steps

  1. Toss the vegetables with the salt and let them stand 30 minutes. Rinse and drain them well.
  2. Simmer vinegar, sugar, mustard seed, and celery seed until the sugar dissolves and the brine is hot, at a simmer around 212°F (100°C).
  3. Pour the hot brine over the vegetables. Cool the bowl. This is not a canning recipe. Do not process jars for shelf storage, and do not give these vegetables a canning time.
  4. Refrigerate and use within a week, holding it below 40°F (4°C). Mustard seed is an allergen.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter chile can be chopped in. It does not change the refrigerator rule.

Appalachian Soup Beans

Creamy pinto beans in a simple broth with onion.

15 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Soup beans are pinto beans simmered into a brothy pot, an everyday Appalachian meal with cornbread. A ham hock is a common seasoning, and this version does not use one. It is a vegetarian pot of beans, onion, garlic, and salt. The beans are boiled until they are completely tender.

Cultural background

A meatless pot of Appalachian soup beans. No ham hock. Serve them with their broth. They are a meal as often as they are a side.

Why it tastes this way

Pintos break down just enough to thicken their own broth, which is the soup in soup beans. Onion cooked the whole time is the savor that a ham hock would otherwise dominate. Cornbread beside the bowl is the mountain habit, because the beans were the daily protein and the bread was the daily grain.

How it is made

Boil dried pinto beans with onion until they are completely tender and still soupy. No pork.

Authentic touch. Leave them brothy. Soup beans are not refried and not drained dry. Spoon off broth with the beans.

How it is used

In bowls, with cornbread and often chow-chow. The cornbread is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • A ham hock is common in soup beans. This pot does not use one.
  • The beans are served in their broth, which is why they are called soup beans.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound dried pinto beans, picked over and rinsed
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 8 cups water

Steps

  1. Rinse the beans and put them in a pot with onion, garlic, and water. Do not add a ham hock.
  2. Bring them to a full boil at 212°F (100°C), boil for several minutes, then simmer.
  3. Cook until the beans are completely tender and the pot is still brothy, usually 1 1/2 to 2 1/2 hours. Add water if they get dry. Do not hold dried beans warm from raw without boiling them.
  4. Salt them once they are soft. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Boil dried beans until they are completely tender. Kidney beans must be boiled hard, not held warm from raw. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A dried red pepper in the pot adds heat without adding meat.

Banana Pudding

Vanilla custard, banana, and soft vanilla wafers.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Banana pudding is a Southern bowl dessert of vanilla wafers, sliced bananas, and custard, served at gatherings that sometimes include a barbecue plate. It is not fire cooking. It does not go on a grill or in a pit. Vanilla wafers bring wheat, the custard brings milk and egg, and the bananas are the fruit.

Cultural background

A chilled dessert side. Not smoked, not grilled, and not a pit technique. It contains dairy, egg, and wheat. It is vegetarian and not vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Custard softens the wafers and coats the banana, so the dessert eats as one spoonful rather than as three ingredients. Vanilla is the flavor that runs through it. It landed on barbecue tables because Southern suppers already knew the bowl, not because smoke improves a custard.

How it is made

Cook a vanilla custard, layer it with bananas and vanilla wafers, and chill it. Do not put it near the fire.

Authentic touch. Cook the custard until it thickens and the egg is heated through, then chill the dish. A raw egg mixture poured over bananas and left warm is not this pudding.

How it is used

Cold, in bowls, after the barbecue or beside it on a crowded table. It is plainly not a fire dish.

Field notes

  • Banana pudding is a bowl dessert. It does not go on the pit or the grill.
  • Vanilla wafers are the usual cookie, and they contain wheat.

Ingredients

  • 4 ripe bananas, sliced
  • 1 box vanilla wafers, about 11 ounces
  • 2 cups milk
  • 3/4 cup sugar
  • 3 egg yolks
  • 3 tablespoons cornstarch
  • 1 teaspoon vanilla extract
  • 1 pinch salt

Steps

  1. Whisk sugar, cornstarch, salt, and egg yolks in a saucepan. This dessert is not cooked on a fire.
  2. Stir in milk and cook over medium heat, stirring, until the custard thickens and reaches 175°F (79°C), so the egg yolks are cooked.
  3. Take it off the heat, stir in vanilla, and cool it 10 minutes. Layer wafers, bananas, and custard in a dish.
  4. Cover and refrigerate until cold, below 40°F (4°C). Serve cold. Do not set the dish on a grill.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A meringue top is another Southern finish. This version stops at custard, bananas, and wafers.

Pairings and uses

  • chopped pork
  • iced tea
  • pecans
  • Vegetarian

Brunswick Stew

A thick chicken stew with corn, lima beans, and tomato.

30 min prep · 2 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Brunswick, Georgia, and Brunswick County, Virginia, both claim Brunswick stew. This page does not settle the argument. The pot here is a chicken-and-vegetable kettle of the kind served beside barbecue, not a reconstruction of a first pot. Chicken is cooked to a poultry temperature, and the vegetables are cooked until soft.

Cultural background

A chicken and vegetable stew claimed by both Georgia and Virginia. This version does not pick a winner. It is a side kettle, not a whole-hog plate.

Why it tastes this way

Long cooking breaks chicken and vegetables into one thick spoonful, with tomato acid against sweet corn and lima beans. That thickness is what picnic lines expect from the stew beside chopped pork. The two Brunswicks can keep their dispute; the pot still tastes like a Southern barbecue side.

How it is made

Simmer chicken with onion, tomato, potatoes, corn, and lima beans until the chicken is safe to eat and the stew is thick.

Authentic touch. Cook it until the spoon stands up a little. A thin chicken soup is not yet the stew people mean.

How it is used

In bowls, beside chopped pork or on its own.

Field notes

  • Brunswick, Georgia, and Brunswick County, Virginia, both claim this stew.
  • This pot is a chicken-and-vegetable kettle, not a settlement of that argument.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds bone-in chicken thighs
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 potatoes, diced
  • 1 can crushed tomato, 15 ounces
  • 2 cups frozen corn
  • 2 cups frozen lima beans
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 6 cups water

Steps

  1. Put chicken, onion, potatoes, tomato, salt, pepper, and water in a heavy pot.
  2. Simmer until the chicken reaches 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, away from bone, and is easy to shred. Do not serve the chicken rare.
  3. Remove the bones, return the meat, and add corn and lima beans. Cook until the vegetables are tender and the stew has thickened.
  4. Taste for salt. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Both Georgia and Virginia claim the name; this pot does not decide it.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some older descriptions use other meats. This written kettle is chicken and vegetables.

  • white bread
  • pickles

Central Texas Pinto Beans

Soft pinto beans with onion, garlic, and salt.

15 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Pinto beans are the usual pot beside Central Texas barbecue. Many pits season them with salt pork or a piece of bacon, and this version does not. It is a meatless pot of beans, onion, garlic, and salt, so it can sit next to any plate. Salt pork is a common addition you are not using here.

Cultural background

A vegetarian pot of pinto beans. No salt pork in this batch. The beans must be boiled until they are completely tender. This is a side, not the brisket.

Why it tastes this way

Long boiling turns dried pintos creamy, and onion and garlic are enough seasoning when the meat on the board is already salty and smoky. Leaving out salt pork keeps the bean flavor cleaner and makes the pot usable for a vegetarian at the table. Central Texas markets served beans because they were the inexpensive pot next to expensive beef.

How it is made

Boil dried pinto beans with onion and garlic until they are completely tender. No meat.

Authentic touch. Boil the beans hard until they are fully soft before you decide they need more salt. Acid too early keeps them gritty.

How it is used

In a cup beside brisket or beef ribs, with some of the bean broth.

Field notes

  • Salt pork is a common addition to Texas pinto beans. This pot leaves it out.
  • The beans are boiled until they are completely tender, which is the doneness that matters.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound dried pinto beans, picked over and rinsed
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 3 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 8 cups water

Steps

  1. Rinse the beans and cover them with the water in a heavy pot. Add onion and garlic. Do not add salt pork or bacon.
  2. Bring them to a full boil, 212°F (100°C), and boil for several minutes, then simmer.
  3. Cook until the beans are completely tender, which often takes 1 1/2 to 2 1/2 hours from dried beans. Add water if they go dry. Do not hold raw dried beans warm without a real boil.
  4. Salt them near the end, once they are soft. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Boil dried beans until they are completely tender. Kidney beans must be boiled hard, not held warm from raw. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A jalapeño in the pot adds heat. It is not required for the meat-market bean.

Eastern North Carolina Vinegar Slaw

Crisp cabbage in a sweet-sharp vinegar dressing, with no mayonnaise.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Eastern North Carolina coleslaw is dressed with vinegar, sugar, and pepper, in the same spirit as the hog sauce, and it does not use mayonnaise. It sits beside chopped pork. It is not Lexington red slaw, which is tinted with dip, and it is not a creamy picnic slaw.

Cultural background

A vinegar slaw with no egg and no dairy. A side for eastern Carolina barbecue. The cabbage stays crisp.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and a little sugar match the sharp pork sauce so the plate tastes like one idea. Cabbage adds crunch that chopped meat does not have. Leaving out mayonnaise keeps the slaw from going dull in a warm picnic yard as fast as a creamy slaw would, though it still needs the refrigerator if it sits.

How it is made

Dress shredded cabbage with vinegar, sugar, salt, and pepper. No mayonnaise.

Authentic touch. Salt the cabbage, wait, and squeeze out a little water before you dress it. Wet cabbage makes a watery slaw.

How it is used

Cold, beside chopped hog. It can go on a sandwich if the sandwich can take the vinegar.

Field notes

  • Eastern North Carolina slaw in this style uses vinegar, not mayonnaise.
  • It is a different slaw from Lexington red slaw, which is dressed with tomato dip.

Ingredients

  • 1 small green cabbage, shredded
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon crushed red pepper

Steps

  1. Toss the cabbage with the salt and let it stand 15 minutes, then squeeze out some of the water.
  2. Stir vinegar, sugar, black pepper, and red pepper. There is no mayonnaise.
  3. Dress the cabbage and refrigerate it. Hold it cold, below 40°F (4°C). This is not a canning recipe.
  4. Serve beside pork that has already been cooked to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). The slaw itself is raw cabbage.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of the eastern pepper sauce can replace part of the vinegar if you want it hotter.

Farofa

Toasted cassava flour, buttery and crumbly, with a little onion.

10 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Farofa is cassava flour toasted in fat until it becomes a savory crumb for meat, beans, and rice. This recipe uses commercial toasted manioc flour, the kind sold as farinha de mandioca torrada. It does not start from raw cassava, and it does not process bitter cassava at home. Raw bitter cassava is not part of this recipe. Bacon is a common addition. This version uses butter so the written side stays vegetarian.

Cultural background

Read the package and use flour that is already safe to cook. Do not grate or soak raw cassava for this page. Butter makes it dairy. It is not vegan. There is no wheat if the flour is only manioc.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted manioc flour turns nutty in hot butter, and onion softens into the crumb. Beside grilled beef it soaks up juice without becoming a porridge. Cassava is an American root that Brazilian cooks made into this everyday side, and the flavor here is the toasting, not a meat stock.

How it is made

Cook onion in butter, then toast commercial manioc flour until it smells nutty and looks dry.

Authentic touch. Stir constantly once the flour goes in. It burns in spots if it sits, and burned farofa tastes bitter.

How it is used

Sprinkle it over rice, beans, or grilled meat. It is a side, not a dough.

Field notes

  • This recipe starts with commercial toasted manioc flour and does not process raw cassava.
  • Farofa is a toasted crumb. Raw bitter cassava is not an ingredient.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups commercial toasted manioc flour (farinha de mandioca torrada), not raw cassava
  • 3 tablespoons butter
  • 1 small onion, finely chopped
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Read the package. Use commercial toasted manioc flour only. Do not start from raw or bitter cassava.
  2. Melt the butter in a skillet and cook the onion until it is soft. There is no meat in this pan.
  3. Add the flour and salt. Stir over medium heat until the crumb smells toasted and looks dry, about 8 minutes.
  4. Taste for salt. Serve warm. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The butter means this version is not vegan.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chopped cooked egg is a common add-in and would add egg. It is not in this version.
  • Oil instead of butter would make it dairy-free. This one uses butter.

Pairings and uses

  • rice
  • beans
  • grilled beef
  • Vegetarian

Hush Puppies

Crisp fried cornmeal, salty, with a little onion.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Hush puppies are fried cornmeal dough served at fish fries and at some barbecue pits. They are fried, not smoked. This batter uses cornmeal and a spoon of wheat flour, plus an egg, and it is fried in oil that is not peanut oil. The name has folk explanations this page does not treat as documented history.

Cultural background

A fried side, not a pit smoke. Wheat and egg are in the batter. There is no dairy and no peanut oil, so the written recipe is dairy-free and nut-free, and not gluten-free.

Why it tastes this way

Cornmeal fries into a crust while the center stays soft, which is the contrast people want next to smoky fish or pork. Onion in the batter is the savory note. Frying in a skillet is a different craft from a smoker, which is why these belong beside the pit and not in it.

How it is made

Mix a cornmeal batter and fry spoonfuls in hot oil until the centers are cooked.

Authentic touch. Fry in small spoonfuls and do not crowd the pan. A crowded pot of oil drops in temperature and greases the dough instead of crusting it.

How it is used

Hot, beside fried fish or barbecue. They are a bread, not a meat.

Field notes

  • Hush puppies are fried cornmeal dough, which is why they show up at fish fries.
  • They are fried in a skillet of oil, not smoked over the pit.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup cornmeal
  • 2 tablespoons wheat flour
  • 1 egg
  • 3/4 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons finely chopped onion
  • Neutral oil for frying, such as corn or canola, not peanut oil

Steps

  1. Stir cornmeal, wheat flour, egg, water, salt, and onion into a thick batter. The flour is wheat, so this is not gluten-free.
  2. Heat 2 inches of oil to 350°F (177°C) in a deep skillet. Do not use peanut oil.
  3. Fry spoonfuls, turning once, until they are deep golden and the center is cooked and hot, about 2 to 3 minutes. Do not crowd the pan.
  4. Drain them. They are fried, not smoked. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A batter of cornmeal only, with no wheat flour, could be gluten-free if every other ingredient agrees. This one uses a spoon of wheat flour.

Pairings and uses

  • Sides
  • fried fish
  • chopped pork

Lexington Red Slaw

Cabbage in a red vinegar-ketchup dressing, tangy and a little sweet.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Lexington red slaw, sometimes called barbecue slaw, is cabbage dressed with the same style of dip used on Piedmont pork shoulder: vinegar with a little ketchup. It is not a mayonnaise slaw and not the paler vinegar slaw of eastern Carolina. The red color is the dip, not food dye.

Cultural background

Cabbage dressed with Lexington-style dip. No mayonnaise. A side for pork shoulder, and often a sandwich topping.

Why it tastes this way

The dip's vinegar and ketchup soak the cabbage so the slaw tastes like the pork sauce beside it. A little sugar softens the acid. Because there is no egg or cream, the texture stays crunchy rather than coated.

How it is made

Dress shredded cabbage with vinegar, a little ketchup, sugar, and pepper.

Authentic touch. Use a thin dip. If you stir in enough ketchup to make a paste, the slaw stops being red slaw and becomes a tomato salad.

How it is used

Cold, on or beside chopped Lexington shoulder.

Field notes

  • Lexington red slaw is dressed with the dip, which is why it is red, and it does not use mayonnaise.
  • It is a different slaw from the tomato-free vinegar slaw of eastern North Carolina.

Ingredients

  • 1 small green cabbage, finely chopped
  • 1/3 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons ketchup
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Chop the cabbage fine and toss it with the salt. Let it stand 10 minutes and drain off excess water.
  2. Stir vinegar, ketchup, sugar, and pepper into a thin red dip. No mayonnaise.
  3. Mix the dip into the cabbage and refrigerate it below 40°F (4°C). This is not a canning recipe.
  4. Serve with pork shoulder cooked to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). The slaw is a vegetable side.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Finely chopped cabbage, rather than long shreds, is how many Lexington trays make it.

Mustard Potato Salad

Potatoes in a sharp mustard dressing with egg.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Mustard potato salad is a Southern picnic and pit side, sharper than a sweet mayonnaise-only salad. This one uses boiled potatoes, hard-cooked eggs, mayonnaise, and yellow mustard. Egg and mustard are both in it. It is a cold side, not a smoke.

Cultural background

A cold potato salad with egg and mustard. Vegetarian, and dairy-free if the mayonnaise is the egg-and-oil kind. Not vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard and vinegar cut boiled potato starch, and egg yolk from the mayonnaise and the chopped eggs makes the dressing rich without cream. Beside fatty barbecue, the sharpness is the reason it lasts on the plate. It is picnic food, held cold, not a hot grill dish.

How it is made

Boil potatoes and eggs, then fold them into mayonnaise and mustard.

Authentic touch. Dress the potatoes while they are still slightly warm so they take the seasoning, then chill the bowl.

How it is used

Cold, beside pork, chicken, or ribs.

Field notes

  • This potato salad uses both mustard and egg, so those allergens are part of the side.
  • It is dressed and chilled. It is not smoked.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds potatoes, peeled and cubed
  • 3 eggs
  • 1/2 cup mayonnaise made from egg, oil, and vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons yellow mustard
  • 1 rib celery, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon apple cider vinegar

Steps

  1. Cover the potatoes with water, bring the pot to a boil at 212°F (100°C), and cook until the potatoes are tender. In a second pot, boil the eggs until the yolks are firm, about 10 minutes, then cool them.
  2. Drain the potatoes. Chop the eggs.

Steps

  1. Put pork, onions, mustard, vinegar, salt, pepper, and water in a heavy pot.
  2. Simmer, covered, until the pork reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and then continue until it is soft enough to chop, about 195°F (90°C) in the chunks. Whole pork is safe at 145°F with a 3-minute rest; hash pork is cooked longer.
  3. Chop the pork in the pot and simmer uncovered until the stew is thick. The onions should have collapsed.
  4. Serve over rice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some pots add potato. This one stays with pork and onion.

Southern Collard Greens

Soft, savory collards in a smoky pot liquor.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Collard greens simmered until soft, with the broth saved, are a foundation of Black Southern cooking. A smoked turkey wing is a seasoning meat in many of those pots. The liquid left in the pot is pot liquor, and it is meant to be eaten, not thrown out. This is a greens pot, not a myth about who invented barbecue.

Cultural background

Collards cooked fully, with a smoked turkey wing and the pot liquor kept. The turkey means the pot is not vegetarian. The greens, not a barbecue legend, are the subject.

Why it tastes this way

Long cooking softens collard leaves that are too tough for a quick saute, and the turkey wing seasons the broth with smoke. Vinegar at the end brightens the richness. Pot liquor carries that flavor, which is why it is served with the greens rather than drained off like a blanching water.

How it is made

Simmer cleaned collards with onion and a smoked turkey wing until the leaves are fully tender and the wing is heated through.

Authentic touch. Save the pot liquor. A ladle of it over the greens, or with cornbread for dipping, is the point of the pot.

How it is used

With a cup of pot liquor. Cornbread is a usual companion and is a separate recipe.

Field notes

  • Pot liquor is the broth in the collard pot, and it is meant to be eaten.
  • A smoked turkey wing is a common seasoning meat in Black Southern collard pots.

Ingredients

  • 2 large bunches collard greens, stems stripped, leaves chopped and washed
  • 1 smoked turkey wing
  • 1 onion, chopped
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 6 cups water
  • 1 tablespoon apple cider vinegar

Steps

  1. Wash the collards in several changes of water so the grit is gone. Put them in a pot with the turkey wing, onion, garlic, salt, pepper, and water.
  2. Simmer, covered, until the leaves are completely tender, not chewy, usually about 1 to 1 1/2 hours.
  3. Heat the smoked turkey wing through in the simmer to 165°F (74°C). It is already a cooked product; the long pot finishes the greens and heats the wing.
  4. Stir in the vinegar. Serve the greens with pot liquor. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a story about the origin of barbecue.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A piece of smoked pork can season the pot instead of turkey. It would still be a meat dish, cooked through.

Syracuse Salt Potatoes

Small potatoes with a salty crust on the skin, served with butter.

5 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Syracuse salt potatoes are small potatoes boiled in water salty enough to leave a white crust on the skins. The city was built on salt works, and the potatoes are associated with that history. They are the usual companion to Cornell chicken, and they are boiled, not grilled. Butter at the table makes this a dairy dish.

Cultural background

A boiled potato side for Cornell chicken. Not a grill recipe. The salt is in the water, and the butter is dairy. They are vegetarian and not vegan.

Why it tastes this way

Very salty water seasons the potato through the skin and dries to a light crust, so each bite is saltier than a normally boiled potato. Butter melts into that crust. They share a plate with Cornell's vinegar chicken because upstate cookouts put the sharp bird next to the salty potato, not because the potatoes ever saw the coals.

How it is made

Boil small potatoes in very salty water until tender, then serve them with butter.

Authentic touch. Use small potatoes and enough salt that the water tastes like brine. A pinch of salt makes ordinary boiled potatoes, not salt potatoes.

How it is used

Hot, skins on, with melted butter, beside Cornell chicken. They are not grilled.

Field notes

  • Syracuse salt potatoes are boiled in water salty enough to leave a crust on the skins.
  • They are the companion to Cornell chicken, and they are not a grill dish.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds small potatoes, skins on and scrubbed
  • 1 cup kosher salt
  • 2 quarts water
  • 4 tablespoons butter, melted

Steps

  1. Put the potatoes, salt, and water in a pot. The water should taste strongly salty. This is a boil, not a grill, and it is the companion to Cornell chicken.
  2. Bring the pot to a boil at 212°F (100°C).
  3. Cook until the potatoes are tender all the way through when pierced. Drain them. A salty film on the skins is expected.
  4. Serve hot with melted butter. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Without the butter they would be dairy-free. This recipe serves them with butter.
