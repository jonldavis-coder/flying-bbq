Rubs, Mops & Sauces

The dry coats, thin mops, and table sauces that travel with the fire.

A rub is salt and spice on the surface. A mop is a thin liquid during the cook. A sauce may be the whole point, as in eastern North Carolina, or a bowl on the side, as in many Central Texas markets.

This booklet is every passport entry whose method is a sauce or a rub. Print it when you want the seasonings without the whole animal.

Alabama White Sauce

Cool, peppery mayonnaise loosened with vinegar.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 10 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Alabama white sauce comes from Big Bob Gibson's barbecue in Decatur in the 1920s, a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce for smoked chicken. It is a documented restaurant sauce, not an ancient folk recipe. Mayonnaise means egg. The sauce is not vegan, and it should not be boiled.

Cultural background

A cold mayonnaise sauce for smoked chicken. Egg is in it. It is not a tomato sauce and not a vinegar-only mop.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries black pepper and cayenne onto smoked chicken skin, and vinegar keeps the fat from tasting flat. Sugar, just a little, takes the edge off the acid. Because the base is already an emulsion, heat would break it, so the sauce stays cold against hot chicken.

How it is made

Whisk mayonnaise, vinegar, pepper, and a little sugar. Serve it cold.

Authentic touch. Whisk it and refrigerate it. Do not simmer mayonnaise. Use a mayonnaise made from egg and oil, and keep it out of the sun.

How it is used

On or beside smoked chicken. It is also used on turkey by some cooks. This batch is the sauce alone.

Field notes

  • Big Bob Gibson in Decatur put this mayonnaise sauce on smoked chicken in the 1920s.
  • The egg in the mayonnaise means the sauce is not vegan.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup mayonnaise made from egg, oil, and vinegar
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Whisk mayonnaise, vinegar, pepper, cayenne, salt, and sugar until smooth.
  2. Do not heat the sauce. Boiling will break the mayonnaise.
  3. Refrigerate it immediately and use it within a few days. Do not leave it at room temperature more than 2 hours.
  4. Serve it with chicken that has been cooked to 165°F (74°C). The sauce itself is egg and vinegar, not a meat cook.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of prepared horseradish makes it sharper. Leave it out if you want the Decatur baseline.

Argentine Chimichurri

Parsley, oregano, garlic, vinegar, oil, and chile, chopped and sharp.

15 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Chimichurri is the green sauce of the Argentine asado: parsley, oregano, garlic, vinegar, oil, and chile. Stories about the name are disputed, so this page does not pick one. It is a table sauce, stirred and rested, not a cooked glaze. It belongs beside grilled beef, and it is not the Dominican wasakaka or the Nicaraguan garlic spoon.

Cultural background

There is no meat, egg, dairy, or wheat in the bowl. It is vegetarian and vegan. Garlic in oil should be refrigerated and used within a few days. Do not leave it at room temperature.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and raw garlic cut the fat of grilled beef, and parsley and oregano keep the sauce green and herbal. Dried chile adds a small heat without making a pepper paste. On a Río de la Plata table the sauce is the contrast to salted meat and wood coals.

How it is made

Chop parsley, oregano, garlic, and chile, then stir them with vinegar, oil, and salt.

Authentic touch. Chop the parsley by hand if you can. A blender makes a bitter, uniform puree that is a different sauce.

How it is used

Spoon it over sliced grilled beef, or serve it in a bowl so people take what they want.

Field notes

  • The sauce is parsley, oregano, garlic, vinegar, oil, and chile.
  • Stories about the name are disputed, so this page does not choose one.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup finely chopped fresh parsley
  • 1 tablespoon dried oregano
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/4 cup red wine vinegar
  • 1/2 cup olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon dried chile flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Chop the parsley and mince the garlic. There is no meat in this sauce.
  2. Stir in oregano, vinegar, oil, chile flakes, and salt.
  3. Taste for salt. Let it sit 15 minutes in the refrigerator so the garlic softens into the vinegar.
  4. Serve cold or cool. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A red chimichurri adds tomato and more chile. This one stays green.
  • Red wine vinegar and white vinegar are both used. This recipe uses red wine vinegar.

Argentine Salsa Criolla

Raw onion, tomato, and pepper in vinegar and oil.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Argentine salsa criolla is an onion, tomato, and pepper relish for the asado, dressed with vinegar and oil. It is not Peruvian salsa criolla, which is built more on onion and lime, and it is not chimichurri, which is a herb sauce. The vegetables stay raw. It is a salad-sauce, eaten the day it is made.

Cultural background

There is no meat and no dairy. It is vegetarian and vegan. Salt the onion and let it sit so it softens slightly. Keep it cold. It is not a preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Raw onion and vinegar cut grilled fat the way a pickle does, and tomato and pepper add water and sweetness. On a Córdoba or Buenos Aires table it sits next to chimichurri so people can choose herbal or fresh. The flavor depends on the onion being fine and the salt being enough.

How it is made

Chop onion, tomato, and pepper and dress them with vinegar, oil, and salt.

Authentic touch. Slice the onion very thin and rinse it if you want less bite, then dry it before it meets the oil.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled meat or eat it as a small salad beside the fire.

Field notes

  • Argentine salsa criolla is an onion, tomato, and pepper relish.
  • It is not Peruvian salsa criolla, which is built differently.

Ingredients

  • 1 large white onion, sliced very thin
  • 2 tomatoes, seeded and chopped
  • 1 bell pepper, chopped
  • 3 tablespoons red wine vinegar
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Toss the onion with the salt and let it stand 10 minutes. There is no meat in this relish.
  2. Add the tomato, bell pepper, vinegar, and oil.
  3. Stir and taste. Add salt only if the onion needs it.
  4. Serve the same day, cold. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canned preserve.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little parsley is common. This version leaves the herbs to the chimichurri.
  • Red or green bell pepper both work. Keep the pieces small.

Eastern North Carolina Vinegar Sauce

Sharp vinegar with red pepper, black pepper, and salt, and no tomato.

10 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 1/2 cups

History

Eastern North Carolina sauce is vinegar, pepper, and salt. Tomato is not part of it, which is how a cook tells it from Lexington dip. It dresses chopped whole hog, or the home shoulder-and-belly version of that plate. No single inventor owns the formula.

Cultural background

A vinegar-pepper sauce with no tomato. It is the seasoning for eastern Carolina pork, not a sweet glaze and not a ketchup dip.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar cuts hog fat, and cayenne and black pepper supply the heat that a tomato sauce would sweeten away. In a humid climate the sharpness stays bright instead of cloying. The sauce is the barbecue, as much as the smoke is.

How it is made

Stir vinegar, water, salt, and pepper. Warm it only enough to dissolve the salt.

Authentic touch. Do not add ketchup. If the sauce turns red from tomato, you have left eastern Carolina.

How it is used

On chopped pork, and in a bottle on the table so people can add more.

Field notes

  • Eastern North Carolina sauce has vinegar and pepper and no tomato.
  • Lexington dip is the neighbor that adds a little ketchup. This one does not.

Ingredients

  • 2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir vinegar, water, salt, red pepper, and black pepper in a saucepan. There is no tomato in this sauce.
  2. Warm over low heat about 5 minutes, just until the salt dissolves. It does not need to boil.
  3. Cool it. This is a sauce, not a meat cook and not a canning recipe. Keep it refrigerated and use it within a week.
  4. Spoon it over chopped pork that has already been cooked to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). The sauce itself has no meat temperature.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More crushed red pepper makes it hotter. The base stays vinegar.

  • chopped pork
  • vinegar slaw
  • white bread

English Mint Sauce

Sharp vinegar, fresh mint, and a little sugar, meant to cut lamb fat.

15 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Mint sauce is the English table sauce of vinegar, mint, and sugar served with lamb. It belongs next to a roast, not on a grill of its own. Garden mint and malt or white vinegar are the usual pantry pieces. The sauce is mixed and left to stand so the mint softens in the acid. It is not a jelly and not a pesto.

Cultural background

This page is only the sauce. The ingredients are mint, vinegar, and sugar. It is not a barbecue rub, and it is not cooked over a fire.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat is rich, and vinegar cuts it. Mint brings a cool, green note that the British kitchen has long put with spring lamb. A pinch of sugar keeps the vinegar from tasting only harsh. The combination is about balance, not sweetness.

How it is made

Stir chopped mint into vinegar with sugar and a pinch of salt. This is a bowl sauce, not a grill.

Authentic touch. Chop the mint fine and stir it into warm vinegar so the leaves soften. Let it stand before you serve it.

How it is used

Cold or cool, in a jug beside roast or grilled lamb. A spoonful on the chop is enough.

Field notes

  • English mint sauce is vinegar, mint, and sugar, and it is served with lamb.
  • The sauce is not a grill and not a mint jelly.

Ingredients

  • 1 packed cup fresh mint leaves
  • 4 tablespoons malt or white wine vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons boiling water

Steps

  1. Chop the mint finely. Stir the sugar and salt into the boiling water until they dissolve.
  2. Add the vinegar and the mint. This is a sauce, not a grill, so there is no animal-protein thermometer target.
  3. Let the sauce stand at least 20 minutes so the mint softens in the acid.
  4. Taste and add a few drops more vinegar or a pinch more sugar if you want it sharper or rounder. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little hot water to loosen a very sharp mix.
  • White wine vinegar in place of malt vinegar.

Filipino Lechon Sarsa

A savory brown sauce of chicken liver, vinegar, garlic, and bread crumbs.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Lechon sarsa is the liver sauce served with Filipino roast pig, including Cebu lechon. Liver is simmered with vinegar, garlic, sugar, and bread crumbs, then blended. The liver has to reach 165°F (74°C) because this recipe uses chicken liver, which is poultry offal. Bread crumbs contain wheat; it is a sauce for the pork, not the pork itself, and it is cooked and refrigerated, not canned.

Cultural background

Feast cooks in the Philippines make a pot of sarsa while the pig or the shoulder roasts. There is chicken liver and wheat. There is no dairy and no peanut. Cook the liver through. Do not serve it pink.

Why it tastes this way

Liver gives the sauce a deep savory taste, and vinegar keeps it from feeling heavy on fatty pork. Sugar rounds the vinegar, and bread crumbs thicken the pot so it sits on the meat. Garlic is the aroma. Without the vinegar it would taste muddy. The acid is why it belongs with lechon.

How it is made

Cook chicken liver to 165°F (74°C) with garlic and vinegar, then blend it with bread crumbs into a sauce.

Authentic touch. Brown the liver first so the sauce tastes roasted, then add the vinegar. Boiling raw liver in vinegar from the start tastes harsh.

How it is used

Spoon it over chopped roast pork, or serve it in a bowl beside the meat.

Field notes

  • Lechon sarsa is a liver sauce for Filipino roast pork.
  • Chicken liver in this pot is cooked to 165°F (74°C).

Ingredients

  • 1/2 pound chicken livers, trimmed
  • 3 tablespoons vinegar
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 cup wheat bread crumbs
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 cup water

Steps

  1. Trim the livers. They are still raw. Heat the oil and cook the onion and garlic for a minute. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  2. Add the livers and cook them until the center of the thickest piece reaches 165°F (74°C). Do not serve the liver pink. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  3. Add vinegar, sugar, salt, bread crumbs, and water. Simmer 5 minutes. Blend until mostly smooth, and check that the sauce is hot and the liver was already at 165°F (74°C). Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  4. Serve warm with pork. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).

Cook beef offal to at least 145°F (63°C) with a rest. Cook poultry offal, including chicken hearts and livers, to 165°F (74°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork liver is used in some pots. This page uses chicken liver so the temperature is the poultry-offal one.
  • A gluten-free crumb would change the wheat allergen. These are wheat bread crumbs.

Kansas City Barbecue Sauce

Thick, sweet, and smoky-tasting tomato sauce with molasses.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 cups

History

Kansas City's table sauce is thick, tomato-sweet, and darkened with molasses, the glaze associated with the city's ribs and burnt ends. Henry Perry is the early barbecue seller local history credits; the thick sauce is the later restaurant style, and this page does not claim he bottled this exact formula. Molasses is not honey. Ordinary Worcestershire in the pot contains anchovy and usually wheat.

Cultural background

A thick molasses-tomato sauce. Not gluten-free, because of Worcestershire, and not vegetarian, because of the anchovy in that ingredient.

Why it tastes this way

Molasses adds a bitter-sweet depth that plain sugar does not, and tomato and vinegar keep it from being only sweet. A simmer thickens it so it clings to ribs at the end of a smoke. Kansas City's railroad stockyards and later restaurants made this heavy sauce the city's signal, against the thinner dips of the Carolinas.

How it is made

Simmer ketchup, molasses, vinegar, and Worcestershire into a thick sauce.

Authentic touch. Simmer until it coats a spoon, then stop. Cooked too long, the sugar turns harsh. Put it on meat late in the cook.

How it is used

On ribs, burnt ends, or in a cup beside sliced meat. It is a finishing sauce, not a mop for the whole smoke.

Field notes

  • This Kansas City sauce uses molasses, not honey.
  • Ordinary Worcestershire sauce contains anchovy and usually wheat, so the sauce is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir ketchup, molasses, brown sugar, vinegar, Worcestershire, paprika, and pepper in a saucepan. Molasses is not honey. Ordinary Worcestershire contains fish and usually wheat.
  2. Simmer about 20 minutes, stirring, until the sauce is thick enough to coat a spoon.
  3. Cool and refrigerate. Use within a week. This is not a canning recipe.
  4. Brush it on ribs only near the end of cooking, after the meat is approaching about 195°F (90°C), so the sugar does not burn for hours.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A gluten-free Worcestershire, if the label truly has no wheat, would change only that allergen. This recipe uses the ordinary kind.

Pairings and uses

  • spareribs
  • burnt ends
  • fries

Kentucky Black Dip

Dark, peppery, and sharp from Worcestershire and vinegar.

5 min prep · 5 min cook · Serves 10 · about 1 1/2 cups

History

Black dip is the dark sauce Owensboro serves with mutton barbecue. Worcestershire and black pepper dominate it, which is why it looks nearly black beside a tomato sauce. It belongs to the parish-picnic mutton table, not to a pork shoulder with Carolina vinegar. Ordinary Worcestershire contains anchovy and usually wheat.

Cultural background

A Worcestershire-heavy dip for mutton or lamb. Not a sweet glaze. Not vegetarian, because of the anchovy in Worcestershire.

Why it tastes this way

Mutton is stronger than young lamb or pork, and a peppery, salty dip cuts that flavor instead of covering it with sugar. Vinegar thins the Worcestershire so it works as a dip rather than as a glaze. Western Kentucky picnic pits made this the companion to sheep, which is why the sauce is not a generic steak sauce.

How it is made

Stir Worcestershire, vinegar, water, and black pepper and warm the dip briefly.

Authentic touch. Keep it thin enough to spoon, not boil it into a syrup. The darkness should come from Worcestershire, not from burnt sugar.

How it is used

Beside chopped or sliced mutton, and on the meat if people want it.

Field notes

  • Black dip is the Worcestershire sauce Owensboro serves with mutton.
  • Ordinary Worcestershire contains anchovy and usually wheat, so this dip is not gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup Worcestershire sauce
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground allspice

Steps

  1. Stir Worcestershire, water, vinegar, pepper, and allspice. Ordinary Worcestershire contains fish and usually wheat.
  2. Warm the dip 5 minutes. Do not reduce it to a glaze.
  3. Cool and refrigerate. Use within a week. This is not a canning recipe.
  4. Serve with lamb or mutton that has reached at least 145°F (63°C) and rested, or about 195°F (90°C) if you are chopping a shoulder.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A pinch of allspice is used by some picnic cooks. This written dip relies on pepper.

Pairings and uses

Korean Ssamjang

Salty, hot, and savory, from soybean paste, chili paste, garlic, and sesame.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Ssamjang is the Korean paste set on the table for ssam, the lettuce wraps that hold grilled pork or beef. It is stirred from doenjang, a fermented soybean paste, and gochujang, a fermented chili paste, with garlic, sesame oil, and a little onion. Many commercial gochujang brands contain wheat. The paste is not cooked; it is a condiment for the grill, not the meat itself.

Cultural background

Korean homes mix ssamjang by the spoon for a barbecue meal. There is soy, wheat, and sesame. There is no meat, dairy, egg, or honey in this bowl, so it is vegetarian. Keep it cold. It is not a canned preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Doenjang is salty and deeply savory, and gochujang adds chili and a little sweetness. Sesame oil rounds the ferments, and raw garlic keeps the paste sharp against grilled fat. On a Korean table it seasons a plain slice of belly or neck after the fire, which is why those meats can be cooked with only salt.

How it is made

Stir doenjang, gochujang, garlic, onion, and sesame oil into a thick paste.

Authentic touch. Stir it thick enough to sit on a lettuce leaf. A watery mix runs off the meat and seasons the table instead.

How it is used

Put a little on a lettuce leaf with grilled meat and rice.

Field notes

  • Ssamjang is the wrap paste for Korean grilled meat, made from doenjang and gochujang.
  • Many gochujang brands contain wheat, so this paste is not assumed gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup doenjang (fermented soybean paste)
  • 2 tablespoons gochujang (chili paste; many brands contain wheat)
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Stir the doenjang, gochujang, garlic, onion, sesame oil, sesame seeds, and water into a thick paste. There is no meat in the bowl.
  2. Taste for salt. Doenjang is already salty, so do not add salt first.
  3. Cover it and refrigerate it until the meat is ready. Do not leave garlic sitting in oil at room temperature.
  4. Serve cold or cool with lettuce wraps. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of honey is common and would add honey and remove the vegan label. This paste uses no honey.
  • More gochujang makes it hotter. This balance is medium.

Lexington Dip

Vinegar dip blushed with ketchup, still thin and peppery.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 12 · about 2 1/2 cups

History

Lexington dip is the Piedmont sauce: vinegar with a little ketchup or tomato, plus pepper and salt. It is different from eastern North Carolina sauce, which has no tomato. The dip seasons pork shoulder and also dresses red slaw. This page does not name an inventor.

Cultural background

A thin vinegar-and-ketchup dip for Lexington shoulder. It is not the tomato-free eastern sauce and not a thick city glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Most of the flavor is still vinegar, so the pork stays sharp, and a small amount of ketchup adds color and a little sweetness. Pepper keeps it from tasting like a mild tomato soup. The dip is thin because it is meant to soak chopped shoulder, not to coat it like paint.

How it is made

Stir vinegar, ketchup, water, salt, and pepper. Do not boil it down into a thick sauce.

Authentic touch. Stop at about one part ketchup to three parts vinegar. More ketchup and you have left the dip.

How it is used

On chopped pork shoulder and in red slaw. Put a bottle on the table.

Field notes

  • Lexington dip contains a little ketchup or tomato. Eastern vinegar sauce does not.
  • The same dip is used to dress Lexington red slaw.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir vinegar, ketchup, water, salt, and both peppers until the ketchup is dissolved and the dip is thin.

Paprika, salt, black pepper, and a little brown sugar.

History

A Memphis dry rub is the seasoning on ribs that are served without sauce on the meat. Paprika, salt, pepper, and sugar are the core, and the sugar helps the bark color. Cultural background

A dry spice mix for Memphis ribs. No sauce is part of this rub. It is a pantry mix, not a cooked condiment.

Why it tastes this way

Paprika and a little sugar brown in hickory smoke and make the crust people expect on a dry rib. Salt seasons the pork underneath. Leaving out tomato and molasses is the decision that keeps a dry rib dry.

How it is made

Stir paprika, salt, pepper, sugar, and garlic powder. Store the mix dry.

Authentic touch. Rub the meat and then leave it alone. Do not oil it so heavily that the spices steam instead of crusting.

How it is used

Pressed onto pork ribs or shoulder before a smoke. Sauce, if any, stays off the dry-rib plate.

Field notes

  • A Memphis dry rub uses paprika, salt, pepper, and sugar, and the rib under it is served without sauce.

  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper

  • 2 teaspoons garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoons onion powder

Steps

  2. Store the rub in a dry jar. It is not cooked, and it is not a canning recipe.
  3. Press it onto pork before smoking. Do not add sauce to a dry rib.

Molho à Campanha

A fresh chop of tomato, onion, and pepper in vinegar and oil.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

Molho à campanha is the vinaigrette relish of a Brazilian churrasco, named for the southern grasslands, the campanha. It is chopped tomato, onion, and pepper with vinegar and oil. It is not chimichurri. The herbs, when they appear, are a handful, and the body of the sauce is the vegetables. It is eaten the day it is made.

Cultural background

There is no meat in the bowl. It is vegetarian and vegan. The campanha name points at Rio Grande do Sul, and the same relish is now on plates far from that border. Keep it cold.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and raw onion brighten fatty grilled beef, and the tomato makes the sauce wetter than a herb paste. On a southern Brazilian table it sits next to farofa, so you get something fresh and something toasted. The taste is the chop, not a cooked tomato sauce.

How it is made

Chop tomato, onion, and pepper and dress them with vinegar, oil, and salt.

Authentic touch. Cut the pieces small and even. Large chunks of onion fall off the meat and never season it.

How it is used

Spoon it over picanha, linguiça, or rice. Use it the same day.

Field notes

  • Molho à campanha is the fresh vinaigrette relish of a Brazilian churrasco.
  • It is not chimichurri. The body is chopped tomato, onion, and pepper.

Ingredients

  • 2 tomatoes, seeded and finely chopped
  • 1 onion, finely chopped
  • 1 green bell pepper, finely chopped
  • 1/4 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1/4 cup white vinegar
  • 1/4 cup neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Chop the vegetables small. There is no meat in this relish.
  2. Stir in cilantro, vinegar, oil, and salt.
  3. Let it stand 10 minutes in the refrigerator, then taste for salt.
  4. Serve cold the same day. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canned preserve.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Cilantro or parsley can be added. This bowl uses cilantro.
  • A minced hot pepper makes it hotter. The written amount has none.

Nam Jim Jaew

Hot, sour, and salty, with toasted rice, chili, lime, and fish sauce.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Nam jim jaew is the Isan dipping sauce served with grilled chicken and grilled pork. Toasted sticky-rice powder thickens it, dried chili provides the heat, and lime and fish sauce make it sour and salty. Shallot is usual. It is a table sauce, not a cooked glaze; fish sauce means it contains fish, so it is not a vegetarian condiment even though there is no piece of meat in the bowl.

Cultural background

Isan cooks set this bowl beside gai yang and pork. The rice is toasted, not boiled into porridge. There is no dairy and no wheat if you toast rice, not barley. Keep it cold, and do not treat it as a preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted rice smells nutty and gives the sauce body so it clings to grilled meat. Lime cuts fat, dried chili brings heat, and fish sauce is the salt of the Mekong table. Shallot adds a sharp fresh bite. Without the toasted rice, the same liquids would be a thinner nam jim.

How it is made

Toast rice and grind it, then stir it with chili, lime, fish sauce, and shallot.

Authentic touch. Toast the rice until it smells popped, then grind it. Raw rice flour will taste raw and dusty.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken or pork, or leave it in a bowl for dipping.

Field notes

  • Toasted rice powder is what makes jaew different from a thin chili fish sauce.
  • Fish sauce is in the bowl, so the sauce is not vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons uncooked sticky rice or jasmine rice
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 tablespoon crushed dried hot chili
  • 1 shallot, thinly sliced
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Toast the rice in a dry pan until it is golden and smells nutty. Grind it to a coarse powder. There is no meat to cook in this sauce.
  2. Stir the rice powder with fish sauce, lime juice, chili, shallot, and sugar.
  3. Taste for salt and sour. Add cilantro.
  4. Serve the same day. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe. Do not leave it at room temperature.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More dried chili makes it extra-hot. This cup is hot.
  • A little extra lime is the right fix if the fish sauce is very salty.

Santa Maria Salsa

Fresh tomato, onion, chile, and cilantro, chunky and bright.

20 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 3 cups

History

Santa Maria tri-tip is served with a fresh tomato salsa, not with a sweet barbecue sauce. The salsa is chopped tomato, onion, chile, and cilantro, and it belongs to the central-coast beef plate. It is not a cooked Texas mop and not a molasses glaze. This version is gluten-free, dairy-free, and nut-free.

Cultural background

A fresh salsa for tri-tip. Uncooked. Not a bottle of sweet barbecue sauce. Western US table habit beside red-oak beef.

Why it tastes this way

Cold raw tomato and onion cut the richness of grilled beef the way a pickle does in other regions, and cilantro and green chile add a fresh top note. Because the salsa is not cooked, it stays bright against smoke. The dry central coast grows the beef and the tomatoes that end up on the same plate.

How it is made

Chop tomatoes, onion, jalapeño, and cilantro and season them with salt and lime.

Authentic touch. Salt it and let it sit 15 minutes so the tomato makes a little juice. Do not cook it.

How it is used

Cold, beside sliced tri-tip. It can also go on beans. It is not brushed on the roast while it cooks.

Field notes

  • Santa Maria salsa is a fresh tomato relish for tri-tip, not a sweet cooked barbecue sauce.
  • This batch is uncooked and contains no gluten, dairy, or nuts.

Ingredients

  • 4 ripe tomatoes, chopped
  • 1/2 onion, finely chopped
  • 1 jalapeño, seeded and minced
  • 1/4 cup chopped cilantro
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt
  • 1 lime, juiced

Steps

  1. Chop the tomatoes, onion, jalapeño, and cilantro. Stir in salt and lime juice.
  2. Let the salsa sit 15 minutes in the refrigerator so it juices. Do not cook it.
  3. Taste for salt and heat. This is not a canning recipe. Keep it cold, below 40°F (4°C) if you are holding it, and use it within a few days.
  4. Serve it beside tri-tip that has been cooked to 145°F (63°C) and rested. Do not paint it on the meat over the fire.

Yellow mustard, vinegar, and a little sugar, tangy rather than sweet.

History

The mustard sauce of the South Carolina Midlands is widely linked to German settlement there. That is an association, not a proven single origin, and this recipe does not crown an inventor. Cultural background

A Midlands mustard sauce. Yellow mustard is the body of it. It is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard brings acid and a sharp aroma that cuts pork fat, and vinegar keeps the sauce pourable. A little sugar rounds the raw edge of the mustard without turning the bowl into molasses. The yellow color is how the Midlands plate is recognized.

How it is made

Stir yellow mustard with vinegar and sugar and warm it briefly.

Authentic touch. Warm it only until the sugar dissolves. A long boil dulls the mustard.

How it is used

On chopped pork and in a bowl beside the meat. It also seasons some pots of hash.

Field notes

Ingredients

  3. Cool and refrigerate. Use within a week. This is not a canning recipe. Mustard is an allergen.

Texas Beef Mop

A thin, savory basting liquid of beef stock, vinegar, and Worcestershire.

5 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 8 · about 2 cups

History

A Texas beef mop is a thin liquid brushed on brisket or beef ribs during a long cook, not a thick table sauce. Beef stock, vinegar, and Worcestershire keep the surface moist without becoming the point of the meat. Salt and pepper remain the rub. Ordinary Worcestershire contains anchovy and usually wheat, so this mop is not gluten-free and not vegetarian.

Cultural background

A thin basting mop, not a Kansas City glaze and not a finishing sauce. It uses cooked beef stock. Do not mop with raw meat juices that have not been boiled.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and a little Worcestershire refresh the bark during a cook that would otherwise dry the surface, while beef stock echoes the meat instead of sweetening it. Because the mop is thin, it does not hide the post-oak smoke. It is a tool during the cook, which is why it should not be confused with sauce on the side.

How it is made

Simmer beef stock, vinegar, and Worcestershire into a thin mop and brush it on sparingly.

Authentic touch. Mop lightly. A soaking-wet brisket steams and softens the bark you waited for.

How it is used

Used on beef during the smoke. It is not usually poured over the sliced meat.

Field notes

  • A mop is a thin baste used during the cook, not the same thing as a thick table sauce.
  • Worcestershire in this mop brings fish and, in ordinary brands, wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 cups cooked beef stock, not raw drippings
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons Worcestershire sauce
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Combine cooked beef stock, vinegar, Worcestershire, and pepper. Ordinary Worcestershire contains anchovy and usually wheat.
  2. Simmer 10 minutes. If you started from anything that touched raw meat, boil it fully before it goes back on cooked food.
  3. Cool what you will not use immediately and refrigerate it. This is not a canning recipe.
  4. Brush a little on beef during a smoke. Finish the brisket at about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Do not soak the bark.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Texas Dalmatian Rub

Only salt and coarse black pepper.

5 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1/2 cup

History

The Central Texas rub called Dalmatian is salt and coarse black pepper, nothing else, and the speckled look suggested the name. It is the seasoning on Lockhart-style brisket and on beef plate ribs. Sugar, paprika, and garlic are left out on purpose. The mix is gluten-free, dairy-free, and nut-free because those ingredients are not in it.

Cultural background

Salt and coarse pepper only. Not a Memphis paprika rub and not a sweet Kansas City sauce. A dry mix for beef.

Why it tastes this way

Coarse pepper toasts in post-oak smoke and makes the bark, while salt seasons the beef all the way through a long cook. Extra spices would cover the meat, which is the opposite of the meat-market idea. The wood, not the rub, supplies the smoke.

How it is made

Stir equal parts kosher salt and coarse black pepper. Stop there.

Authentic touch. Use coarse pepper, not fine dust. Fine pepper disappears into the salt and does not speckle the crust.

How it is used

Rubbed onto brisket, beef ribs, or other Central Texas beef before the smoke.

Field notes

  • A Dalmatian rub is only salt and coarse black pepper.
  • Central Texas brisket uses this rub and treats sauce as optional.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt for the fat side
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper for the fat side
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt for the meat side
  • 1 tablespoon coarse black pepper for the meat side

Steps

  1. Stir the salt and the coarse pepper together. Do not add sugar, paprika, or garlic.
  2. Keep the mix dry in a jar. It is not cooked.
  3. Coat beef on every side before a low smoke over post oak or other oak.
  4. For brisket, cook to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest. The rub itself is only salt and pepper.

Toyomansi Dip

Salty soy cut with sour calamansi, with garlic and black pepper.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 3/4 cup

History

Toyomansi is a Filipino dipping sauce of toyo, soy sauce, and kalamansi, the small citrus squeezed over grilled pork, chicken, and fish. It is a table mix, stirred when the grill is going, not a cooked glaze. Ordinary soy sauce contains soy and wheat. This bowl has no meat and no chili, so the heat stays at none; chili can be added by the person eating.

Cultural background

Filipino tables set this dip beside inihaw. There is soy and wheat, and no meat, dairy, egg, or peanut. It is vegetarian. Use it the day you mix it, and refrigerate it. It is not a preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Soy is salty and dark, and calamansi is sharply sour, so together they cut grilled fat the way a heavy gravy would not. Garlic and black pepper make it more than salted citrus. On a Manila grill table it is the spoonful that finishes liempo, chicken, or fish without covering the charcoal taste.

How it is made

Stir soy sauce, calamansi juice, garlic, and black pepper.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the calamansi at the last hour. The juice dulls if the dip sits all day in a warm kitchen.

How it is used

Put it in a small bowl and let people spoon it over grilled meat or fish.

Field notes

  • Toyomansi is soy sauce and calamansi, a standard Filipino grill dip.
  • Ordinary soy sauce contains wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup calamansi juice, or lime juice

  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon water

  2. Taste it. If the soy is very salty, add another spoon of citrus, not more salt.
  3. Refrigerate it until the grill is ready. Do not leave garlic sitting out in the sauce for hours.
  4. Serve cool, the same day. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe.

A sweet, salty soy glaze with mirin, reduced until it clings to a skewer.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Tare is the sweet soy glaze brushed on yakitori. Shop pots are sometimes reduced again and again in the same pan. Tokyo yakitori shops brush tare on in the last moments so the sugar does not burn. This pan has soy and wheat, and no meat, dairy, egg, or sesame. It is vegetarian. Cool it and keep it cold. It is not a canned sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, mirin, and sugar reduce into a lacquer that tastes sweet and salty at once, and a little bitterness from the grill sticks to it. Brushed on late, it glazes the chicken instead of boiling it. A shop pot that has held years of drippings is deeper. A fresh pot is still the same idea: soy and sugar meeting charcoal.

How it is made

Simmer soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar until the glaze clings, then cool it.

Authentic touch. Reduce it until it coats a spoon. A thin soy splash runs off the skewer and flares in the coals.

How it is used

Brush it on grilled chicken in the last minute, or serve a spoon of it beside salted yakitori.

Field notes

  • Yakitori tare is a sweet soy glaze. Salt is the other usual finish.
  • This pot has no chicken in it. Some shop sauces are reduced with meat and would not be vegetarian.
