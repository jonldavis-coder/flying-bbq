Eastern North Carolina Whole Hog

Chopped pork, sharp with vinegar and red pepper, both lean and fatty in each bite.

40 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings from a home shoulder and belly

History

Eastern North Carolina barbecue is whole-hog cooking, chopped and dressed with vinegar and pepper, with no tomato in the sauce. The recipe here is a home version that uses a pork shoulder and a strip of pork belly, labeled as such because most kitchens cannot cook a whole hog. Enslaved pitmasters did this labor, and Black pitmasters carried it into the public trade, while the vinegar habit fits English and Scots-Irish settlers. No single inventor owns the plate.

Cultural background

Chopped eastern Carolina hog in a vinegar-pepper sauce. The written cook is shoulder plus belly, not a whole animal, and it is not Lexington shoulder.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar cuts hog fat in a humid climate, and cayenne and black pepper were the heat on hand. Mixing a little belly into shoulder copies the way a whole hog eats, with fat against lean. The sauce is the seasoning, not a glaze.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and a piece of belly, chop them together, and dress the chop with vinegar and pepper.

Authentic touch. Chop the pork while it is still warm and dress it with sauce. Do not leave the meat in dry chunks.

How it is used

Piled on a tray or a bun with more sauce on the table. Coleslaw beside it is the vinegar kind, not a mayonnaise slaw.

Field notes

  • Eastern North Carolina sauce is vinegar and pepper, without tomato, which is how it differs from Lexington dip.
  • A whole hog was plantation and public-pit work. This page uses shoulder and belly so a home cook can make the same chopped plate.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 7 pounds
  • 1 pound pork belly, skin removed
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt the shoulder and belly. Heat a smoker for indirect cooking at about 250°F (121°C) with hickory or oak.
  2. Stir vinegar, red pepper, black pepper, and the sauce salt. This sauce has no tomato.
  3. Smoke the meats until the shoulder reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and shreds. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; chopped hog is cooked hotter so the collagen softens. The belly should be tender and hot throughout.
  4. Rest the meats, chop shoulder and belly together, and fold in sauce until the chop is moist. Serve the rest of the sauce on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Shoulder alone is acceptable if belly is hard to find; the chop will be a little leaner.

Pairings and uses

  • Feasts
  • vinegar slaw
  • hush puppies
  • white bread
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Lexington Pork Shoulder

Smoked pork shoulder chopped and dressed with a vinegar dip that has a little tomato.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Lexington, in the North Carolina Piedmont, cooks pork shoulder rather than a whole hog, and the dip is vinegar reddened with a little tomato or ketchup. That is a different plate from eastern Carolina whole hog, which leaves the tomato out. Pitmasters in the Piedmont, many of them Black, made this the town style. A single inventor is not something this recipe claims.

Cultural background

Piedmont shoulder with Lexington dip. It is not eastern vinegar-only hog and not a thick Memphis or Kansas City sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder fat stands up to vinegar, and a small amount of tomato rounds the dip without turning it into a sweet glaze. Hickory smoke is common in this part of the state. The red slaw beside it uses the same dip, so the plate tastes like one idea.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and chop or pull it, then dress it with a thin vinegar dip that contains a little ketchup.

Authentic touch. Use only enough ketchup to blush the vinegar. If the dip tastes like tomato sauce, it has left Lexington.

How it is used

Chopped on a plate or a tray, with red slaw and more dip. It is often a sandwich, but the bun is optional.

Field notes

  • Lexington dip contains a little tomato. Eastern North Carolina sauce does not.
  • The Piedmont plate is pork shoulder, not the whole hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 1/2 cups apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 1 teaspoon crushed red pepper
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the dip
  • 1/2 cup water

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder. Smoke it indirectly over hickory at about 250°F (121°C).
  2. Stir vinegar, ketchup, red pepper, sauce salt, and water into a thin dip. It should look blush-red, not thick.
  3. Cook the shoulder to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), until it pulls. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; this shoulder goes further for tenderness.
  4. Rest, chop, and moisten with dip. Serve extra dip on the side.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pulled in large pieces is fine; many Lexington plates are chopped finer.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • Sandwiches
  • red slaw
  • hush puppies
  • dip
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

South Carolina Midlands Mustard Pork

Smoked pork shoulder with a tangy, yellow mustard sauce.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

The South Carolina Midlands are known for pork shoulder dressed with a mustard sauce. That style is widely linked to German settlement in the region. The link is an association, not a proven single origin, and this page does not name an inventor. It is a different sauce from eastern Carolina vinegar and from Lexington's tomato dip.

Cultural background

Midlands pork with mustard sauce. It is not a Carolina vinegar hog and not a hash, which is the stew served beside some South Carolina pits.

Why it tastes this way

Yellow mustard brings acid and a sharp edge that cuts shoulder fat, and a little sugar keeps it from tasting raw. Smoke from the pit sits under the sauce because the sauce goes on after the shoulder is tender. The yellow color is the regional signal on the plate.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder, then dress the chopped meat with a mustard, vinegar, and sugar sauce.

Authentic touch. Sauce the chopped pork, and also send a bowl of sauce to the table. Do not paint it on so early that the sugars burn.

How it is used

Chopped, with extra mustard sauce, often beside rice and hash or a simple slaw.

Field notes

  • Midlands mustard sauce is widely linked to German settlement, which is not the same as a documented single inventor.
  • The sauce is yellow mustard, so this plate is a different Carolina barbecue from the vinegar hog of the east.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 cup yellow mustard
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper, for the sauce
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder and smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) until it reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and pulls apart. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; shoulder for chopping goes hotter.
  2. Stir mustard, vinegar, brown sugar, pepper, and salt into a sauce. Warm it only until the sugar dissolves.
  3. Rest the shoulder, chop or pull it, and fold in sauce.
  4. Serve the remaining sauce on the side. The mustard is an allergen.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spicier sauce can take more cayenne. The mustard stays the base.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • rice
  • barbecue hash
  • sliced onion
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Alabama White-Sauce Chicken

Smoked chicken with a peppery, tangy mayonnaise sauce.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Alabama white sauce is a documented restaurant invention: Big Bob Gibson in Decatur, in the 1920s, served smoked chicken with a mayonnaise and vinegar sauce. It is not a folk sauce of unclear age. The chicken is still a smoked bird; the white sauce is what makes the plate recognizable.

Cultural background

Smoked chicken dressed with mayonnaise white sauce from Decatur. It is not a vinegar mop and not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Mayonnaise carries pepper, acid, and a little sugar onto skin that has taken hickory smoke. The sauce is cool and sharp against the rich skin. Because it is mayonnaise, it is an egg sauce and it should stay cold until it is served.

How it is made

Smoke chicken indirectly and serve it with a cold mayonnaise, vinegar, and pepper sauce.

Authentic touch. Smoke the chicken until the skin is set, then sauce it. Do not boil the mayonnaise sauce or it will break.

How it is used

Jointed, with sauce over the meat or on the side, often with pickles and white bread.

Field notes

  • Big Bob Gibson's restaurant in Decatur is the documented 1920s source of Alabama white sauce.
  • The sauce is mayonnaise and vinegar, so it contains egg and is not a tomato barbecue sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 pounds, halved
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 cup mayonnaise made from egg, oil, and vinegar
  • 1/4 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper, for the sauce
  • 1/2 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 teaspoon kosher salt, for the sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the chicken halves. Heat a smoker for indirect cooking at about 275°F (135°C).
  2. Whisk mayonnaise, vinegar, pepper, cayenne, salt, and sugar. Refrigerate the sauce. Do not leave mayonnaise in the sun.
  3. Smoke the chicken until the thickest part of the thigh, not touching bone, reaches 165°F (74°C).
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve with the cold white sauce. Do not serve the chicken rare.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Half chickens cook more evenly on a small grill than a whole bird crowded into the center.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • white bread
  • pickles
  • potato salad
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Memphis Dry-Rubbed Ribs

Pork ribs with a paprika, sugar, and pepper crust and no sauce on the meat.

25 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Memphis barbecue is pork, and one of its defining plates is a rib coated in a dry rub and served without sauce on the meat. Black pitmasters and Black neighborhoods built much of the city's public barbecue. A wet shoulder is a different Memphis plate. This one stays dry.

Cultural background

Dry-rubbed pork spare ribs. Sauce is not brushed on these ribs. A sauced shoulder is the wet Memphis entry, not this one.

Why it tastes this way

Paprika, salt, pepper, and a little sugar form a bark in hickory smoke. Leaving the sauce off lets that crust stay crisp instead of steaming. The sweetness is in the rub, not in a molasses glaze.

How it is made

Rub pork ribs and smoke them indirectly, with no sauce on the meat.

Authentic touch. If someone wants sauce, put it in a cup. Brushing it on this rib turns the plate into something else.

How it is used

Whole bones on paper, with pickles and white bread. Any sauce is strictly on the side.

Field notes

  • A Memphis dry rib is defined by the rub on the meat and the absence of sauce on top.
  • Memphis wet shoulder is a separate plate that uses a thin tomato-vinegar sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 racks pork spareribs, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons paprika
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar
  • 2 teaspoons garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoons onion powder
  • Hickory wood for the fire

Steps

  1. Pull the membrane from the bone side if it is still attached. Mix the rub and coat both sides.
  2. Set a smoker for indirect heat around 250°F (121°C) with hickory.
  3. Cook until the meat has drawn back from the bone ends and the thickest meat is about 195–203°F (90–95°C). Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest; tenderness comes later.
  4. Rest briefly and serve with no sauce on the ribs.

Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest. For tender ribs, cook until the meat has pulled back from the ends of the bones, often around 195–203°F (90–95°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Baby back ribs cook faster than spareribs. Use a thermometer rather than the clock.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • pickles
  • white bread
  • coleslaw
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Owensboro Mutton

Smoky mutton or lamb shoulder with a dark, peppery Worcestershire dip.

30 min prep · 7 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Owensboro, Kentucky, made mutton its barbecue, closely tied to Catholic parish picnics that cooked sheep for crowds. The dark table sauce is called black dip, heavy on Worcestershire and black pepper. This is western Kentucky's plate, not a pork shoulder with a Carolina sauce. Burgoo is the stew that often sat beside the same pits.

Cultural background

Lamb or mutton shoulder cooked for a chopped or sliced plate and served with black dip. It is parish-picnic barbecue, not a weeknight chop.

Why it tastes this way

Older sheep has a stronger flavor than young lamb, and a peppery Worcestershire dip was built to meet it. Smoke rounds the fat. The dip's anchovy and vinegar cut the richness the way a vinegar sauce cuts pork, but the meat is not pork.

How it is made

Smoke a lamb or mutton shoulder until it is tender enough to slice or chop, and serve it with black dip.

Authentic touch. If the market sells lamb shoulder rather than true mutton, say lamb. Mutton is an older animal, and the flavor is stronger.

How it is used

Chopped or sliced, with black dip, often beside burgoo at a large picnic.

Field notes

  • Owensboro's mutton barbecue is closely tied to Catholic parish picnics.
  • Black dip is the dark Worcestershire sauce served with the meat, not a tomato glaze.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in lamb or mutton shoulder, about 6 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1/2 cup Worcestershire sauce
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper, for the dip
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground allspice

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) over hickory or oak.
  2. Stir Worcestershire, water, pepper, and allspice for a thin black dip. Ordinary Worcestershire contains anchovy and usually wheat.
  3. Cook the shoulder to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes. For a choppable picnic shoulder, continue to about 195°F (90°C) so it softens.
  4. Slice or chop and serve with the dip. The dip is not vegetarian.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lamb shoulder is the workable market cut when mutton is not labeled as such.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Feasts
  • black dip
  • burgoo
  • white bread
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

St. Louis Pork Steaks

Thick pork shoulder steaks, seared, with a light tangy glaze.

15 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

A St. Louis pork steak is a slice of pork shoulder, cooked on a hot grill rather than smoked all day like a whole butt. It is a city and backyard plate along the Missouri side of the river. It is not a loin chop, and it is not a Kansas City sparerib.

Cultural background

Shoulder steaks over direct heat. The cut is the reason the steak is wide and a little tough unless it is cooked with care.

Why it tastes this way

Shoulder meat has more fat and connective tissue than a loin, so a hard sear plus a short rest keeps it juicy. A thin glaze of ketchup and vinegar is a local habit, added late so it does not scorch. Direct charcoal is the fire, not an offset pit.

How it is made

Grill pork shoulder steaks over direct heat and glaze them at the end.

Authentic touch. Ask the butcher for shoulder steaks about three-quarters of an inch thick. A thin slice will dry out before the center is done.

How it is used

Whole steaks on a plate, often with a simple salad or potatoes. They are a cookout cut, not a sandwich filling.

Field notes

  • St. Louis pork steaks are cut from the shoulder, so they are wider than a loin chop.
  • They are a hot-grill plate, not a day-long smoke of a whole shoulder.

Ingredients

  • 4 pork shoulder steaks, about 3/4 inch thick
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 2 teaspoons black pepper
  • 1/4 cup ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the steaks. Heat a charcoal or gas grill for direct cooking, with one cooler zone.
  2. Stir ketchup, vinegar, and brown sugar into a thin glaze.
  3. Grill the steaks, turning once, until the center reaches 145°F (63°C). Rest at least 3 minutes. Cooks who want them less pink can continue toward 175°F (79°C).
  4. Brush with glaze in the last minute so the sugar does not burn.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Many cooks take them well past pink. The safety line here is still 145°F with a rest, and you may cook longer if you want them softer.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • potato salad
  • grilled onion
  • pickles
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Chicago South Side Rib Tips

Chewy, smoky rib tips with a peppery rub and a light tangy sauce.

20 min prep · 3 hr cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Rib tips are a Black South Side barbecue plate in Chicago. They are the cartilaginous ends left when spareribs are trimmed, cooked as their own meat rather than discarded. A local equipment habit is the aquarium smoker, a pit built in a metal aquarium frame. This page describes that city tradition without naming one shop as the inventor.

Cultural background

South Side rib tips. They are not a full slab of Memphis ribs and not a Texas beef rib.

Why it tastes this way

Tips have skin, fat, and cartilage, so they stay rich in a shorter smoke than a whole spare rib. A pepper rub and a thin sauce match the sweet-hot plates of Chicago barbecue. Smoke from a closed cooker, including those aquarium-frame pits, wraps the meat more tightly than an open grate.

How it is made

Rub rib tips and smoke them covered until tender, then sauce them lightly if you want.

Authentic touch. Cook tips in a covered pit so the smoke stays. On an open grill they dry out before the cartilage softens.

How it is used

By the pile, often with fries, white bread, and a hot link. The fries are a side, not part of this cook.

Field notes

  • Rib tips are the ends trimmed from spareribs, and Chicago cooks them as their own plate.
  • Aquarium smokers, built in metal aquarium frames, are a South Side pit tradition.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds pork rib tips
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 2 teaspoons black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon cayenne
  • 1/2 cup ketchup
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 tablespoon brown sugar

Steps

  1. Rub the tips with salt, paprika, black pepper, and cayenne.
  2. Smoke them covered and indirect at about 275°F (135°C). A covered cooker is the stand-in for an aquarium smoker.
  3. Cook until the meat is tender and the thickest piece is about 195–203°F (90–95°C). Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest; tips are cooked longer so the cartilage softens.
  4. Stir ketchup, vinegar, and brown sugar and toss the tips with a little of it at the end, or serve the sauce on the side.

Pork ribs are safe at 145°F (63°C) after a 3-minute rest. For tender ribs, cook until the meat has pulled back from the ends of the bones, often around 195–203°F (90–95°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A light sauce at the end is common. Leave it off if you want to taste only the rub.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • white bread
  • fries
  • hot links
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Kansas City Burnt Ends

Cubes of brisket point, tender, peppery, and sweet-smoky at the edges.

40 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Kansas City sat on rail lines that moved cattle and cooks, and its barbecue became known for a sweet, smoky plate. Henry Perry, a Black cook from the Memphis area, is the early figure local history credits with selling barbecue in the city in the early twentieth century. Burnt ends are cubes from the fatty point of the brisket, returned to the pit. This page credits Perry as that early local figure without claiming he invented every later sauce.

Cultural background

Point-muscle cubes finished sweet and smoky. They are a Kansas City plate, not a plain Central Texas slice, though both start with brisket.

Why it tastes this way

The point is fattier than the flat, so cubes stay moist while the edges caramelize. A sweet glaze meets pepper and smoke because the city sauce tradition runs sweeter than Texas salt and pepper. The second cook is what makes an end, not a single slice of brisket.

How it is made

Smoke a brisket point, cube it, glaze it, and return it to the pit until the edges are sticky.

Authentic touch. Use the point, not the lean flat. Flat cubes dry out before they become burnt ends.

How it is used

In a pile, with bread and pickles. They are also eaten on a sandwich.

Field notes

  • Burnt ends are cut from the brisket point, the fattier of the two muscles.
  • Local Kansas City history credits Henry Perry as an early barbecue seller there, without a claim that one cook invented the later sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 brisket point, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper
  • 1 cup ketchup
  • 1/4 cup molasses
  • 2 tablespoons brown sugar
  • 2 tablespoons apple cider vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon paprika
  • Oak or hickory for the fire

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the point. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) until it is about 190°F (88°C) inside and the bark is set.
  2. Stir ketchup, molasses, brown sugar, vinegar, and paprika into a glaze. This glaze uses molasses, not honey, and it leaves out Worcestershire so the plate stays free of that fish and wheat ingredient.
  3. Cube the point, toss with glaze, and return the cubes to the pit until they are sticky and the center of a cube is about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; burnt ends are cooked hotter for tenderness.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole brisket can be sliced for the flat while the point is cubed for ends.

Pairings and uses

  • Beef
  • Sandwiches
  • white bread
  • pickles
  • sweet sauce
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

East Texas Chopped Beef

Smoked beef chopped into a thinner tomato sauce, tangy and a little sweet.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

East Texas chopped beef is a sauced plate, different from Central Texas brisket, where pepper and oak are the whole story and sauce is optional. In the Piney Woods the beef is often chopped and mixed with a thinner tomato sauce. This version keeps that distinction: smoke the beef, then dress it.

Cultural background

Chopped beef in a loose tomato sauce for sandwiches or plates. It is not a Lockhart slice and not a thick Kansas City burnt-end glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Hickory or oak smoke gives the beef a deeper edge, and the sauce loosens the chop so it eats like a sandwich filling. Tomato and vinegar cut the fat. The sauce is thinner than a molasses-heavy city sauce, which is the East Texas point.

How it is made

Smoke a brisket or beef roast, chop it, and moisten it with a thin tomato-vinegar sauce.

Authentic touch. Chop the beef after it rests, then fold in only enough sauce to moisten it. The meat should not swim.

How it is used

On a bun or on a plate with the extra sauce in a cup. It is a chopped sandwich as often as a sliced platter.

Field notes

  • East Texas chopped beef is sauced on purpose, which separates it from salt-and-pepper Central Texas brisket.
  • The sauce in this plate is kept thin so the chopped beef stays loose rather than candy-thick.

Ingredients

  • 1 beef brisket flat or chuck roast, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 2 tablespoons coarse black pepper
  • 1 cup crushed tomato
  • 1/2 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/4 cup brown sugar
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 8 hamburger buns

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the beef. Smoke it indirectly over hickory or oak at about 250°F (121°C).
  2. Cook until it is probe-tender, about 195–205°F (90–96°C). The roast is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; chopped barbecue beef is cooked longer so it will break apart.
  3. Simmer tomato, vinegar, brown sugar, and onion until the onion softens, about 15 minutes. Taste for salt.
  4. Rest the beef, chop it, and fold in enough sauce to moisten. Serve on buns, which contain wheat, so this plate is not gluten-free.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A chuck roast can stand in for brisket if that is the cut you can buy.

Central Texas Salt-and-Pepper Brisket

Beefy and peppery, with a dark bark and a slice that is smoky rather than sweet.

30 min prep · 12 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

In meat-market towns such as Lockhart, German- and Czech-speaking butchers smoked beef, including the tough chest cut that became brisket, over post oak. African American pitmasters worked those fires and ran their own pits, so the craft is both a Central European butcher tradition and a Black Texas one. Salt and coarse black pepper became the seasoning that defines the style. Sauce, if a market offers it, is optional and is not the point.

Cultural background

This is sliced Central Texas brisket, not East Texas chopped beef in tomato sauce and not a Kansas City burnt end. The cook is a long indirect smoke.

Why it tastes this way

Post oak burns milder than mesquite, so the smoke stays in the background while salt and coarse pepper make the bark. A long cook renders the fat cap and softens collagen that a steak temperature would leave chewy. The dry Central Texas air and an offset pit are why the surface can darken without steaming.

How it is made

Season a whole brisket with salt and coarse pepper only, then smoke it indirectly over post oak until the flat is tender.

Authentic touch. Leave the fat cap about a quarter inch thick, and do not wrap the brisket until the bark is the color you want.

How it is used

Sliced across the grain, with the point and flat both on the board. Sauce stays on the side if it is there at all. White bread, raw onion, and pickles are the usual companions.

Field notes

  • Post oak is the wood Central Texas pitmasters usually name for this kind of brisket.
  • Lockhart is one of the meat-market towns associated with salt-and-pepper beef.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole beef brisket, about 12 pounds, fat cap trimmed to about 1/4 inch
  • 1/4 cup kosher salt
  • 1/4 cup coarse black pepper
  • Post oak chunks, or other oak if post oak is unavailable
  • Water in a spray bottle

Steps

  1. Mix the salt and pepper and coat the brisket on all sides. Refrigerate it if you season it ahead, and do not leave it on the counter.
  2. Heat an offset smoker or a covered grill for indirect cooking to about 250°F (121°C). Burn post oak, or another oak. Keep the fire clean rather than smoldering wet chips.
  3. Smoke the brisket fat cap up until the thickest part of the flat reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance. Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; brisket is taken higher so it will slice instead of bounce. The stall, often around 150–170°F (65–77°C), is evaporative cooling.
  4. Rest the brisket at least 30 minutes before slicing across the grain. A 3-minute rest is the safety minimum; a large brisket needs longer. Serve sauce on the side only if you want it.

Whole beef is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For brisket that slices instead of bouncing, keep cooking until the thickest part of the flat is about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and a probe slides in with little resistance, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A butcher paper wrap during the stall keeps the bark drier than foil.
  • The point can be cubed later for burnt ends, which is a Kansas City plate rather than this one.

South Texas Barbacoa de Cabeza

Soft, beefy cheek with onion, cumin, and dried chile, meant to be shredded.

30 min prep · 5 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Mexican barbacoa is a pit cook, and in much of central Mexico the meat was wrapped with maguey leaves over heated stones. South Texas barbacoa de cabeza is the beef-head version of that pit tradition, closely tied to Mexican and Mexican American cooking along the border. This recipe is a covered braise of beef cheek, the home version of that tenderness, not a pit. It does not claim that a single Indigenous nation invented the modern cheek recipe.

Cultural background

A home covered braise for beef cheek in the South Texas barbacoa de cabeza tradition. It is not a Lockhart brisket and not a whole-head pit cook.

Why it tastes this way

Cheek is full of collagen, so a covered pot turns it silky instead of searing it like a steak. Cumin, dried chile, and onion are market seasonings of northern Mexico and South Texas. The maguey-and-pit aroma of a real earth oven is not something a home braise copies.

How it is made

Braise seasoned beef cheeks in a covered pot or oven until they shred, as a home version of pit barbacoa.

Authentic touch. If beef cheek is unavailable, use chuck and say so. Chuck is a workable home stand-in, not the same cut as cachete.

How it is used

Chopped or shredded, with onion, cilantro, and salsa, often in a warm tortilla. The tortillas are corn in this recipe.

Field notes

  • Barbacoa de cabeza means head barbecue, and the cheek is the part most home cooks can actually buy.
  • The Spanish word barbacoa comes from a Taíno word for a stick framework, which is a word history, not a single recipe for beef cheek.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds beef cheeks, or chuck roast if cheeks are unavailable
  • 2 teaspoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon ground cumin
  • 2 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 1 bay leaf
  • 2 cups water

Steps

  1. Toast the guajillos in a dry pan until fragrant, then soak them in hot water until soft and blend them with a little of that water, the cumin, and garlic.
  2. Salt the cheeks, coat them with the chile puree, and put them in a heavy pot with onion, bay, and water. This covered braise is the home version, not a maguey-lined pit.
  3. Cover and cook in a 325°F (163°C) oven or over low heat until the cheek shreds and the center is about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Beef offal is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a rest; cheek needs the higher temperature for tenderness.
  4. Rest 10 minutes, shred, and taste for salt. Discard the bay leaf.

Cook beef offal to at least 145°F (63°C) with a rest. Cook poultry offal, including chicken hearts and livers, to 165°F (74°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chuck roast, cooked the same covered way, is the workable substitute when cheek is not sold nearby.
