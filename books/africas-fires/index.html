Moroccan Mechoui

Lamb with cumin, salt, and the fat of a long roast.

40 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Mechoui is a Moroccan celebration lamb, cooked in a pit or on a spit for Eid al-Adha and other gatherings. Amazigh (Berber) communities and other Moroccan families both cook it. It is not one village's secret. A whole lamb is the feast; a shoulder is the home piece on this page, still seasoned with salt and cumin and roasted slowly. The name refers to roasting. It is a celebration, not a weeknight chop.

Cultural background

Moroccan hosts, in the Atlas and in cities, cook mechoui when the occasion calls for a whole animal or a large cut. The home shoulder is a scale change, not a different cuisine. It is not a tagine and not a minced brochette.

Why it tastes this way

Slow heat renders lamb fat over salt and cumin until the spice smells toasted rather than raw. A pit or a spit smokes the outside; an oven roast is milder and still tastes of the same seasoning. Cumin is the spice people expect on the meat once it is pulled apart. The celebration setting, especially Eid, is why the animal is large and shared.

How it is made

Rub a lamb shoulder with salt and cumin and roast it slowly on a spit or in a covered grill. A pit or a whole lamb is the celebration method when you have both.

Authentic touch. Salt the lamb more than you would a chop, and rub the cumin in with oil. If you are roasting a shoulder at home, say it is a shoulder, not a whole pit lamb.

How it is used

Pulled or carved, with bread and cumin on the side for dipping. People eat it with their hands at a celebration table.

Field notes

  • Mechoui is a Moroccan celebration lamb, pit or spit, cooked for Eid and other gatherings.
  • Amazigh communities and other Moroccan families cook it. It is not one village's secret.

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb shoulder, about 6 lb
  • 2 tablespoons salt
  • 2 tablespoons ground cumin
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 4 garlic cloves, chopped

Steps

  1. Mix salt, cumin, pepper, garlic, and oil. Rub the shoulder thoroughly.
  2. Spit-roast over coals or roast on a rack in a covered grill at a moderate heat. A whole lamb in a pit is the celebration version if you have the animal and the pit.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest. For a shoulder you want to pull, continue toward 195°F (90°C).
  4. Rest, then carve or pull. Offer more cumin and salt. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole lamb in a pit or on a spit for a real gathering, with the same salt and cumin.
  • A home oven shoulder, labeled as a home oven, if there is no spit.

Egyptian Kofta

Minced beef and lamb with parsley, onion, and a grilled crust.

25 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Egyptian kofta is grilled minced beef or lamb, mixed with parsley, onion, and spices, and cooked over charcoal in Cairo and elsewhere. It is a different plate from Moroccan kefta brochettes, which have their own spice and skewer habit. The Egyptian mix here leans on parsley, cumin, and coriander. The meat is ground and cooked through. It is street and home food, not a festival invention.

Cultural background

Egyptian households and grill shops cook kofta as ordinary meat. North Africa is the region for this page. It is not raw kibbeh and not a meatball in tomato sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Parsley and onion cut the fat of a beef-and-lamb mince, and cumin and coriander toast on the grill. Charcoal gives a bitter-brown edge that pan frying only approximates. The blend is familiar across Egyptian grills and still not the same as a Moroccan brochette heavy on paprika. Ground meat needs a cooked center, which sets the texture.

How it is made

Mix minced beef and lamb with herbs and spices, form sausages or patties, and grill them until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Grate and squeeze the onion. If the mix is wet, the kofta will slump through the grate.

How it is used

With salad, tahini sauce if you eat sesame, and rice. Tahini is not in this recipe, so sesame is not an allergen here.

Field notes

  • Egyptian kofta is grilled minced beef or lamb with parsley and onion.
  • It is a different plate from Moroccan kefta brochettes.

Ingredients

  • 1 lb ground beef
  • 1/2 lb ground lamb
  • 1 small onion, grated and squeezed dry
  • 1 cup parsley, finely chopped
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the beef, lamb, onion, parsley, cumin, coriander, and salt until the meat holds together.
  2. Form eight short sausages. Heat a grill to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Serve with salad. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All beef or all lamb, still ground.
  • On skewers, which is a shape change, not a different spice mix.

Nigerian Suya

Beef with a hot peanut spice, smoke, and a salty crust.

25 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Suya is a Hausa beef skewer from northern Nigeria, now sold at night stands in cities across the country. Yaji, the spice sprinkled on the meat, commonly includes ground peanut, ginger, and hot pepper. The beef is sliced thin from whole muscle, not minced. Kano is one of the northern centers. The peanut is an allergen, and this recipe is not nut-free.

Cultural background

Hausa mallams and the suya stands that grew from that grill are the cooks. It is a street skewer. It is not kilishi, the dried meat, and it is not Ghanaian kyinkyinga, which is related and separate.

Why it tastes this way

Thin beef browns fast over charcoal, and the peanut-pepper dust sticks to the oil on the surface. Ginger and chili heat the fat. Groundnut, long grown in the region, is what makes yaji taste like itself rather than like a plain pepper rub. Smoke from the roadside coals is the other half.

How it is made

Coat thin beef in a peanut-pepper spice and grill it on skewers until the whole muscle is safe, then rest it.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin, grill it hot, and dust it with more yaji as it comes off. Tell guests about the peanut before they eat.

How it is used

With more yaji, sliced onion, and tomato. Eat it off the stick while it is hot.

Field notes

  • Suya is a Hausa beef skewer, widely sold at Nigerian street grills.
  • Yaji commonly includes ground peanut, which is an allergen, so this dish is not nut-free.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb beef sirloin, sliced thin
  • 1/2 cup roasted peanuts, ground fine
  • 1 tablespoon ground ginger
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 2 teaspoons cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 8 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the peanuts, ginger, paprika, cayenne, and salt. Toss the beef with oil and half the spice. Thread onto skewers.
  2. Heat a grill until it is hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Give the beef a 3-minute rest. This is whole muscle, not ground meat.
  4. Dust with the remaining spice and serve. The peanut allergen is in the spice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicken pieces only if they reach 165°F (74°C), which is a different protein.
  • A milder dust for people who want less chili, still with peanut.

  • Street food
  • Sliced onion
  • Tomato

Nigerian Asun

Chopped grilled goat with ginger, garlic, and a heavy pepper heat.

25 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Asun is spicy grilled goat associated with Yoruba parties in southwestern Nigeria. The meat is grilled, chopped, and tossed with pepper, onion, and ginger so the spice coats every piece. Ibadan and Lagos parties both claim it as a tray that disappears fast. It is party food, not a quiet weekday soup. The goat is a whole-muscle cut cooked to a safe temperature before it is chopped.

Cultural background

Yoruba hosts and the cooks they hire for celebrations make asun. It is goat, not beef suya, though both can show up at a large party. The pepper is the point.

Why it tastes this way

Goat fat carries ginger and chili better than a lean chop does. Grilling first gives a browned edge, and chopping lets the raw-heat of fresh pepper sit on the hot meat without a long stew. That two-step is why asun tastes both smoky and freshly hot. It belongs to a crowded table.

How it is made

Season goat, grill it, rest it, chop it, and toss it with onion, ginger, and chili.

Authentic touch. Grill the goat far enough that the center is safe, then chop it and toss it with pepper off the fire so the fresh chili stays bright.

How it is used

In a pile, with more onion, at a party. Plantain is a common side and is not fried in this recipe.

Field notes

  • Asun is spicy grilled goat associated with Yoruba parties in southwestern Nigeria.
  • The meat is grilled and then chopped and peppered, which is why it is party food rather than a single chop on a plate.

Ingredients

  • 3 lb bone-in goat shoulder or leg, in large pieces
  • 1 tablespoon ground ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 3 fresh hot chiles, chopped
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Rub the goat with ginger, garlic, salt, and oil. Heat a grill for medium-high cooking.
  2. Grill, turning, until the meat is browned.
  3. Cook whole goat to at least 145°F (63°C) in the thickest part, away from bone, and give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Chop the meat, toss it with onion and fresh chile, and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A milder pepper level, still goat.
  • Bone-in pieces served whole if you would rather not chop them.

Ghanaian Kyinkyinga

Beef skewers with ginger, chili, and ground peanut, charred at the edges.

25 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Kyinkyinga, also called chichinga, is a Ghanaian meat skewer sold on the street in Accra and in other cities. The kinship with Hausa suya is real: both are spiced beef on sticks with a peppery groundnut dust. They are not identical. This mix is ginger-forward, with lime, and it is cooked and sold as Ghanaian street food. The beef is whole muscle. Peanut is in the spice, so the dish is not nut-free.

Cultural background

Ghanaian street vendors grill these to order. The page does not treat them as a renamed Nigerian suya. Northern Ghanaian Hausa grilling is part of the kinship, and the Accra stick is its own sale.

Why it tastes this way

Peanut, ginger, and chili cling to thin beef and toast over charcoal. Lime at the end brightens a spice that would otherwise taste only of fat and pepper. The kinship with suya is in that groundnut heat. The difference is in the balance and in the Ghanaian street where you buy it, not in a claim that one copied the other word for word.

How it is made

Spice thin beef with a ginger and peanut mix, grill it on skewers, and finish with lime.

Authentic touch. Grill the meat hot and dust it again after cooking. Name the peanut when you serve it.

How it is used

Off the stick with onion. It is street food.

Field notes

  • Kyinkyinga is a Ghanaian meat skewer, also called chichinga.
  • It is kin to Hausa suya and it is not the same dish.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb beef steak, sliced thin
  • 1/3 cup roasted peanuts, ground
  • 1 tablespoon ground ginger
  • 2 teaspoons cayenne
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 lime
  • 8 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix peanuts, ginger, cayenne, and salt. Toss the beef with oil and most of the spice. Thread onto skewers.
  2. Grill over a hot fire, turning once or twice.
  3. Cook the beef to at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Dust with the rest of the spice and add lime. Peanut is in the dust. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Goat instead of beef, still a whole-muscle cut.
  • Less cayenne, with the peanut and ginger kept.

Pairings and uses

  • Beef
  • Street food
  • Onion
  • Lime
  • Tomato
  • Gluten-free

Ghanaian Grilled Tilapia

Charred tilapia with ginger, garlic, and chili.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 fish

History

Grilled tilapia is a common fish plate in Ghana, often eaten with banku and shito. Banku is a fermented corn and cassava dough, and shito is a hot sauce whose recipes vary. This page grills the fish and names those pairings without copying a full shito or banku recipe, because the sauces and the dough are their own crafts. The fish is seasoned with ginger and chili and cooked until it flakes.

Cultural background

Ghanaian chop-bar and street grill cooks make this. The recipe is the fish. Shito is mentioned, not reproduced. It is not a fried fillet in batter.

Why it tastes this way

Tilapia is a mild freshwater fish, so ginger, garlic, and chili do the seasoning that an oily sea fish would not need. A hot grill dries the skin into a crust. Banku on the side is sour from fermentation, and shito is hot, which is why the plate is a contrast even though only the fish is written out here. The lake and pond fisheries are why tilapia is the fish on the grate.

How it is made

Rub whole tilapia with ginger, garlic, and chili, and grill until the flesh flakes.

Authentic touch. Score the fish so the ginger paste reaches the flesh. Oil the skin or it will stick and tear.

How it is used

With banku and shito as pairings. Shito recipes differ and are not copied in full here. Read a bought sauce for fish and other allergens.

Field notes

  • Grilled tilapia in Ghana is commonly paired with banku and shito.
  • This page does not copy a full shito recipe, because those sauces vary.

Ingredients

  • 4 whole tilapia, gutted and scaled
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 fresh hot chile, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Cut slashes in the fish. Mix ginger, garlic, chile, salt, and oil and rub it inside and out.
  2. Heat a grill and oil the grate.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve with the pairings you have. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole fish from another freshwater species, named as what it is.
  • Less chili in the rub, with the heat left to the sauce on the side.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • Banku
  • Shito
  • Sliced onion
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Senegalese Dibi

Grilled lamb with onion and a sharp mustard relish.

20 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Dibi is grilled lamb at Wolof street grills in Dakar and in other Senegalese cities. The meat is often lamb chops or a larger piece carved after the fire, served with raw onion and mustard. It is street food you eat at the grill, not a restaurant tasting menu. This recipe uses chops and a simple onion-mustard relish. The lamb is a whole-muscle cut.

Cultural background

Wolof dibiterie cooks tend these fires. The dish is lamb and charcoal. It is not yassa, the onion stew, though onion is on the plate.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat over charcoal is the base, and raw onion and mustard cut it the way a heavy sauce would not. The street grill is close to the coals, so the outside browns before the inside overcooks. Mustard is sharp and familiar at these stands. The combination is simple because the fire is doing the work.

How it is made

Season lamb chops, grill them, rest them, and serve with an onion and mustard relish.

Authentic touch. Serve the onion raw and the mustard cold against the hot meat. Do not cook the relish into a jam.

How it is used

Chopped or on the bone, with the relish, at the grill or at home.

Field notes

  • Dibi is grilled lamb associated with Wolof street grills in Senegal.
  • Onion and mustard are a common accompaniment at the stand.

Ingredients

  • 8 lamb chops
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 onion, thinly sliced
  • 2 tablespoons prepared mustard
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Salt and oil the chops. Stir the onion with mustard and pepper for the relish.
  2. Grill the chops over hot coals, turning once.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with the onion-mustard relish. Mustard is an allergen in the relish. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A larger leg, roasted and then sliced, still checked with a thermometer.
  • Less mustard in the relish if you want the onion to lead.

Senegalese Poisson Braise

Grilled fish with chili, lime, garlic, and a charred skin.

20 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Poisson braisé is grilled fish in Senegal, cooked at street fires and at home along the coast, including in Saint-Louis. The catch changes, and the method does not: a whole fish, scored, rubbed with garlic, chili, and lime, and set over charcoal. It is a different plate from dibi, which is lamb. The fish is done when it flakes.

Cultural background

Coastal Senegalese grill cooks make this from the day's fish. It is not a fried fritter and not a fish yassa stewed in onion, though those are also Senegalese.

Why it tastes this way

A hot grate blisters the skin and keeps the flesh moist if the fish is not left over dying coals. Lime and chili cut the oil of a sea fish. Garlic cooks on the surface. Saint-Louis and the Atlantic coast supply the habit of cooking the whole animal rather than a boneless fillet.

How it is made

Rub a whole fish with garlic, chili, and lime, and grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Leave the fish whole and turn it once with a wide spatula. Stuffing the cavity with too much wet marinade will steam it.

How it is used

With lime, onion, and rice or boiled millet. Eat it hot.

Field notes

  • Poisson braisé is grilled fish in Senegal, at street fires and at home.
  • The species follows the catch, and the method is a whole fish over charcoal.

Ingredients

  • 4 whole firm fish, about 1 lb each, gutted and scaled
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 fresh hot chile, chopped
  • 1 lime, juiced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Score the fish. Mix garlic, chile, lime juice, salt, and oil and rub the fish inside and out.
  2. Heat a charcoal grill and oil the grate.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh flakes.
  4. Serve with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sea bream or another firm fish, named as the fish you bought.
  • A milder chile if the one you have is very hot.

Pairings and uses

  • Street food
  • Lime
  • Rice
  • Sliced onion

Ivorian Poulet Braise

Charcoal chicken with garlic, onion, and chili, served beside fried plantain.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Poulet braisé is grilled chicken in Côte d'Ivoire, a street and maquis staple in Abidjan. The bird is seasoned with garlic, onion, and chili and cooked over charcoal. Alloco, fried ripe plantain, is the usual pairing and is named here rather than turned into a second full recipe. The chicken is poultry and is cooked through. It is everyday grill food.

Cultural background

Ivorian grill cooks at open-air restaurants make this to order. It is not a groundnut stew and not a festival ceremony. Alloco sits beside it.

Why it tastes this way

Chicken skin over charcoal picks up smoke and chili, and garlic browns in the fat. The heat is present without being the only note. Sweet fried plantain, if you make it separately, balances the char. The street fire is close and a little uneven, which is why the skin blisters in spots.

How it is made

Rub chicken with garlic, onion, and chili, and grill it over charcoal until the thick meat is cooked.

Authentic touch. Spatchcock or cut the chicken so the thighs are not still raw when the breast is done. Check the thigh, not the wing.

How it is used

With alloco, the fried plantain pairing, and sliced onion. The plantain is a side, fried in its own pan.

Field notes

  • Poulet braisé is grilled chicken sold widely in Côte d'Ivoire.
  • Alloco, fried ripe plantain, is a usual pairing.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 4 lb, cut into pieces
  • 1 onion, grated
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 fresh hot chile, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Mix onion, garlic, chile, salt, and oil. Rub the chicken and rest it 20 minutes in the refrigerator.
  2. Heat a charcoal grill for medium heat.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest part of the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), not touching bone.
  4. Rest 5 minutes. Serve with alloco if you have fried it separately. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thighs and drumsticks only, which are simpler to cook evenly.
  • A hotter chili paste if that is how the stand you know does it.

  • Street food
  • Alloco
  • Sliced onion

Cameroonian Soya

Beef skewers with a heavy local chili heat and ginger, and no groundnut.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Soya in Cameroon is a beef skewer related to Nigerian suya, sold on streets in Douala and other cities. This mix is not a copy of yaji. It uses more chili and no groundnut, so it is a different spice and it is nut-free. The kinship is the thin beef, the stick, and the pepper culture of the region. The meat is whole muscle and is cooked to a safe temperature.

Cultural background

Cameroonian street grillers cook soya as snack food. The page keeps it distinct from suya by the spice. It is not a groundnut sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Chili and ginger on thin beef toast quickly over charcoal and taste hotter because there is no groundnut to round them. That sharpness is the choice of this recipe. Smoke still binds it to the wider family of West and Central African street skewers. Leaving the groundnut out changes the dish on purpose.

How it is made

Rub thin beef with chili and ginger, without groundnut, and grill it on skewers.

Authentic touch. Use a fresh chili you know the heat of, and salt the meat before the spice so the crust is not only burn.

How it is used

With onion and tomato. It is a hot skewer, not a mild one.

Field notes

South African Boerewors

Beef and pork sausage with toasted coriander, vinegar, and a grilled casing.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Boerewors means farmer's sausage in Afrikaans, and grilling a coil of it is a defining braai habit in South Africa. Coriander is the spice people expect. Commercial wors often contains a grain filler; this home mix does not. The sausage is fresh and ground, and it is cooked through. It is backyard and holiday food.

Cultural background

South African households cook boerewors on a braai. This page is a fresh coil without a grain filler. It is not a smoked frank and not a lamb chop.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted coriander and a splash of vinegar cut a fatty beef-and-pork grind. Charcoal browns the casing and renders the surface. Leaving out a grain filler changes the texture toward a looser sausage, which is an honest home choice. The braai smoke is shared with the rest of the South African fires in this set.

How it is made

Mix a fresh beef and pork sausage with coriander and vinegar, form a coil or logs, and grill until the center is cooked.

Authentic touch. Toast the coriander in a dry pan before you grind or mix it. Grill the coil over moderate coals so it does not split and dump its fat.

How it is used

Sliced, with pap or a salad. Mustard on the side is optional and is not in the sausage.

Field notes

  • Boerewors means farmer's sausage, and it is a standard sausage on a South African braai.
  • This home mix leaves out the grain filler many shop sausages contain.

Ingredients

  • 1 lb ground beef
  • 1 lb ground pork
  • 2 tablespoons coriander seeds, toasted and ground
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/4 teaspoon ground cloves

Steps

  1. Mix the meats, coriander, vinegar, salt, pepper, and cloves. Form a long coil or six fat logs. Chill 20 minutes. There is no grain filler in this mix.
  2. Heat a braai or charcoal grill to medium.
  3. Grill, turning often, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C). Fresh sausage is ground meat.
  4. Rest a few minutes and slice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • In natural casings if you have them, still cooked to the same temperature.
  • A higher share of beef, which is traditional in many recipes, as long as there is enough fat.

History

Karoo and other ranching districts built a reputation on lamb, and the chop is the quick piece: salted and grilled, not marinated for days. Beaufort West stands in for that dry interior. The meat is a whole cut and is rested after it reaches temperature.

Cultural background

South African braai cooks put tjops on the grid next to sausage and salad. It is lamb, plainly seasoned. It is not sosaties and not a mutton stew.

Why it tastes this way

A hot grid crisps the fat cap and leaves the eye of the chop juicy if you do not cook it to shreds. Salt and wood or charcoal smoke are the seasoning. Karoo and similar grazing have a reputation for a clean lamb flavor, which a thick sauce would cover. The braai is social, and the chops are one of the faster items on it.

How it is made

Salt lamb chops and grill them over a hot braai, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Salt the chops and leave them. Turn once. A flare-up from the fat should be moved, not pressed out.

How it is used

With a green salad or pap. Lemon is optional.

Field notes

Cape Malay Sosaties

Lamb skewers with apricot, cumin, coriander, and a little chili.

40 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 6 · 6 skewers

History

Sosaties are Cape Town lamb skewers marinated with apricot and warm spice. Cape Malay cooking was built by enslaved and exiled people from the Indian Ocean world who were brought to the Cape under the Dutch East India Company, including people from Southeast Asia and South Asia, and by their descendants. They were not one people with one recipe, and this skewer should not be told as a single stereotype. The name is widely connected to sate. The meat here is lamb, a whole-muscle cut.

Cultural background

Cape Town families who keep Cape Malay foodways grill sosaties for gatherings and for the table at home. The history is forced movement and skill under the Dutch East India Company, not a costume. It is not a generic curry and not Nigerian suya.

Why it tastes this way

Apricot jam sweetens the edge of the lamb, and cumin, coriander, and chili toast on the grill. That sweet-spice balance is a Cape kitchen pattern built through Indian Ocean trade and through the cooking of people who were enslaved and exiled at the Cape. Charcoal browns the cubes. The fruit and the spice are doing different jobs, which is why the skewer is not just hot and not just sweet.

How it is made

Marinate lamb in apricot and spice, skewer it with onion, and grill until the cubes are safe, then rest.

Authentic touch. Thread onion between the lamb cubes. Wipe off thick marinade so the apricot does not burn black before the meat is done.

How it is used

With rice and a salad. Yellow rice is a common Cape side and is not required for the meat to be finished.

Field notes

  • Sosaties are a Cape Town skewer, and the name is widely connected to sate.
  • Cape Malay cooking comes from enslaved and exiled people of the Indian Ocean world at the Cape, including people from Southeast Asia and South Asia, not from one stereotype.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb boneless lamb, cut in 1 1/2 inch cubes
  • 2 onions, cut in chunks
  • 3 tablespoons apricot jam
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 2 tablespoons vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 6 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the jam, cumin, coriander, chili, vinegar, and salt. Toss with the lamb and one chopped onion. Refrigerate for a few hours if you can.
  2. Thread lamb and onion chunks on skewers. Heat a braai to medium-hot.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest cube reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Give the meat a 3-minute rest.
  4. Serve with rice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Variations

  • A firmer apricot jam or a few soaked dried apricots chopped into the marinade.
  • Beef cubes with the same marinade, still a whole-muscle cut.

Shisa Nyama

Beef and pork chops with salt, char, and the smoke of a shared fire.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Shisa nyama is a township braai. The Zulu name means burn the meat. In practice it is a social fire in Black South African townships, including places such as Soweto, where people buy meat, grill it, and eat together. It is not a costume and not a theme party. This recipe uses beef steaks and pork chops, both whole cuts, each cooked to temperature. Pap and chakalaka often share the table and have their own pages.

Cultural background

The cooks are township butchers, grillers, and the people who come to eat. The fire is social. It should be described as food and company, not as dress-up.

Why it tastes this way

Meat over an open coal fire tastes of fat, salt, and smoke, and the point of shisa nyama is that taste in company. Beef and pork on the same grid give two fats and two textures. The name's plainness, burn the meat, matches the method: a hot fire, not a sweet glaze. The setting is the township braai, which is a real social institution.

How it is made

Grill beef steaks and pork chops over coals, and pull each when it reaches a safe temperature and has rested.

Authentic touch. Salt the meat and tend your own grid. Do not turn the meal into a costume. Check beef and pork separately.

How it is used

With pap, chakalaka, or a simple salad, eaten at the fire with other people.

Field notes

  • Shisa nyama is Zulu for burn the meat.
  • It is a social fire in Black South African townships, not a costume.

Ingredients

  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. Oil the steaks and chops and season them with salt and pepper.
  2. Grill over a hot open coal fire, turning as needed. Keep beef and pork identifiable so you can temp them.
  3. Cook the beef and the pork chops, both whole cuts, to at least 145°F (63°C) and give them a 3-minute rest.
  4. Slice and serve at the fire. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Chicken Potjiekos

Chicken and vegetables simmered in a covered pot over coals, lightly spiced.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Potjiekos is food cooked in a three-legged cast-iron pot set over coals. The pot style came with European settlers and became a South African camp, farm, and backyard dish. It is not a grill. The lid stays on, and the pot simmers. This version is a chicken and vegetable pot. The chicken still has to reach a poultry temperature. You do not stir it like a stew on the stove.

Cultural background

South African camp cooks and households make potjie when there is time to leave a pot alone. It is a covered coal cook, not a chop on a grid. The chicken and vegetables are layered.

Why it tastes this way

Steam trapped in iron softens onions, potatoes, and chicken together, and a little smoke leaks in from the coals under the pot. Paprika and garlic season the juice. Because you do not grill the meat in the open, the flavor is rounded and soupy rather than charred. That is the point of using a pot instead of a grid.

How it is made

Brown chicken, layer it with vegetables in a cast-iron pot, and simmer over coals with the lid on. This is not a grill.

Authentic touch. Layer the ingredients and do not stir. Check the chicken temperature without turning the pot into a boil.

How it is used

From the pot, with rice or pap. The juices are the sauce.

Field notes

  • Potjiekos is cooked in a cast-iron pot over coals, and it is not a grill.
  • The lid stays on so the pot simmers instead of roasting in the open.

Ingredients

  • 6 chicken thighs, bone in
  • 2 onions, sliced
  • 4 potatoes, chunked
  • 3 carrots, chunked
  • 4 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 2 tomatoes, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil
  • 1 cup water

Steps

  1. Brown the chicken in oil in the pot. Take it out. Soften the onion in the same pot. This cook is a pot over coals, not a grill.
  2. Layer potatoes, carrots, garlic, tomatoes, paprika, salt, and the chicken. Add the water. Cover.
  3. Set the pot over a small bed of coals and cook gently until the thickest thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), not touching bone, and the vegetables are tender.
  4. Rest 10 minutes. Do not stir it into mush. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lamb neck in place of chicken, cooked as a whole muscle to at least 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest, and longer if you want it tender.
  • A Dutch oven on a grate if you do not own a three-legged potjie.

Oily snoek with apricot jam, a little chili, and braai char.

History

Snoek is an oily fish braaied on the Cape west coast, often split open and painted with a little apricot and chili near the end. Lambert's Bay is one of the harbors tied to the catch. If snoek is unavailable, a firm oily fish is a different dish. This recipe is written for snoek. The fish is cooked until it flakes. Apricot on snoek is a Cape habit, not a dessert glaze.

Cultural background

West-coast and Cape Town braai cooks grill snoek when the boats land it. A mackerel cooked the same way should be called mackerel. It is not the fish this page is naming.

Why it tastes this way

Snoek is rich and firm, so a hot grid chars the skin while the flesh stays moist. A thin apricot and chili brush sweetens the oil without burying it. Sea fish and a beach or harbor fire are the setting. Substituting another species changes the fat and the flake, which is why the name should change with the fish.

How it is made

Season split snoek, grill it, and brush it lightly with apricot and chili at the end.

Authentic touch. Grill snoek skin-side carefully and add the apricot late so the sugar does not blacken. If you cannot buy snoek, cook a different oily fish and do not call it snoek.

How it is used

With a salad. Sweet potato is a common Cape side.

Field notes

  • Snoek is an oily fish braaied on the Cape coast, often with a little apricot.
  • If snoek is unavailable, a firm oily fish is a different dish.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb snoek, split open
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 2 tablespoons apricot jam
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon vinegar

Steps

  1. Pat the snoek dry. Oil and salt it. Mix jam, chili, and vinegar for a late brush. This recipe is for snoek.
  2. Grill skin-side down over a medium-hot braai, then turn.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh flakes. Brush with the apricot mix in the last minute.
  4. Serve hot. A different oily fish would be a different dish. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Salt goat pieces and grill them over charcoal until they reach a safe temperature, rest them, and chop if you want the usual plate.

South African households make pap for the braai and for ordinary meals. It is vegetarian food as written. It is not a sweet breakfast porridge unless you change it on purpose.

Why it tastes this way

Maize meal swells in salted water into a mild, toasty starch that carries chakalaka, gravy, or juices from the grill. The stiffness is a choice: loose pap is a different texture. The grain is a southern African staple with a long history in the region, and the braai made the stiff version a cookout side. Oil at the end keeps it from drying into crumbs.

How it is made

Boil salted water, stir in maize meal, and cook until the porridge is thick and the raw taste is gone.

Authentic touch. Add the meal in a stream while stirring so it does not lump, then let it steam covered. Stir stiff pap with a strong spoon.

How it is used

Beside the grill or with chakalaka. It is a side.

  • 4 cups water
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil

South African Chakalaka

Tomato, pepper, onion, and beans with chili and a little curry-style spice.

20 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Chakalaka is a vegetable relish served with pap at a South African braai. A single inventor is not established, so this page does not name one. Township kitchens and later canned versions spread it. This pot uses onion, pepper, tomato, chili, and beans that are already boiled if they began dried. It is a side. The spice is cumin, paprika, and chili.

Cultural background

South African cooks make chakalaka to sit next to pap. It is vegetables and beans. It is not a lamb curry. The history does not need a fake founder.

Why it tastes this way

Tomato and onion cook down into a relish, beans make it filling, and chili keeps it lively next to mild pap. The spices are warm rather than a long list of secret blends. It was built as food that stretches a meal, which is why it belongs at a braai where the grill is the center and the relish feeds the table.

How it is made

Soften onion and pepper, add tomato, spice, and cooked beans, and simmer into a relish.

Authentic touch. Cook the vegetables until the tomato breaks down. If you start with dried beans, boil them until they are completely tender before they join the pot.

How it is used

Warm or at room temperature, beside pap. The relish itself is the recipe.

Field notes

  • Chakalaka is a vegetable relish served with pap at a South African braai.
  • A single inventor is not established, so this page does not name one.

Ingredients

  • 2 onions, chopped
  • 2 bell peppers, chopped
  • 4 tomatoes, chopped
  • 2 cups cooked beans, boiled until tender if they started dried
  • 2 teaspoons paprika
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons oil

Steps

  1. If the beans are dried, boil them until completely tender before using them. Kidney beans must be boiled hard, not held warm from raw.
  2. Soften the onion and peppers in oil. Add paprika, cumin, chili, tomatoes, and salt.
  3. Stir in the cooked beans and simmer until the relish is thick. There is no animal-protein thermometer target.
  4. Taste for salt. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

  • Grated carrot in the pot, which many cooks add.
  • A hotter chili for a sharper relish.

Roosterkoek

Grilled bread dough with a browned crust and a soft interior.

1 hr 20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · 8 rolls

History

Roosterkoek is bread dough cooked on a braai grid in South Africa. The Afrikaans name means grill cake. It is a side at cookouts, torn and eaten beside the fire. The dough is wheat, yeast, water, salt, and oil. It is vegetarian. It is not a vetkoek fried in deep fat, which is a different bread.

Cultural background

South African braai cooks bake these on the grid while the coals are still useful. The food is bread. It is not the chop and not a sweet cake, despite the word koek.

Why it tastes this way

Dough on a grid browns where it touches the metal and stays soft inside if the coals are moderate. A little smoke clings to the crust. Wheat and salt are the taste, which is why it works next to a salty grill and a sharp relish. It is camp bread: made because the fire is already going.

How it is made

Make a simple yeast dough, shape rounds, and grill them on a braai grid until they are cooked through.

Authentic touch. Cook over coals, not flames, and turn the cakes when they have a proper crust. Split one to be sure the center is not doughy.

How it is used

Hot, torn open, beside the braai. A dairy spread is not in this dough.

Field notes

  • Roosterkoek is bread dough cooked on a South African braai grid.
  • The Afrikaans name means grill cake, and the bread is not the same as deep-fried vetkoek.

Ingredients

  • 4 cups wheat flour
  • 1 1/2 cups water
  • 1 teaspoon instant yeast
  • 1 1/2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 1 teaspoon sugar

Steps

  1. Mix the flour, yeast, salt, sugar, oil, and water into a dough. Knead until smooth. Cover and let it rise until puffy, about an hour.
  2. Divide into eight rounds. Let them rest 15 minutes. Heat a braai to moderate coals.
  3. Grill the rounds, turning, until both sides are browned and the center is cooked through, not raw dough. There is no animal-protein thermometer target.
  4. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little crushed garlic on the hot bread after grilling.
  • Smaller rounds if the grid is crowded.
