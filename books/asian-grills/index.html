Thai Gai Yang

Grilled chicken with garlic, coriander root, white pepper, and fish sauce.

30 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Gai yang is grilled chicken from Isan, in northeastern Thailand, usually a bird opened flat and cooked over charcoal. Coriander root, garlic, and white pepper are pounded into the marinade, and fish sauce provides the salt. The chicken is often eaten with a chili dipping sauce. Across the Mekong, Lao ping kai is a related grill; this page is the Thai bird.

Cultural background

Isan cooks and Bangkok shops that learned from them grill this chicken. Fish sauce means the dish contains fish as well as chicken. There is no dairy and no wheat in the marinade. The chili sauce can sit beside it.

Why it tastes this way

Coriander root is earthier than the leaf, and with garlic and white pepper it makes a fragrant paste that chars on the skin. Charcoal dries the surface while the thigh cooks through. Fish sauce salts the meat more deeply than a sprinkle of salt at the end. The sour chili dip, if you serve one, is there to cut the fat.

How it is made

Marinate a butterflied chicken with a coriander-root paste and grill it over charcoal until the thigh is done.

Authentic touch. Pound the coriander root and garlic. Chopped leaf coriander is a different flavor and will burn.

How it is used

Chop it into pieces and eat it with sticky rice and a chili dip such as nam jim jaew.

Field notes

  • Gai yang is the grilled chicken of Isan, in northeastern Thailand.
  • Coriander root, garlic, and white pepper are the usual pounded marinade.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 3.5 pounds, backbone removed
  • 4 cilantro roots, or 1/2 cup cilantro stems, chopped
  • 6 garlic cloves, peeled
  • 1 teaspoon white pepper
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Pound the cilantro roots or stems, garlic, and white pepper to a paste. Stir in fish sauce, oil, sugar, and salt. Rub the chicken and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The chicken is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Heat a charcoal grill for medium heat, with a cooler side if the skin starts to darken too fast. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Grill, turning, until the thickest part of the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone. Do not serve it rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest 5 minutes, then chop it. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter dipping sauce is how the table raises the heat. The chicken marinade here is mild.
  • Thigh pieces cook faster than a whole bird and still need the same poultry temperature.

Thai Moo Ping

Sweet, salty pork skewers with garlic, white pepper, and a little coconut milk.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Moo ping is a Thai street skewer of pork, sold from small grills in Bangkok and other cities. The marinade is sweet and salty, with garlic, white pepper, fish sauce or salt, sugar, and often a spoon of coconut milk so the edges brown. The pork is cut in thin pieces, which makes it a whole-muscle skewer rather than a sausage. It is everyday street food, cooked to order.

Cultural background

Street cooks thread the pork and grill it in front of you. Coconut milk is not a dairy milk. Fish sauce, if used, contains fish. This recipe uses fish sauce. There is no wheat and no peanut in the marinade.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar and coconut milk caramelize on a hot grate, and fish sauce salts the pork. White pepper and garlic stay sharp under that sweetness. Thin slices cook before the sugar turns black, which is why the skewers are small.

How it is made

Marinate thin pork in coconut milk, sugar, garlic, and fish sauce, then grill it on skewers.

Authentic touch. Keep the pieces thin and do not let the sugar flame up. A black skewer tastes bitter.

How it is used

Eat the skewers hot, with sticky rice or a sour chili dip.

Field notes

  • Moo ping is a sweet pork skewer sold from small street grills in Thailand.
  • Coconut milk helps the edges brown. It is not dairy.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds pork shoulder, sliced thin
  • 1/4 cup coconut milk
  • 1 tablespoon palm sugar
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon white pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the coconut milk, palm sugar, fish sauce, garlic, white pepper, and oil. Coat the pork and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the slices on small skewers. Heat a grill until it is hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning often, until the center of the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Palm sugar and white sugar are both used. This recipe uses palm sugar.
  • A chili dip on the side adds heat. The skewer itself is mild.

Thai Kor Moo Yang

Pork neck with garlic, fish sauce, and white pepper, sliced after a rest.

25 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Kor moo yang is grilled pork neck, a cut with fat and lean in the same slice. Thai cooks marinate it with garlic, white pepper, and fish sauce, grill it over charcoal, and slice it against the grain. It is a common plate in Chiang Mai and across the country, often with a spicy dip. The neck is whole muscle, not ground pork.

Cultural background

Grill shops cook the neck in a larger piece than moo ping and slice it at the board. Fish sauce means fish is an allergen along with pork. There is no wheat and no dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Pork neck stays moist because of the fat running through it, and a hot grill browns that fat. Garlic and white pepper are the aroma, and fish sauce is the salt. Slicing it thin lets the dip season each piece. A lean loin would dry out on the same fire.

How it is made

Marinate a pork neck in garlic, fish sauce, and white pepper, grill it, rest it, and slice it.

Authentic touch. Rest the neck before you slice it. Cutting it on the grill dumps the juice onto the coals.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with sticky rice, herbs, and a chili dip.

Field notes

  • Kor moo is pork neck, a cut with fat through the lean meat.
  • The neck is grilled in a piece and sliced, not cubed onto small skewers.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless pork neck
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 teaspoon white pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Rub the pork with garlic, fish sauce, white pepper, oil, sugar, and salt. Refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a charcoal grill to medium-high, with room to move the meat if the fat flares. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then slice it. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A shoulder steak can stand in if neck is not sold. Keep it a whole cut.
  • The dipping sauce can be very hot while the meat stays mild.

Thai Pla Pao

A whole fish steamed inside a salt crust, with moist flesh and a clean taste.

20 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Pla pao is a Thai way of grilling a whole fish inside a jacket of salt, often set near an open fire. The salt crust keeps the flesh moist and seasons it. River fish and tilapia are both used. The crust is cracked open at the table; it is not a chili curry and not a fried fish; the only heat is the fire, not chili.

Cultural background

Riverside and home cooks in Thailand pack the fish in salt and give it time by the fire. There is fish and salt, and no chili in the crust. Egg white is sometimes used to bind a salt dough. This recipe uses egg white, so it contains egg.

Why it tastes this way

The salt crust blocks direct flame, so the fish steams in its own moisture instead of drying. A little salt reaches the flesh. Herbs in the cavity perfume it without a sauce. When the crust is cracked, the fish tastes clean, which is the point of the method.

How it is made

Stuff a whole fish, pack it in a salt and egg-white crust, and cook it beside a fire or in a hot oven until the flesh flakes.

Authentic touch. Make the crust thick enough that you cannot see the skin. Thin salt burns and does not protect the fish.

How it is used

Crack off the salt crust and eat the fish with lime and a chili sauce on the side if you want heat.

Field notes

  • Pla pao cooks the fish inside a salt crust so the flesh steams.
  • The crust is broken away. It is not a sauce you eat by the spoon.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole fish, about 2 pounds, cleaned and scaled
  • 4 cups coarse salt
  • 2 egg whites
  • 2 stalks lemongrass, crushed
  • 4 lime leaves, torn
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Fill the cavity with lemongrass and lime leaves. Stir the salt, egg whites, and water into a packable crust. The fish is still raw. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  2. Pack the crust all over the fish in a thick layer. Heat an open fire with a grill rack to the side, or heat an oven to 400°F (200°C) and call it an oven. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  3. Cook until the center of the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque. Crack a little crust to check, then close it if it needs more time. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Rest it briefly, crack away the crust, and serve the fish. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hot oven can finish the crust if you do not have an open fire. Say that it is an oven.
  • Lemongrass in the cavity is a common herb. Chili is for the table, not the crust.

Nam Jim Jaew

Hot, sour, and salty, with toasted rice, chili, lime, and fish sauce.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Nam jim jaew is the Isan dipping sauce served with grilled chicken and grilled pork. Toasted sticky-rice powder thickens it, dried chili provides the heat, and lime and fish sauce make it sour and salty. Shallot is usual. It is a table sauce, not a cooked glaze; fish sauce means it contains fish, so it is not a vegetarian condiment even though there is no piece of meat in the bowl.

Cultural background

Isan cooks set this bowl beside gai yang and pork. The rice is toasted, not boiled into porridge. There is no dairy and no wheat if you toast rice, not barley. Keep it cold, and do not treat it as a preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Toasted rice smells nutty and gives the sauce body so it clings to grilled meat. Lime cuts fat, dried chili brings heat, and fish sauce is the salt of the Mekong table. Shallot adds a sharp fresh bite. Without the toasted rice, the same liquids would be a thinner nam jim.

How it is made

Toast rice and grind it, then stir it with chili, lime, fish sauce, and shallot.

Authentic touch. Toast the rice until it smells popped, then grind it. Raw rice flour will taste raw and dusty.

How it is used

Spoon it over grilled chicken or pork, or leave it in a bowl for dipping.

Field notes

  • Toasted rice powder is what makes jaew different from a thin chili fish sauce.
  • Fish sauce is in the bowl, so the sauce is not vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 2 tablespoons uncooked sticky rice or jasmine rice
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 tablespoon crushed dried hot chili
  • 1 shallot, thinly sliced
  • 1 teaspoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon chopped cilantro

Steps

  1. Toast the rice in a dry pan until it is golden and smells nutty. Grind it to a coarse powder. There is no meat to cook in this sauce.
  2. Stir the rice powder with fish sauce, lime juice, chili, shallot, and sugar.
  3. Taste for salt and sour. Add cilantro.
  4. Serve the same day. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe. Do not leave it at room temperature.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • More dried chili makes it extra-hot. This cup is hot.
  • A little extra lime is the right fix if the fish sauce is very salty.

Hanoi Bun Cha

Grilled pork patties and pork belly with rice noodles, herbs, and a lime-fish sauce bowl.

35 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Bun cha is a Hanoi lunch: grilled pork, rice noodles, herbs, and a sweet-sour bowl of fish sauce, lime, and sugar. The pork is often both a minced patty and a slice of belly. Rice noodles do not contain wheat, so the written dish is gluten-free if you do not add a wheat sauce. It is street and shop food in Hanoi, eaten at midday more than as a court recipe.

Cultural background

Hanoi cooks grill the pork over charcoal and set noodles and herbs on the table. The patties are ground pork. The belly is a whole cut. Fish sauce contains fish. There is no wheat in the noodles or the marinade.

Why it tastes this way

Charcoal sweetens the edges of the pork, and the dipping bowl is sour, salty, and a little sweet, which cuts the fat of the belly. Rice noodles stay mild so the grill and the lime show. Herbs add freshness. Wheat noodles would make a different bowl.

How it is made

Season ground pork and pork belly, grill both to their own temperatures, and serve them with rice noodles and a lime-fish sauce bowl.

Authentic touch. Grill the patties over real heat so they brown. If they only poach in a pan of sauce, they are not bun cha.

How it is used

Put noodles in a bowl, add herbs and the warm pork, and spoon on the dipping sauce.

Field notes

  • Bun cha is a Hanoi plate of grilled pork and rice noodles.
  • Rice noodles contain no wheat when they are made only from rice.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound ground pork
  • 1 pound pork belly, sliced 1/4 inch thick
  • 3 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 8 ounces dry rice noodles
  • 1 small head of lettuce, leaves separated

Steps

  1. Mix the ground pork with 1 tablespoon fish sauce, half the garlic, and the pepper. Shape thin patties. Rub the belly with the remaining garlic and 1 tablespoon fish sauce. Refrigerate both. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  2. Stir the remaining fish sauce with lime juice, sugar, and 1/4 cup water for the bowl. Boil the rice noodles, drain them, and rinse them cool. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the patties and the belly over a hot fire. The center of each patty must reach 160°F (71°C). The belly must reach at least 145°F (63°C). Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the belly 3 minutes. Serve pork, noodles, and lettuce with the sauce. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook the ground pork to 160°F (71°C). Cook the pork belly, a whole cut, to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest it 3 minutes.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some shops serve only the patties. This page includes belly as well.
  • Extra chili in the bowl raises the heat. The written bowl is mild.

Vietnamese Nem Nuong

Sweet grilled pork mince on skewers, with garlic and a little sugar.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Nem nướng is grilled ground pork, shaped on skewers or as small logs, and it is especially associated with south-central cities such as Nha Trang. The mince is slightly sweet, with garlic and fish sauce, and it is wrapped at the table with herbs and rice paper. It is street and feast food, not a dried cured sausage. Ground pork has to be cooked through.

Cultural background

Cooks in Nha Trang and other southern kitchens grill this mince to order. Fish sauce contains fish. There is no wheat in the meat. Rice paper at the table is a wrapper, not a wheat tortilla, when it is made from rice.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar browns on the skewer and keeps the pork from tasting plain. Fish sauce and garlic season the mince all the way through, unlike a sauce poured on at the end. Herbs and rice paper make each bite fresh against that sweet grill.

How it is made

Season ground pork, shape it on skewers, and grill it until the center reaches the temperature for ground meat.

Authentic touch. Keep the logs small. A thick roll of ground pork browns outside and stays under temperature in the center.

How it is used

Wrap pieces in rice paper with herbs and lettuce, and dip them in a fish-sauce dip.

Field notes

  • Nem nướng is grilled ground pork, strongly associated with Nha Trang.
  • It is cooked fresh. It is not a cured sausage.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds ground pork
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the pork, fish sauce, sugar, garlic, pepper, oil, and salt. Refrigerate the mince. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. It is still raw. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  2. Shape the mince around flat skewers in small logs. Heat a grill. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of each log reaches 160°F (71°C). Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A spoon of toasted rice powder in the dip adds the same nutty note used in other Vietnamese grills.
  • Honey would sweeten it and would add honey. This recipe uses sugar.

Vietnamese Suon Nuong

Pork chops with lemongrass, garlic, sugar, and fish sauce, charred at the edge.

25 min prep · 16 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Sườn nướng is Vietnamese grilled pork chop or pork rib meat, marinated with lemongrass, garlic, sugar, and fish sauce. It is a home and cơm tấm plate, the broken-rice meal of southern Vietnam, and it is also grilled in home kitchens farther north. The chop is a whole cut, cooked and rested. It is not the ground pork of nem nướng.

Cultural background

Home cooks and rice-plate shops grill these chops over charcoal. Fish sauce contains fish. Lemongrass and sugar are the aroma. There is no wheat and no dairy in the marinade.

Why it tastes this way

Lemongrass oil chars lightly and perfumes the fat, while sugar helps the surface brown. Fish sauce salts the meat. A hot grill does this faster than a covered bake, which is why the chop tastes like a grill and not like a braise.

How it is made

Marinate pork chops with lemongrass, garlic, sugar, and fish sauce, then grill them and rest them.

Authentic touch. Pound the lemongrass before it goes into the marinade. Whole stalks on the side will not season the pork.

How it is used

Slice the chops and eat them with rice, cucumber, and a fish-sauce dip.

Field notes

  • Sườn nướng is a grilled pork chop, a usual part of a southern Vietnamese rice plate.
  • Lemongrass, sugar, and fish sauce are the marinade.

Ingredients

  • 4 bone-in pork chops, about 3/4 inch thick
  • 2 stalks lemongrass, tender parts minced
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Stir the lemongrass, garlic, fish sauce, sugar, oil, and pepper. Coat the chops and refrigerate them. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill until it is hot. Oil the grate lightly. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning once, until the center of each chop reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the chops 3 minutes. Slice and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thin rib pieces are used in some kitchens. This page uses chops, which are a whole cut.
  • A spoon of honey would add honey. Sugar is the sweetener here.

Lao Sai Oua

Grilled pork sausage heavy with lemongrass, galangal, lime leaf, and chili.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Sai oua is a Lao sausage of ground pork mixed with lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaf, shallot, and chili, then grilled. It is a fresh sausage, cooked the day it is mixed, not a cured or dried link. Luang Prabang and other Lao towns sell it beside sticky rice. Ground pork has to reach a ground-meat temperature; this recipe forms the meat into logs without a curing salt.

Cultural background

Feast cooks in the Philippines make a pot of sarsa while the pig or the shoulder roasts. There is chicken liver and wheat. There is no dairy and no peanut. Cook the liver through. Do not serve it pink.

Why it tastes this way

Liver gives the sauce a deep savory taste, and vinegar keeps it from feeling heavy on fatty pork. Sugar rounds the vinegar, and bread crumbs thicken the pot so it sits on the meat. Garlic is the aroma. Without the vinegar it would taste muddy. The acid is why it belongs with lechon.

How it is made

Cook chicken liver to 165°F (74°C) with garlic and vinegar, then blend it with bread crumbs into a sauce.

Authentic touch. Brown the liver first so the sauce tastes roasted, then add the vinegar. Boiling raw liver in vinegar from the start tastes harsh.

How it is used

Spoon it over chopped roast pork, or serve it in a bowl beside the meat.

Field notes

  • Lechon sarsa is a liver sauce for Filipino roast pork.
  • Chicken liver in this pot is cooked to 165°F (74°C).

Ingredients

  • 1/2 pound chicken livers, trimmed
  • 3 tablespoons vinegar
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 small onion, chopped
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1/2 cup wheat bread crumbs
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 cup water

Steps

  1. Trim the livers. They are still raw. Heat the oil and cook the onion and garlic for a minute. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  2. Add the livers and cook them until the center of the thickest piece reaches 165°F (74°C). Do not serve the liver pink. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  3. Add vinegar, sugar, salt, bread crumbs, and water. Simmer 5 minutes. Blend until mostly smooth, and check that the sauce is hot and the liver was already at 165°F (74°C). Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).
  4. Serve warm with pork. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe. Cook the liver to 165°F (74°C).

Toyomansi Dip

Salty soy cut with sour calamansi, with garlic and black pepper.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 3/4 cup

History

Toyomansi is a Filipino dipping sauce of toyo, soy sauce, and kalamansi, the small citrus squeezed over grilled pork, chicken, and fish. It is a table mix, stirred when the grill is going, not a cooked glaze. Ordinary soy sauce contains soy and wheat. This bowl has no meat and no chili, so the heat stays at none; chili can be added by the person eating.

Cultural background

Filipino tables set this dip beside inihaw. There is soy and wheat, and no meat, dairy, egg, or peanut. It is vegetarian. Use it the day you mix it, and refrigerate it. It is not a preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Soy is salty and dark, and calamansi is sharply sour, so together they cut grilled fat the way a heavy gravy would not. Garlic and black pepper make it more than salted citrus. On a Manila grill table it is the spoonful that finishes liempo, chicken, or fish without covering the charcoal taste.

How it is made

Stir soy sauce, calamansi juice, garlic, and black pepper.

Authentic touch. Squeeze the calamansi at the last hour. The juice dulls if the dip sits all day in a warm kitchen.

How it is used

Put it in a small bowl and let people spoon it over grilled meat or fish.

Field notes

  • Toyomansi is soy sauce and calamansi, a standard Filipino grill dip.
  • Ordinary soy sauce contains wheat.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup soy sauce
  • 1/4 cup calamansi juice, or lime juice
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Stir the soy sauce, calamansi or lime juice, garlic, pepper, and water. There is no meat in the dip.
  2. Taste it. If the soy is very salty, add another spoon of citrus, not more salt.
  3. Refrigerate it until the grill is ready. Do not leave garlic sitting out in the sauce for hours.
  4. Serve cool, the same day. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Sliced chili makes it hot. The written dip has none.
  • Lime juice can stand in when calamansi is not sold. The dip will be a little less fragrant.
