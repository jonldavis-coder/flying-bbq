Asado & Churrasco

Parrillas, spits, and Saturday fires from the pampas to Brazil.

Asado and churrasco are related words and different tables. Argentina and Uruguay cook on a parrilla or a cross of wood. Brazil salts a picanha and turns it on a skewer.

This book keeps the southern-cone and Brazilian fires that are actually in the passport. It is a route, not a claim that one capital invented the grill.

Argentine Asado

Salted beef cooked over wood coals, with chimichurri as a table sauce.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

An Argentine asado is a mixed grill on a parrilla, wood burned down to coals, with salt as the seasoning. Cattle ranching on the pampas made beef the center of the meal. Vacío, a flank cut, and tira de asado, cross-cut beef short ribs, are two cuts the fire is built to hold, and each has its own page here. Chimichurri is the green sauce beside the meat, not a glaze cooked onto it. Indigenous nations of the plains had their own fires; this parrilla is the later criollo cattle cook.

Cultural background

This page is the mixed fire, not a single steak and not a Uruguayan cross. The meat is whole beef. Chimichurri is a pairing, with its own recipe in this file, so it is not repeated as an ingredient here.

Why it tastes this way

Hard woods of the region burn to a steady coal, and the beef cooks in dry heat with little more than salt. Fat bastes the cut as it sits. The flavor is beef and wood, and the sharp parsley sauce is there to cut it afterward. A rushed gas grill without coals will brown the meat and miss the wood.

How it is made

Grill vacío and tira de asado over wood coals with coarse salt, to a whole-beef temperature, and serve chimichurri beside them.

Authentic touch. Salt early, then leave the meat alone. Moving it every minute tears the crust the coals are trying to make.

How it is used

Eat the cuts as they are ready, with chimichurri, bread, and a simple salad. Bread is a pairing and adds wheat.

Field notes

  • Vacío and tira de asado are two cuts a parrilla is built to cook, and each has its own page.
  • Chimichurri is a table sauce. The meat on this fire is salted, not buried in the sauce.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef vacío or flank
  • 2 pounds tira de asado, beef short ribs cut across the bone
  • 2 tablespoons coarse salt
  • Wood such as a hard regional firewood, burned to coals

Steps

  1. Salt both cuts. They are still raw and are not ready to eat. Let the salt sit while the wood burns to coals. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Spread the coals under a grill grate. This is a parrilla fire, not a closed smoker. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the thickest part of each cut reads 145°F (63°C). Rest the meat 3 minutes. The rib may stay on longer if you want it looser, which will be past 145°F (63°C).
  4. Slice the rested beef. Both cuts should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Serve chimichurri on the side. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some parrillas serve beef pinker than this guidance. This recipe uses 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest.
  • A kettle grill with wood chunks is a home fire. Say so if it is not a parrilla grate over coals.

Argentine Vacío

Flank grilled over coals, salted, sliced thin across the grain.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Vacío is a flank cut on the Argentine parrilla, thinner than a roast and quicker than a rack of ribs. It is its own page because the cut cooks differently from tira de asado and from a mixed grill. The seasoning is coarse salt. Chimichurri stays on the side. Mataderos in Buenos Aires is one neighborhood where Sunday fires still draw a crowd.

Cultural background

This is flank, whole muscle, not short ribs and not a sausage. Slice it across the grain after the rest. It is still an asado cut, cooked on the same kind of wood fire.

Why it tastes this way

Flank is beefy and a little loose, so it picks up coal smoke quickly and stays interesting when sliced thin. Salt is enough because the cut has its own flavor. A long sauce cook would hide the thing the parrilla is trying to show.

How it is made

Salt a flank and grill it over coals to a whole-beef temperature, then slice it across the grain.

Authentic touch. Grill it in one piece and slice after the rest. Precut strips dry out before the center is done.

How it is used

Serve the slices with chimichurri and bread. The bread is a pairing.

Field notes

  • Vacío is a flank cut, thin enough to grill more quickly than a thick rib.
  • It is not short ribs and not a ground sausage.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef flank, in one piece
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Wood or charcoal burned to coals
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Salt the flank. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Grill it over coals, turning when the first side is browned. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested flank across the grain. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A labeled flank steak from another butcher chart is the right home cut if vacío is not the name in the case.
  • Some cooks like it pinker than 145°F (63°C). This recipe holds the safety line and the rest.

Pairings and uses

  • bread
  • tomato salad

Argentine Tira de Asado

Cross-cut beef short ribs, salted and grilled on the bone.

15 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Tira de asado is beef short rib cut across the bones into a strip, a standard cut on an Argentine parrilla. The bones stay in. It is not vacío and not a boneless steak. Salt and coals are the method. Cooks who want the meat looser leave the strips on past the safety temperature. This page is only the ribs.

Cultural background

Ask a butcher for beef short ribs cut across the bone, about an inch thick. Pork ribs are a different animal and a different temperature conversation. These are whole beef.

Why it tastes this way

The bone and the fat between the ribs baste the meat while the coals brown the outside. Because the strip is thin, you taste char and beef in the same bite. Salt is the only seasoning, so the wood and the fat are what you notice.

How it is made

Salt cross-cut beef short ribs and grill them over coals to at least a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep the strips in one layer. Stacked ribs steam, and steamed tira is not the grill cut.

How it is used

Serve the strips with chimichurri and a tomato and onion relish.

Field notes

  • Tira de asado is beef short rib cut across the bones into strips.
  • The bones stay in. This is not a boneless steak.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds beef short ribs, cut across the bone into strips
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Wood or charcoal burned to coals
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Salt the strips. They are still raw and are not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Grill them over coals in one layer, turning as the fat browns. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest meat reads 145°F (63°C). Rest 3 minutes. If you want them looser, continue past 145°F (63°C) after that line is met.
  4. Serve the rested ribs. They should have reached at least 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leaving the ribs on longer, past 145°F (63°C), makes the meat looser. The safety line is still 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest.
  • A thicker strip needs more time. Check the meat, not the clock.

Choripán

Grilled fresh chorizo in bread, with a sharp green sauce.

15 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 sandwiches

History

Choripán is a grilled fresh chorizo split into bread, a street sandwich of the Río de la Plata asado. The sausage is ground pork, so it has to be cooked through the way other ground meat is. The bread is wheat. Chimichurri or salsa criolla goes on the crumb. It is not a dried Spanish chorizo eaten cold.

Cultural background

Buy a fresh, raw chorizo meant for the grill, often called chorizo parrillero. A fully cooked smoked sausage is a different product. The roll is part of the dish, which is why this page includes wheat.

Why it tastes this way

Pork fat, paprika, and garlic inside the sausage drip onto the coals and flavor the crust. The bread catches that fat. A vinegar sauce cuts it. The sandwich tastes of a quick street grill, not of a long cured sausage.

How it is made

Grill fresh chorizo to a ground-meat temperature and serve it in bread.

Authentic touch. Split the sausage lengthwise once the outside is set, so the center can brown and so you can check the temperature in the middle.

How it is used

Put the split sausage in a roll with chimichurri. Eat it as a sandwich.

Field notes

  • The sausage is a fresh chorizo, so it is cooked through like other ground meat.
  • The bread is part of the dish, which is why this one is a sandwich.

Ingredients

  • 6 fresh raw pork chorizos, about 4 ounces each
  • 6 crusty wheat rolls
  • 1/2 cup chimichurri, from the chimichurri recipe or a parsley, garlic, vinegar, and oil sauce
  • A hot grill

Steps

  1. Prick the sausages once if the casings are very tight. They are raw ground pork and are not ready to eat. Ground meat is not ready to eat until it reaches 160°F (71°C).
  2. Grill them over coals or a hot grate, turning, until the center of each sausage reads 160°F (71°C). Split them to check the middle if you need to.
  3. Toast the cut rolls on the grill for a minute. The bread is wheat. Ground meat is not ready to eat until it reaches 160°F (71°C).
  4. Every sausage should have reached 160°F (71°C) before it goes in the bread. Add chimichurri. Refrigerate leftover meat within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A Uruguayan choripán is the same idea. This page is written from the Argentine street grill.
  • Salsa criolla instead of chimichurri is a common choice on the same sausage.

Pairings and uses

  • Pork
  • Sandwiches
  • Street food
  • Breads
  • chimichurri
  • salsa criolla
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Argentine Grilled Mollejas

Grilled sweetbreads with a browned crust and a mild, rich center.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Mollejas on an Argentine menu are sweetbreads, usually the thymus, grilled on the parrilla. They are offal, cooked through and served with lemon. They are a standard cut beside the steaks, not a novelty. This page treats them as food and gives a clear temperature.

Cultural background

Buy fresh sweetbreads from a butcher you trust. Soak and trim them, then grill them. They are beef offal and need at least 145°F (63°C) and a rest. They are not a sausage and not a steak.

Why it tastes this way

Sweetbreads are mild and fatty, so a hard grill gives them a crust that the inside does not have on its own. Lemon and a little salt are the usual finish. Wood coals add the same dry heat the rest of the asado uses, which keeps the plate in one fire rather than in a separate fryer.

How it is made

Simmer, trim, and grill beef sweetbreads until they reach an offal temperature.

Authentic touch. Peel the membrane after a short simmer, or the grilled outside will be papery. Dry the pieces well before they hit the grate.

How it is used

Salt them and eat them with lemon. Chimichurri is optional and is a pairing.

Field notes

  • Mollejas on an Argentine menu are sweetbreads, usually the thymus.
  • They are offal, and they are cooked to at least 145°F (63°C).

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds beef sweetbreads
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil or a brush of beef fat
  • 1 lemon, cut into wedges
  • Water and a teaspoon of salt, for the short simmer

Steps

  1. Soak the sweetbreads in cold water in the refrigerator for an hour, then simmer them in salted water for 10 minutes. They are not yet grilled and are not ready to eat. Drain, cool enough to handle, and peel the membrane. This offal is not ready to eat until it reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes.
  2. Dry the pieces, oil them, and salt them. This offal is not ready to eat until it reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes.
  3. Grill over coals until the center of the thickest piece reads at least 145°F (63°C). Rest 3 minutes.
  4. The rested sweetbreads should have reached at least 145°F (63°C). Serve with lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook beef offal to at least 145°F (63°C) with a rest. Cook poultry offal, including chicken hearts and livers, to 165°F (74°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A piece of grilled lemon is enough. A heavy spice rub is not the parrilla habit.
  • If you buy them already cleaned, you still dry them and you still take a temperature.

Pairings and uses

  • Offal
  • lemon
  • chimichurri
  • bread
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Provoleta

Grilled provolone, browned at the edge, with oregano and a little chile.

10 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Provoleta is a thick round of provolone grilled in a small metal dish on the Argentine parrilla, usually before the larger cuts are eaten. It is a side of the asado, and it is cheese, so it is vegetarian and it is not vegan. Oregano and dried chile are the usual scatter on top.

Cultural background

There is no meat in the dish. Butter is not required if the cheese has enough fat to brown. The provolone is dairy. People who avoid dairy should skip this page.

Why it tastes this way

Provolone browns where it meets the hot dish and stays soft in the center, so you get a toasted edge and a mild, salty middle. Oregano and chile are dry and sharp against the fat of the cheese. It tastes of the same fire as the beef, without being beef.

How it is made

Grill a thick piece of provolone in a small pan until it softens and the top browns.

Authentic touch. Use a thick slice, at least an inch. A thin deli slice melts through the grate and disappears.

How it is used

Eat it hot with bread. The bread is a pairing and adds wheat. The cheese itself is the side.

Field notes

  • Provoleta is a thick slice of provolone grilled on the same fire as the meat.
  • It is vegetarian, and it is not vegan, because the ingredient is cheese.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound provolone, in a single thick slice about 1 inch deep
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1/2 teaspoon dried chile flakes
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil

Steps

  1. Set the provolone in a small cast-iron skillet or provoletera. There is no meat in this dish.
  2. Drizzle the oil and scatter the oregano and chile.
  3. Grill over coals or broil until the cheese is soft throughout and the top is spotted brown. Do not walk away. It goes from melted to scorched quickly.
  4. Eat it hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. It is cheese, so it is not a vegan plate.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A drizzle of olive oil on top is common. It does not replace the cheese.
  • A home oven broiler can brown the top. It is not a parrilla, and it still melts the cheese.

Pairings and uses

  • Sides
  • bread
  • chimichurri
  • a grilled steak
  • Vegetarian
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Uruguayan Asado al Espeto

Beef roasted slowly beside wood, on a cross rather than a quick grate.

25 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Uruguayan asado al espeto is often a cross of iron or wood set beside a wood fire, and the pace is slower than a steak on a grate. The Argentine meal people picture first is often a parrilla, a grill over coals, though Argentina also uses a cross, especially for lamb in Patagonia. This page does not claim that only Uruguay cooks on an espeto. It describes the Uruguayan cross.

Cultural background

A whole side of beef is the countryside version. A large roast or a rack is the home version and should be named as a smaller cut. The seasoning is salt. The fire sits beside the meat so the cook can watch the pace.

Why it tastes this way

A side fire browns the beef more slowly than a grate directly over coals, so the outside dries a little and the inside stays even. Wood smoke is light if the flames are not licking the meat. Salt and time are the flavor, which is why this cross tastes different from a fast city steak even when the animal is the same.

How it is made

Salt a large beef roast and cook it on a spit or a cross beside wood until it reaches a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Keep flames from touching the meat. The espeto is beside the fire, and the cook turns the cross when one side has had enough.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with chimichurri or a simple salad. The sauce is a pairing.

Field notes

  • Asado al espeto is often a cross beside the fire, at a slower pace than a grate over the coals.
  • Uruguay and Argentina share asado, and both countries use more than one kind of fire.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds boneless beef roast, a home stand-in for a larger cut on the cross
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Wood for a fire beside a spit or a sturdy rotisserie
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Salt the roast. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Tie it if the shape is uneven. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Set it on a spit or a cross beside a wood fire, not in the flames. A home rotisserie should be described as a rotisserie if you have no cross. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook, turning, until the center reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested beef. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A home rotisserie is closer to an espeto than a frying pan is. Say if you are not using a cross.
  • Lamb on a cross is a Patagonian page in this file, not this beef cook.

Brazilian Picanha

Rump cap grilled with its fat cap on, seasoned only with coarse salt.

15 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Picanha is the rump cap of the beef, grilled with the fat cap left on and with coarse salt as the seasoning. It is a defining cut of Brazilian churrasco, strongly associated with the south and now cooked across the country. It is not carne de sol, the salted beef of the Northeast. Some restaurants serve it pinker than the temperature on this page. The guidance here is 145°F (63°C) and a rest.

Cultural background

The fat cap is not trimmed off. It bastes the meat. A long spice paste is not what makes the cut picanha. Steaks or a C-shaped skewer are both used. This page uses a skewer and allows a hot grill.

Why it tastes this way

The fat cap melts over the lean cap of the rump, so each slice tastes richer than a lean steak from the same animal. Coarse salt stays on the surface and crackles. Southern Brazilian churrasco is a wood or charcoal fire, and that dry heat browns the fat instead of steaming it.

How it is made

Salt the fat-on rump cap and grill it on a skewer, fat toward the heat, to a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Score the fat cap in a crosshatch, without cutting into the meat, so the salt has somewhere to sit and the fat can render.

How it is used

Slice it against the grain and eat it with farofa, a vinaigrette relish, and rice. Those sides have their own pages.

Field notes

  • The cut is the rump cap, and the fat cap stays on.
  • Coarse salt is the seasoning. A long spice paste is not what makes it picanha.

Ingredients

  • 2 1/2 pounds beef picanha, rump cap, fat cap left on
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • Long metal skewers, or a hot grill if you name it as the home version
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Score the fat cap and salt the meat. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Fold the piece onto skewers with the fat outward, or lay steaks fat side down on a hot grill and say that is the home version. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested meat across the grain. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Thick steaks on a hot grill, fat cap down first, are a home version of the skewer. Say if you are not using a skewer.
  • Carne de sol is a different Brazilian beef and not a stand-in for this cut.

Pairings and uses

  • molho a campanha
  • rice

Brazilian Grilled Linguiça

Garlicky pork sausage with a browned casing and a cooked-through center.

10 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Linguiça is a Brazilian pork sausage, usually garlic and paprika, grilled beside the other cuts at a churrasco. The sausage on this page is fresh and uncooked, so it is ground meat and needs 160°F (71°C). It is not Argentine chorizo in a roll, and it is not a dried sausage eaten cold. Southern Brazil grills it often. The same sausage also shows up far from the pampas.

Cultural background

Read the label. This recipe assumes pork, salt, garlic, and paprika, with no wheat filler. If your package lists wheat, do not call the plate gluten-free. A fully cooked smoked linguiça only needs to be heated, and it is a different product from the raw one written here.

Why it tastes this way

Garlic and paprika stain the pork fat, and the grill dries the casing until it snaps. The center stays juicy only if you pull it when it is cooked through, not when it is merely brown. Charcoal is the usual fire, and it adds a little bitterness that the sweet fat can stand.

How it is made

Grill fresh pork linguiça until the center reaches a ground-meat temperature.

Authentic touch. Turn the links often. A sausage that splits and dumps its fat onto the coals will dry out before the center is hot enough.

How it is used

Eat it with rice, farofa, and a vinaigrette relish. A roll is optional and would add wheat.

Field notes

  • Linguiça is a Brazilian pork sausage, seasoned with garlic and often paprika.
  • Fresh linguiça is ground meat and needs 160°F (71°C) even when the outside looks browned.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 pounds fresh uncooked pork linguiça, labeled pork, salt, garlic, and paprika, with no wheat
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon coarse salt, only if the links are not already very salty
  • A hot grill of charcoal or wood coals

Steps

  1. Check the label for wheat. The sausages are raw ground pork and are not ready to eat. Ground meat is not ready to eat until it reaches 160°F (71°C).

There is no meat in the recipe. Butter is dairy. The loaf is wheat, so the dish is not gluten-free. Do not leave garlic butter at room temperature.

Why it tastes this way

Butter carries raw garlic into toasted crumb, and a minute on the grill dries the edge of the bread. Parsley keeps it from tasting only of garlic. Next to a salty picanha it is rich and soft, which is why the churrascaria bothers to toast it.

How it is made

Spread garlic butter on a split loaf and heat it until the bread is hot and the edges toast.

Authentic touch. Spread the butter to the crust, then wrap the bread so the garlic steams into the crumb before the outside browns.

How it is used

Cut it in strips and eat it with the grilled meat. It is a side.

Field notes

  • Pão de alho is garlic bread cooked for the Brazilian grill.
  • The bread is wheat, and the butter makes it a dairy side.

Ingredients

  • 1 wheat baguette or small loaf, split
  • 4 tablespoons butter, soft
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley
  • 1/4 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mash the butter with garlic, parsley, and salt. There is no meat in this spread. Use it the same day. Do not leave garlic butter at room temperature.
  2. Spread it on the cut bread. The loaf is wheat.
  3. Wrap it in foil and heat it on a grill or in a 375°F (190°C) oven until the bread is hot and the edges are toasted, about 10 minutes.
  4. Open the foil for a minute if you want more color. Serve warm. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little grated cheese on top is common and is still dairy. This version has no extra cheese.
  • An oven heats it as well as a grill. Say which one you used.
