Punjabi Tandoori Chicken

Yogurt-marinated chicken with Kashmiri chili, lemon, and a charred skin.

35 min prep · 45 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tandoor bread and meat are older in the northwest of the subcontinent than any one restaurant plate. The usual restaurant history of the bright red tandoori chicken places it in twentieth-century Delhi, at Moti Mahal, with the cook Kundan Lal Gujral. A home oven can take the same yogurt marinade, and it should be named as a home oven when there is no clay tandoor.

Cultural background

This is a whole chicken opened flat, cooked by Punjabi tandoor practice and by Delhi restaurant habit. Yogurt in the marinade means dairy. It is not a vegan dish, and it is not boneless chicken tikka.

Why it tastes this way

A charcoal tandoor gives dry, fierce heat, so the yogurt and Kashmiri chili brown instead of steaming. Lemon and ginger cut the fat under the skin. In Delhi the restaurant bird became redder and more vivid than older northwest tandoor meat. A home oven still browns the marinade, without the clay draft.

How it is made

Marinate a butterflied chicken in yogurt, Kashmiri chili, ginger, and lemon, then cook it in a tandoor or a very hot home oven.

Authentic touch. Loosen the skin and rub some marinade under it. Open the bird so the thigh is not buried against another piece of raw chicken.

How it is used

Cut it into pieces and eat it with sliced onion, lemon, and a cucumber salad.

Field notes

  • Tandoor bread and meat are older in the northwest than the bright restaurant chicken.
  • The usual restaurant history names Moti Mahal in Delhi and the cook Kundan Lal Gujral.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole chicken, about 3.5 pounds, backbone removed and opened flat
  • 1 cup plain yogurt
  • 2 tablespoons Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon finely grated ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin

Steps

  1. Stir the yogurt, chili, ginger, garlic, lemon juice, oil, salt, and cumin. Coat the chicken, including under the skin. Refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The chicken is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. If you have a tandoor, heat it until the wall is very hot. If you do not, heat a home oven to 450°F (230°C) and call that oven what it is. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook the chicken until the thickest part of the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone. Do not serve it below that temperature. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest the chicken 5 minutes, then cut it into pieces. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A home oven at high heat is the honest substitute when you have no clay tandoor.
  • Kashmiri chili is used here for color. A hotter chili powder makes a different dish.

Pairings and uses

  • Poultry
  • sliced onion
  • lemon wedges
  • cucumber salad
  • Gluten-free
  • Nut-free

Chicken Tikka

Boneless chicken, tangy from yogurt and warm with cumin and chili.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Chicken tikka is boneless chicken in pieces, cooked on skewers in a tandoor or over a hot grill. It is a different plate from tandoori chicken, which is the whole bird. The marinade is the same family of yogurt, chili, and ginger used across Punjabi tandoor cooking. A home grill is a fair stand-in if you say it is a grill.

Cultural background

These are cubes of thigh, not a butterflied chicken and not a creamy malai tikka. Yogurt means dairy, so the dish is not vegan. It belongs to Punjabi and North Indian grill practice.

Why it tastes this way

Small pieces brown fast, so the yogurt marinade chars at the edge while the thigh stays juicy. Cumin and chili sit on the surface instead of hiding inside a whole bird. The taste is the high heat of a tandoor or a hot grate, which is why a crowded, cool pan will not taste the same.

How it is made

Marinate boneless chicken thighs in yogurt and spices, thread them on skewers, and cook them in a tandoor or on a hot grill.

Authentic touch. Use thigh meat. Breast cubes dry out before the marinade has a chance to color.

How it is used

Slide the pieces off the skewers and eat them with onion, lemon, and a spoon of the yogurt you held back from the raw marinade.

Field notes

  • Chicken tikka is boneless pieces. It is a different dish from whole tandoori chicken.
  • Thigh meat stays moister on a hot skewer than lean breast meat does.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless chicken thighs, cut in 1.5-inch cubes
  • 3/4 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, chili, ginger, garlic, cumin, salt, oil, and lemon. Coat the chicken and refrigerate it for at least 30 minutes. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The meat is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Thread the cubes on skewers, leaving a little space between pieces. Heat a tandoor, or heat a grill until the grate is very hot and say so if it is a grill. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook, turning, until the center of the thickest cube reaches 165°F (74°C). Do not serve the chicken rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest the skewers a few minutes and serve them hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hot grill replaces a tandoor if you name it as a grill.
  • A little extra chili makes the edge hotter. This batch stays medium.

  • Poultry
  • sliced onion
  • lemon
  • cucumber

Seekh Kebab

Minced lamb with onion, green chili, cilantro, and warm spice, browned on a skewer.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Seekh means skewer. The kebab is minced lamb pressed along that skewer and cooked over charcoal or in a tandoor. It is a North Indian Muslim grill dish, kin to other minced kebabs and different from boti, which is cut in chunks. Awadhi cooks in Lucknow made very fine versions; this one is a home seekh, spiced and grilled, not a claim about one inventor.

Cultural background

The meat is ground lamb, so it has to be cooked through. There is no yogurt and no wheat in the mix. It is not a chunk kebab and not a cream-based kakori paste.

Why it tastes this way

Fat in the lamb bastes the mince as it turns, and onion and green chili keep it from tasting like a plain sausage. Charcoal browns the outside while the center comes up to a ground-meat temperature. Cilantro and cumin are the green, bright note against that browned lamb.

How it is made

Knead ground lamb with onion, chili, and spice, press it onto skewers, and grill it until the center is fully cooked.

Authentic touch. Wet your hands and press the mince on firmly. Loose meat falls into the fire before it sets.

How it is used

Slide the kebabs onto a plate and eat them with onion, mint, and lemon.

Field notes

  • Seekh refers to the skewer the minced lamb is cooked on.
  • Ground lamb has to reach a higher temperature than a whole lamb chop.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds ground lamb
  • 1 small onion, very finely chopped and squeezed dry
  • 1/4 cup chopped cilantro
  • 2 green chilies, minced
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground coriander
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt

Steps

  1. Knead the lamb with the onion, cilantro, chili, ginger, garlic, cumin, coriander, and salt until the mince is sticky. Keep it cold. It is still raw. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  2. Press the mince into long shapes around flat skewers. Refrigerate them if they wait. Do not leave garlic sitting in a warm kitchen. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  3. Grill over a hot fire, turning gently, until the center of each kebab reaches 160°F (71°C). Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest them 3 minutes off the fire. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A flat metal skewer holds the mince better than a thin round stick.
  • A hotter green chili is a common change. This amount is medium.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Street food
  • sliced onion
  • mint leaves
  • lemon
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free
  • Nut-free

Boti Kebab

Lamb chunks with yogurt, chili, and ginger, charred at the edges.

30 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Boti kebab is lamb cut in chunks and threaded on skewers, not minced. It sits in the same North Indian kebab tradition as seekh, and the difference is the cut of meat. Yogurt, ginger, and chili are the usual marinade in restaurant and home grills. The chunks are whole muscle, so they are cooked and rested like other whole lamb, not like ground meat.

Cultural background

This is cubed lamb from a leg or shoulder, grilled by North Indian kebab cooks. Yogurt makes it a dairy dish. It is not seekh, which is mince, and it is not a whole roasted leg.

Why it tastes this way

A chunk of lamb keeps a juicy center while the yogurt marinade spots and browns. Ginger and chili season the surface. On charcoal the fat at the edge crisps. That is a different bite from minced seekh, where the spice is mixed all the way through.

How it is made

Marinate lamb chunks in yogurt and spice, skewer them, and grill them to a whole-muscle temperature with a rest.

Authentic touch. Cut the cubes the same size so they finish together. A thick cube next to a thin one will leave one of them overcooked.

How it is used

Eat the chunks with onion, a green herb chutney if you have one made fresh, and lemon.

Field notes

  • Boti is cut lamb on a skewer. Seekh is the minced kebab in the same grill tradition.
  • Whole lamb chunks are rested after they leave the fire.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless lamb leg, cut in 1.5-inch chunks
  • 3/4 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, ginger, garlic, chili, cumin, salt, and oil. Coat the lamb and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the chunks on skewers. Heat a grill or a tandoor until it is hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of the thickest chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes before serving. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Shoulder cubes have more fat. Leg cubes are leaner and still work if they are not overcooked.
  • A hotter chili powder is common in some kitchens. This marinade is medium.

Paneer Tikka

Firm paneer and peppers, stained with yogurt and Kashmiri chili, browned at the edges.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Paneer tikka is a vegetarian tandoor plate: cubes of fresh cheese with peppers and onion, in a yogurt marinade like chicken tikka. It is a restaurant and home dish of the tandoor menu, not an old whole-animal roast. The cheese is dairy, so the plate is vegetarian and not vegan. A hot grill works if you do not have a tandoor.

Cultural background

There is no meat, fish, or egg. Yogurt and paneer are dairy. It is the cheese version of a tikka skewer, cooked by North Indian kitchens that already run a tandoor for bread and meat.

Why it tastes this way

Paneer does not melt the way aged cheese does, so it can sit on a skewer and take a browned edge. Yogurt and chili cling to the cubes, and pepper and onion soften and char. The flavor is the same tandoor family as chicken tikka, with milk instead of meat.

How it is made

Marinate paneer, pepper, and onion in yogurt and chili, then cook them on skewers in a tandoor or on a hot grill until browned.

Authentic touch. Dry the paneer cubes before they go into the marinade. Wet cubes steam and fall apart.

How it is used

Eat the hot cubes with onion and lemon. There is no meat on the plate.

Field notes

  • Paneer is fresh milk cheese, so the skewer is vegetarian and not vegan.
  • A hot grill can stand in for a tandoor if the cubes are kept moving.

Ingredients

  • 14 ounces paneer, cut in 1-inch cubes and patted dry
  • 1 red bell pepper, cut in chunks
  • 1 onion, cut in chunks
  • 3/4 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Stir the yogurt, chili, cumin, salt, oil, and lemon. Fold in the paneer, pepper, and onion. Refrigerate for 20 minutes. Do not leave the mixture warm on the counter.
  2. Thread the cheese and vegetables on skewers. Heat a tandoor or a hot grill.
  3. Cook, turning, until the paneer is hot through and the edges are browned, about 12 to 15 minutes. There is no meat temperature for cheese.
  4. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A home grill is honest to name as a grill when you have no tandoor.
  • A pinch more chili heat is easy to add. This batch is medium.

Tandoori Fish

Firm fish with a tangy yogurt crust, chili color, and a short, hot cook.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tandoori fish is firm fish in a yogurt and chili marinade, cooked in a tandoor or a very hot oven. It is part of the same North Indian tandoor menu as the chicken, using fish instead of poultry. It is not fried Amritsari fish, which is a batter fry from Amritsar and a different technique. Yogurt keeps dairy in the dish.

Cultural background

The cook is a tandoor or a labeled home oven, not a frying pan. There is fish and dairy. It is not the chickpea-batter fish of Amritsar, and it is not a vegan plate.

Why it tastes this way

Fish cooks quickly, so the yogurt marinade browns before the flesh dries. Chili and ajwain, a sharp seed used with fish in North Indian cooking, sit on that crust. The tandoor taste is dry heat and char, which a fryer full of oil does not give.

How it is made

Marinate thick fish steaks in yogurt, chili, and ajwain, then cook them in a tandoor or a very hot home oven until the center flakes.

Authentic touch. Choose a steak that is at least an inch thick. Thin fillets fall apart and overcook before the marinade colors.

How it is used

Eat it with onion, lemon, and a plain salad. Do not batter or fry it.

Field notes

  • This is tandoor-cooked fish. It is not fried Amritsari fish.
  • Yogurt in the marinade means the plate contains dairy.

Ingredients

  • 4 firm fish steaks, about 6 ounces each
  • 3/4 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon ajwain seeds
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, chili, ginger, garlic, ajwain, salt, lemon, and oil. Coat the fish and refrigerate it for 20 minutes. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The fish is still raw. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  2. Heat a tandoor, or heat a home oven to 450°F (230°C) and name it as an oven. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  3. Cook the steaks until the center reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque. Do not fry them. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve hot with lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Kingfish, salmon, or another firm steak can be used if the local fish is the same thickness.
  • A home oven at high heat should be called an oven, not a tandoor.

Pairings and uses

  • sliced onion
  • lemon
  • cucumber salad
  • Gluten-free

Mughal-Style Raan

A lamb leg with yogurt, ginger, and warm Mughal spice, sliced after a rest.

40 min prep · 2 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Raan is a roasted leg of lamb seasoned in the Mughal style: yogurt, ginger, garlic, and warm spices such as cardamom and cumin. It was a feast cut in imperial and later restaurant kitchens of North India, cooked in a tandoor when the oven was large enough and in a covered oven when it was not. This page uses a home oven if you have no tandoor, and it says so. The leg is whole muscle, cooked and rested, not minced.

Cultural background

This is one lamb leg, not a skewer kebab. Yogurt in the paste means dairy. The spice list is the Mughal pantry of ginger, garlic, and cardamom, used by North Indian feast cooks.

Why it tastes this way

A whole leg keeps its juices if it is not rushed, and the yogurt paste browns into a crust. Cardamom and cumin perfume the fat. In a tandoor the crust dries faster. A home oven is gentler and still gives you the spiced lamb, which is the part that makes raan itself.

How it is made

Coat a lamb leg with a yogurt and spice paste and roast it in a tandoor or, labeled as such, a home oven, then rest it.

Authentic touch. Score the leg and push the paste into the cuts. A paste that sits only on the surface seasons the crust and leaves the center plain.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with onion, lemon, and rice.

Field notes

  • Raan is a whole lamb leg, not a minced kebab.
  • A home oven is a substitute here and should be called an oven, not a tandoor.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in lamb leg, about 5 pounds
  • 1 cup plain yogurt
  • 2 tablespoons grated ginger
  • 6 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Score the leg. Mix the yogurt, ginger, garlic, salt, chili, cumin, cardamom, and oil, and rub it into the cuts. Refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. If you have a tandoor large enough, heat it for roasting. Otherwise heat a home oven to 350°F (175°C) and call the cook an oven roast. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Roast until the center of the thickest muscle, away from the bone, reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the leg 3 minutes, then slice it. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A bone-in leg takes longer than a boneless one. Use the thermometer either way.
  • Saffron is a festive addition and is not required for the spice to read as Mughal.

Pairings and uses

  • Lamb and mutton
  • Feasts

  • lemon
  • rice

Hyderabad Pathar ka Gosht

Thin mutton steaks, spiced and seared on a very hot flat stone or a cast-iron griddle.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Pathar ka gosht is a Hyderabad dish of mutton cooked on a pathar, a thick flat stone used as a griddle. The meat is usually a thin steak, marinated with yogurt and spice, and it cooks fast on that hot surface. This home recipe uses a cast-iron griddle. It does not ask you to heat a found rock; a restaurant pathar is a purpose-made cooking stone, not a river stone from outside.

Cultural background

Hyderabadi cooks sear whole-muscle mutton this way. It is not a minced kebab and not a tandoor chicken. Yogurt in the marinade means dairy. The home pan is a griddle, named as a griddle.

Why it tastes this way

A screaming-hot flat surface browns thin mutton before the inside dries, which is why the steaks are cut thin. Yogurt, chili, and coriander make a savory crust. The stone in a Hyderabad shop holds heat the way a thick cast-iron griddle does at home.

How it is made

Marinate thin mutton steaks, then sear them on a cast-iron griddle standing in for the Hyderabad stone.

Authentic touch. Pound or slice the mutton thin. A thick chop will steam on the griddle and miss the point of the dish.

How it is used

Eat it at once with onion, lemon, and a piece of bread only if you want wheat on the side. The meat itself has none.

Field notes

  • Pathar means stone. In Hyderabad the meat is cooked on a flat cooking stone.
  • This recipe uses a cast-iron griddle and does not heat a found rock.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless mutton or lamb leg, sliced 1/3 inch thick
  • 1/2 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon ground coriander
  • 1 teaspoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, ginger, garlic, coriander, chili, salt, and oil. Coat the meat and refrigerate it for 30 minutes. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The meat is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a cast-iron griddle until it is very hot. This griddle stands in for a Hyderabad pathar. Do not heat a found rock. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Sear the steaks until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Work in batches so the pan stays hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the meat 3 minutes. Serve it hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Lamb leg steaks are the right cut if mutton is not sold.
  • A hotter chili is used in some Hyderabadi kitchens. This batch is medium.

Kakori Kebab

Very finely minced lamb, pale and soft, with cream, white pepper, and warm spice.

40 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Kakori kebab is named for Kakori, near Lucknow, and it belongs to Awadhi cooking. The lamb is minced very fine, finer than a standard seekh, and it is seasoned with warm spice rather than a harsh chili heat. Cream is part of many kitchen versions, which makes the kebab dairy. It can be cooked on a skewer or on a flat griddle; this is the home version of that Lucknow kebab, not a single undisputed origin tale.

Cultural background

Awadhi cooks in Kakori and Lucknow are the people tied to this kebab. It is ground lamb, cooked through, with cream. It is not a chunky boti and not a coarse seekh.

Why it tastes this way

Fine mincing changes the texture: the kebab stays soft instead of coarse. Cream and lamb fat keep it tender, and cardamom and white pepper perfume it without a red-chili crust. On a skewer or a hot griddle the outside browns just enough to smell like a grill.

How it is made

Work lamb and cream into a fine paste, shape it on skewers or as small cakes, and cook it on a grill or a griddle until the center is done.

Authentic touch. Mince twice, or chop by hand until the meat is almost a paste. Coarse mince is a seekh, not a kakori.

How it is used

Serve it with onion and a little mint. The chili heat is mild on purpose.

Field notes

  • The kebab is associated with Kakori, a town near Lucknow, and with Awadhi cooking.
  • The meat is minced finer than a typical seekh kebab.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds lamb, minced twice until very fine
  • 3 tablespoons heavy cream
  • 1 small onion, boiled and mashed, water pressed out
  • 1 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground white pepper
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced

Steps

  1. Mix the fine lamb, cream, onion, cardamom, white pepper, salt, ginger, and garlic into a smooth paste. Keep it cold. It is still raw. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  2. Shape the paste on flat skewers, or form small cakes for a griddle. Refrigerate them until the pan or grill is hot. Do not leave the paste at room temperature. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  3. Cook on a hot grill or griddle, turning carefully, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C). Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest 3 minutes and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Skewers are traditional. A hot griddle is a fair home method for the same paste.
  • Cashew is used in some kitchens and would add a tree nut. This paste uses cream instead.

Bihari Kebab

Thin beef with mustard, mustard oil, ginger, and chili, grilled on skewers.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Bihari kebab is beef, often cut thin, marinated with mustard and mustard oil and grilled on skewers. The name points to Bihar, and the kebab is also cooked in North Indian cities by cooks who carried that seasoning with them. Mustard is the marker, more than a yogurt sauce. The meat is whole muscle, so it is rested after it reaches temperature; it is not a minced seekh.

Cultural background

This is skewered beef in a Bihari mustard marinade. Mustard and mustard oil are in the paste. There is no yogurt and no wheat. It is a grill kebab, not a stew.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard oil is sharp and aromatic, and ground mustard clings to thin beef. Chili heat sits on top of that. A hot skewer browns the oil instead of boiling it. The result is pungent in a way a yogurt boti is not, which is why the two kebabs can share a street and still be different.

How it is made

Marinate thin beef in mustard, mustard oil, ginger, and chili, then grill it on skewers and rest it.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin across the grain. Thick pieces hide the mustard in the middle and take too long on the skewer.

How it is used

Eat it with onion and lemon. The heat is meant to be hot.

Field notes

  • Mustard and mustard oil are the seasoning that marks Bihari kebab.
  • The beef is kept in pieces on skewers, not minced.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds beef sirloin, sliced thin across the grain
  • 2 tablespoons mustard oil
  • 1 tablespoon ground mustard seed
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Mix the mustard oil, ground mustard, ginger, garlic, chili, salt, and lemon. Coat the beef and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The beef is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the slices on skewers without folding them into thick bundles. Heat the grill. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the center of the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the beef 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little raw papaya is sometimes used to soften the meat. It is left out of this batch.
  • Green chili can replace some of the red chili. Keep the mustard either way.

Goan Recheado Mackerel

Grilled mackerel stuffed with a hot red paste of chili, garlic, and vinegar.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Recheado is a Goan word from Portuguese recheado, meaning stuffed. In Goa the stuffing for mackerel is a red paste of dried chili, garlic, ginger, spice, and vinegar, packed into a slit in the fish and then grilled or pan-cooked. Vinegar and chili reflect Goa's coastal and Catholic kitchen, shaped in part by Portuguese colonial trade. This version is grilled; it is a Goan fish, from India, and the paste is hot.

Cultural background

Goan home cooks stuff fresh mackerel with this paste. There is fish and vinegar, and no dairy and no wheat. It is a grill, not a tandoor yogurt fish.

Why it tastes this way

Mackerel is oily, so it stands up to vinegar and a heavy chili paste. The paste steams inside the slit while the skin crisps on the grill. That contrast, sharp inside and charred outside, is why a plain salted mackerel is a different dinner.

How it is made

Grind a chili and vinegar paste, stuff it into mackerel, and grill the fish until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Cut the slit deep enough to hold the paste, and do not leave a mound of paste on the skin where it will burn.

How it is used

Eat the fish with lime and a little rice. The heat is hot.

Field notes

  • Recheado comes from the Portuguese word for stuffed, used in Goa for this chili paste.
  • The fish is grilled with the paste inside, not fried in a batter.

Ingredients

  • 4 small whole mackerel, cleaned, heads on or off
  • 8 dried hot red chilies, stemmed and soaked in warm water
  • 6 garlic cloves, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground black pepper
  • 2 tablespoons palm vinegar or white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend the soaked chilies, garlic, ginger, cumin, pepper, vinegar, salt, and oil into a thick paste. Slit each mackerel and fill the slit with paste. Refrigerate the fish. The fish is still raw. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  2. Heat a grill to a steady medium-hot fire. Oil the grate. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  3. Grill the fish, turning once, until the center of the thickest fish reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pomfret is also stuffed this way in Goa. Mackerel is the fish on this page.
  • A milder dried chili lowers the heat. Kashmiri chili alone will not taste like a hot recheado.

Kolkata Kathi Kebab

Skewered lamb with yogurt, ginger, and chili, browned over a hot fire.

30 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Kathi kebab is the skewered meat of a Kolkata street plate. In the city it is often rolled into a paratha and eaten as a kathi roll. This recipe cooks only the meat. The paratha stays a pairing, not an ingredient, so the kebab has no wheat if the marinade has none; yogurt, ginger, and chili are the marinade; the lamb is whole muscle on a skewer, from an Indian kitchen in Kolkata.

Cultural background

Kolkata street cooks grill this meat and, when you ask, wrap it. The written dish is the skewer. Yogurt means dairy. There is no flour in the bowl, which is deliberate so the meat can be gluten-free.

Why it tastes this way

A hot skewer browns the yogurt marinade on small pieces of lamb, and ginger and chili stay bright. The Kolkata roll adds a toasted paratha around that meat. Leaving the paratha on the side lets you taste the kebab itself, which is the grilled part of the street plate.

How it is made

Marinate lamb in yogurt and spice with no wheat, grill it on skewers, and rest it. Offer a paratha beside it if you want the roll.

Authentic touch. Cut the lamb small enough to cook through on a street-style skewer. Huge chunks are a different roast.

How it is used

Eat the meat with onion and lemon. A paratha can sit on the side as a pairing and is not part of the marinade.

Field notes

  • In Kolkata the kebab is often eaten in a paratha roll. The bread is a pairing on this page.
  • The marinade has no wheat, so the meat itself can be gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds boneless lamb, cut in 1-inch chunks
  • 1/2 cup plain yogurt
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon Kashmiri chili powder
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the yogurt, ginger, garlic, chili, cumin, salt, lemon, and oil. Do not add wheat flour. Coat the lamb and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the lamb on skewers. Heat a grill until it is hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of the thickest chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the meat 3 minutes. Serve the kebab with onion and lemon. If you add a paratha, keep it as a side. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Chicken pieces are also sold on Kolkata skewers and would need a poultry temperature. This is lamb.
  • Wrapping the meat in a paratha makes the street roll and adds wheat.

Malai Chicken Tikka

Boneless chicken in a mild cream marinade, with cardamom and white pepper.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Malai means cream. Malai chicken tikka is boneless chicken in a cream marinade, cooked in a tandoor or on a hot grill, and it is a milder plate than chili-red chicken tikka. It is a North Indian restaurant and home dish in the tandoor family. Cream is dairy, so the dish is not vegan; the pieces are not a whole tandoori chicken.

Cultural background

Tandoor cooks in Delhi and other North Indian cities sell this pale, mild tikka beside the red one. There is chicken and cream. There is no wheat. The heat is mild on purpose.

Why it tastes this way

Cream browns into a soft crust instead of a sharp chili crust, and cardamom perfumes the fat. White pepper gives aroma without painting the meat red. High heat still matters: the cream should spot and color, not boil in a cool pan.

How it is made

Marinate boneless chicken in cream, cardamom, and white pepper, then cook it in a tandoor or on a hot grill.

Authentic touch. Do not add a heavy hand of red chili. If the marinade looks like the red tikka, it is no longer malai.

How it is used

Eat the pieces with onion and lemon. The plate is mild.

Field notes

  • Malai means cream, and the marinade is a cream marinade.
  • The chicken is boneless. It is not the whole tandoori bird.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless chicken thighs, cut in cubes
  • 1/2 cup heavy cream
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground cardamom
  • 1/2 teaspoon ground white pepper
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Mix the cream, ginger, garlic, cardamom, white pepper, salt, oil, and lemon. Coat the chicken and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The chicken is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Thread the cubes on skewers. Heat a tandoor, or heat a grill and call it a grill. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Cook until the center of the thickest cube reaches 165°F (74°C). Do not serve the chicken rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest a few minutes and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hot grill replaces a tandoor if you say it is a grill.
  • A green chili on the side can add heat without stirring it into the cream.
