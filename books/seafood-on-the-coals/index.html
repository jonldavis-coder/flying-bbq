Angolan Mufete

Grilled fish with palm oil, onion, beans, and sweet plantain.

30 min prep · 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Mufete is an Angolan plate of grilled fish with beans and plantain, strongly associated with Luanda weekends. The fish is whole, the beans are cooked through, and ripe plantain is boiled or grilled until soft. Palm oil is a common finish. This page does not use raw bitter cassava. The fish is the center, and the beans and plantain are part of the plate rather than optional garnish.

Cultural background

Luanda households cook mufete when there is fish and time. It is a grilled fish plate, not a stew of dried fish. The beans must be fully cooked if they start out dried.

Why it tastes this way

A charred fish, a soft plantain, and a bean relish give three textures. Palm oil adds fruit and color. Onion and a little chili keep the beans savory. The Atlantic catch and the weekend table are why the plate is large. Leaving the cassava out avoids a food this recipe is not equipped to process safely.

How it is made

Grill a whole fish, and serve it with fully cooked beans and ripe plantain.

Authentic touch. Grill the fish whole and simmer the beans until they are completely soft. If you use dried kidney beans, boil them hard. Do not hold them warm from raw.

How it is used

The fish beside the beans and plantain, with onion. It is a shared plate.

Field notes

  • Mufete is an Angolan plate of grilled fish with beans and plantain.
  • Luanda weekends are closely associated with the dish.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole firm fish, about 3 lb, gutted and scaled
  • 2 cups dried beans, or 4 cups beans already boiled until tender
  • 3 ripe plantains, peeled and halved
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 2 tablespoons palm oil or another oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 fresh chili, chopped

Steps

  1. If the beans are dried, boil them in plenty of water until completely tender. Kidney beans must be boiled hard, not held warm from raw. Drain them.
  2. Simmer the plantains in salted water until soft, or grill them beside the fish. Rub the fish with salt and oil.
  3. Grill the fish until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh flakes.
  4. Toss the beans with onion, chili, and palm oil. Serve with the fish and plantain. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Whatever firm local fish is fresh, named as that fish.
  • Sweet potato in place of plantain if plantain is not ripe.

Antiguan Grilled Lobster

Lobster grilled with butter, thyme, and a local hot pepper.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Grilled lobster in Antigua and Barbuda is a butter, thyme, and hot-pepper cook, different from the lime and garlic spiny lobster of the Bahamas. The animal is usually a warm-water lobster tail. Butter carries the thyme and the pepper onto the meat as it browns. This page keeps those three seasonings and does not copy the Bahamian plate.

Cultural background

The written recipe uses butter, so it is not dairy-free. The pepper is minced into the butter rather than served only as a side sauce. The flesh is shellfish and is cooked until opaque.

Why it tastes this way

Butter browns at the edge of the shell and holds thyme against the sweet meat. A local hot pepper, the kind grown in the eastern Caribbean, adds heat without the citrus-first taste of the Bahamian grill. The result is richer and more herbal.

How it is made

Grill lobster tails with thyme butter and a minced hot pepper until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Melt the butter with the thyme before it goes on the lobster so the herb actually flavors the fat.

How it is used

Serve with more thyme butter, bread if you want it, and a green salad. Bread adds wheat and is a pairing, not part of the lobster.

Field notes

  • Antiguan grilled lobster is seasoned with butter, thyme, and a local hot pepper.
  • It is a different seasoning from the Bahamian lime and garlic lobster.

Ingredients

  • 4 lobster tails, about 8 ounces each, split
  • 4 tablespoons butter
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 small Antiguan or other hot pepper, seeded and minced
  • 1 garlic clove, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Melt the butter with thyme, pepper, garlic, and salt. Keep it warm and use it the same day. Do not leave garlic butter at room temperature for later. The lobster is raw and is not ready to eat. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  2. Brush the cut meat with some of the butter. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  3. Grill, shell side down first, until the flesh is opaque and pearly and the center reads 145°F (63°C). Brush once more near the end.
  4. The lobster should already be opaque and at 145°F (63°C). Serve with the remaining warm butter. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook shrimp, lobster, and crab until the flesh is opaque and pearly. An internal temperature of 145°F (63°C) is the target for lobster and shrimp.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A split tail and a whole tail both work if the center of the meat is checked.
  • Clarified butter resists burning. Ordinary butter is what this recipe uses, watched closely.

Pairings and uses

  • bread
  • green salad
  • lime

Australian Grilled Prawns

Prawns with garlic, lemon, and parsley, grilled until the flesh turns opaque.

15 min prep · 8 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Grilled prawns are a coastal Australian meal, especially along the tropical north and the Queensland shore around Cairns, where prawns are landed and cooked the same day when the catch allows. The method is a hot grate, garlic, lemon, and a short cook. Shellfish are done when they are opaque. This is a plain coastal grill, seasoned and taken off the heat before the prawns toughen.

Cultural background

Coastal home cooks and fish shops grill large prawns in the shell or peeled. There is shellfish, and no wheat, dairy, or chili in this recipe. Parsley and lemon finish the plate. Cook them the day you buy them.

Why it tastes this way

Prawns are sweet, and a short blast of heat sets that sweetness before it turns rubbery. Garlic browns in the oil, and lemon cuts the richness. The tropical coast supplies the prawns. The recipe does not need a slogan. It needs a hot grate and a thermometer or a clear look at opaque flesh.

How it is made

Toss prawns with garlic, oil, and lemon, and grill them until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Pull them as soon as they turn opaque. Extra time on a hot grate is how sweet prawns become dry.

How it is used

Eat them with lemon, parsley, and bread only if you want wheat on the side. The prawns themselves have none.

Field notes

  • Large prawns are a coastal Australian grill, cooked briefly over high heat.
  • They are ready when the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds large prawns, peeled and deveined
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons lemon juice
  • 2 tablespoons chopped parsley

Steps

  1. Toss the prawns with oil, garlic, salt, and lemon juice. Refrigerate them for 15 minutes. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The prawns are still raw. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  2. Heat a grill until the grate is hot. Oil it lightly. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  3. Grill, turning once, until the flesh is opaque and the center reaches 145°F (63°C). Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  4. Toss with parsley and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook shellfish until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).

Cook shrimp, lobster, and crab until the flesh is opaque and pearly. An internal temperature of 145°F (63°C) is the target for lobster and shrimp.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leaving the shells on protects the meat and makes them messier to eat.
  • A pinch of chili is a personal addition. This batch has none.

Baghdad Masgouf

Carp with salt, tamarind, olive oil, and the smoke of a fire beside the fish.

20 min prep · 40 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 servings

History

Masgouf is a Tigris fish cook: carp, split open and staked or set beside an open fire in Baghdad, then finished with a brush of sauce. Riverside restaurants made it famous, and families cook it when they can get the fish. If carp is hard to buy, a firm white fish may be eaten as a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf. This recipe is written for carp.

Cultural background

Baghdad cooks along the Tigris are the reference. The dish is carp and fire, not a fried fish and not a stew. A different species does not inherit the name.

Why it tastes this way

Carp is rich, and standing it beside the fire, rather than over a hard flame, renders some of that fat slowly and smokes the skin. Tamarind and salt cut the oil. Olive oil at the end shines it. The river fish and the slow side-fire are the taste, which is why a random white fillet in a pan is a different dinner.

How it is made

Split a carp open, salt it, and cook it beside an open fire until it flakes, then brush it with tamarind and oil.

Authentic touch. Keep the carp beside the fire, skin toward the heat, and do not rush it. If you must use another fish, say so and do not call the plate masgouf.

How it is used

With onion and tomato. The fish is the plate.

Field notes

  • Masgouf is Baghdad's grilled carp, a Tigris fish cook set beside the fire.
  • A firm white fish can be a substitute, and that substitute is not masgouf.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole carp, about 3 lb, split open and scaled
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon tamarind paste
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 1 onion, sliced

Steps

  1. Pat the carp dry and salt it. Mix tamarind, olive oil, and turmeric into a brush.
  2. Build an open fire. Set the carp skin-side toward the heat, beside the flames rather than in them, on a rack or stakes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes. Brush with the tamarind mixture near the end.
  4. Serve with onion. This recipe is for carp. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A covered grill with the fish on a rack away from direct flame, still using carp.
  • A little turmeric in the brush, which some cooks add.

  • Onion
  • Tomato
  • Lemon

Bahamian Grilled Spiny Lobster

Spiny lobster grilled with lime, garlic, and a little pepper.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

The lobster of the Bahamas is spiny lobster, a warm-water animal without the big claws of a cold-water lobster. Grilling a split tail is a common cook on the islands. This version is lime, garlic, and a little goat pepper or other mild heat. It is not the Antiguan grill, which is built on butter and thyme.

Cultural background

Spiny lobster tails are shellfish. They are done when the flesh is opaque and pearly and has reached the shellfish temperature. A clawed lobster is a different animal and not what this page describes.

Why it tastes this way

Lime and garlic stay bright on a lean, sweet shellfish, and a short grill over coals adds a little browning without a dairy sauce. The Bahamian pepper is floral and hot, used in a small amount so the lobster remains the taste. Butter and thyme would push the plate toward the Antiguan version, so they are left off here.

How it is made

Grill split spiny lobster tails with lime and garlic until the flesh is opaque.

Authentic touch. Split the tail and grill it shell side down first so the meat cooks in the shell instead of drying on the open grate.

How it is used

Eat it with lime, peas and rice, and a simple slaw.

Field notes

  • Bahamian lobster is spiny lobster, which does not have the big claws of a cold-water lobster.
  • This lime and garlic grill is a different plate from the Antiguan butter and thyme lobster.

Ingredients

  • 4 spiny lobster tails, about 8 ounces each, split lengthwise
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 limes
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil
  • 1/2 mild hot pepper such as a Bahamian goat pepper, seeded and minced

Steps

  1. Loosen the meat from the shell but leave it in place. Rub it with oil, garlic, salt, lime juice, and pepper. It is raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  2. Heat a grill to medium-high. The shellfish is not ready to eat until the flesh is opaque and reaches 145°F (63°C).
  3. Grill shell side down, then turn, until the flesh is opaque and pearly and the center reads 145°F (63°C).
  4. The lobster should already be opaque and at 145°F (63°C). Serve with more lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook shrimp, lobster, and crab until the flesh is opaque and pearly. An internal temperature of 145°F (63°C) is the target for lobster and shrimp.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Leave the pepper out if you want the lobster only lime and garlic.
  • A clawed lobster tail can be cooked to the same temperature, and it should not be called Bahamian spiny lobster.

Balinese Sate Lilit

Minced fish and shrimp with coconut, lime leaf, and chili, grilled on lemongrass.

35 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Sate lilit is a Balinese skewer of minced seafood pressed around a stalk, often lemongrass, and grilled. Coconut, chili, lime leaf, and shallot are mixed into the mince. It is temple-offering food and also everyday food in Balinese homes and warungs. The fish and shrimp are cooked until opaque; this is a seafood satay, not the pork spit of babi guling.

Cultural background

Balinese cooks shape this mince by hand around the stalk. There is fish and shrimp. Coconut milk is not dairy. There is no peanut and no wheat in the mix. It is a different Balinese fire from the spit-roasted pig.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut fat keeps the mince moist, and lime leaf and chili perfume it. Lemongrass used as the skewer shares a little of its oil with the paste as it grills. The result is aromatic and richer than a plain fish cake, which is why the stalk is part of the dish and not only a stick.

How it is made

Mix fish and shrimp with coconut and spice, press the paste onto lemongrass stalks, and grill until the seafood is opaque.

Authentic touch. Press the mince on firmly and oil your hands. A loose coating falls into the grate.

How it is used

Eat the skewers hot, with rice and a little extra lime.

Field notes

  • Sate lilit is minced seafood pressed onto a stalk, a Balinese grill.
  • Lemongrass often serves as the skewer, not only as a flavor in the bowl.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound firm white fish, minced
  • 1/2 pound shrimp, peeled and minced
  • 1/3 cup coconut milk
  • 4 lime leaves, minced
  • 2 shallots, minced
  • 2 hot chilies, minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 8 lemongrass stalks, for skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the fish, shrimp, coconut milk, lime leaf, shallot, chili, salt, and turmeric. Keep it cold. The seafood is still raw. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  2. Press the paste around the upper part of each lemongrass stalk. Oil your hands so it sticks. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  3. Grill over medium heat, turning carefully, until the fish and shrimp are opaque and the center reaches 145°F (63°C). Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  4. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All fish, with no shrimp, is a common batch and would drop the shellfish allergen.
  • A hotter chili is used for some ceremonies and home meals. This batch is medium.

Barbadian Grilled Flying Fish

A light, herb-seasoned fish with lime, whether it is flying fish or a named substitute.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Flying fish is the fish most associated with Barbadian cooking, often fried or steamed with cou-cou and also grilled at seaside stalls. Oistins is one of the places people go for that fish. When flying fish is not sold where you live, a mild white fish such as tilapia is a substitute and has to be named as one. Do not call the substitute flying fish.

Cultural background

The seasoning is thyme, garlic, onion, lime, and a mild pepper. The fish is small and quick on a hot grill. Cou-cou, a cornmeal side, is a pairing and is not cooked in this recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Flying fish is mild and a little sweet, so thyme and lime do the seasoning instead of a heavy sauce. A short grill keeps it from drying. A substitute white fish can take the same herbs, and it will taste like that fish, which is why the name on the plate has to stay honest.

How it is made

Season flying fish, or a stated mild white fish, and grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Dry the fillets well. Small fish stick and tear if they go onto a wet grate.

How it is used

Eat it with lime, a little pepper sauce, and cou-cou or rice if you have it.

Field notes

  • Flying fish is the fish most associated with Barbadian cooking.
  • When it is not sold where you live, a mild white fish is a substitute and should be named as one.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds flying fish fillets, or 2 pounds mild white fish such as tilapia if flying fish is unavailable
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 small onion, grated
  • 1 teaspoon fresh thyme leaves
  • 1 lime
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. If you are not using flying fish, label the substitute out loud and on any note you serve with it. Pat the fish dry and rub it with salt, garlic, onion, thyme, lime juice, and oil. It is raw and is not ready to eat. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Oil a hot, clean grate. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill the fillets until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C) and the fish is opaque and flakes. Small fillets may be done in only a few minutes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Whole small flying fish and fillets both work. Check the thickest part either way.
  • Cou-cou is the classic side and is not required to call the fish grilled.

  • Fish
  • cou-cou
  • lime
  • pepper sauce

Belizean Grilled Snapper

Whole snapper grilled with lime, garlic, and thyme.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

On the Belizean coast, snapper is a boat fish, and grilling it is ordinary cooking in Creole and Garifuna towns. Garifuna communities carry African and Island Carib histories and have lived on this coast since the late eighteenth century. The seasoning here is lime, garlic, thyme, and a little hot pepper. The page is that grill and nothing invented beyond it.

Cultural background

A whole snapper cooks evenly if the cavity is open and the fire is medium. The flesh is fish, and it is done when it flakes. Plantains or rice and beans are the usual starch beside it.

Why it tastes this way

Coastal thyme and lime are bright against the sweet, lean flesh of snapper. A whole fish over coals picks up a little smoke in the skin and stays moist behind it. The hot pepper is there for the people who want it, not to hide the fish.

How it is made

Stuff a whole snapper with garlic, thyme, and lime and grill it until the flesh flakes.

Authentic touch. Score the sides of the fish so the heat reaches the backbone, and check the temperature there, not only in the thin tail.

How it is used

Eat it with rice and beans, fried plantain, and extra lime.

Field notes

  • Snapper is a routine reef fish on the Belizean coast, which is why it is a grill and not a special import.
  • Creole and Garifuna kitchens both cook coastal fish, and they do not use one identical seasoning.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole snapper, about 3 pounds, gutted and scaled
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 3 garlic cloves, sliced
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 1 lime, sliced, plus 1 lime for the table
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 habanero, seeded, optional

Steps

  1. Pat the fish dry, oil it, and salt the cavity and the skin. Tuck in garlic, thyme, lime, and the pepper if you are using it. The fish is raw and is not ready to eat. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Heat a grill to medium and oil the grate so the skin can release. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill the fish, turning once, until the thickest part at the backbone reads 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve it with lime. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fillets work if a whole fish is not available. Cook them to the same fish temperature and say they are fillets.
  • Leave the pepper out for a milder plate. The lime and thyme still carry the dish.

Pairings and uses

  • Fish
  • rice and beans
  • fried plantain
  • lime
  • Gluten-free
  • Dairy-free

Block Island Grilled Swordfish

Meaty swordfish with salt, oil, and a clean grill mark.

10 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Block Island, off Rhode Island, has long been a port for offshore swordfish boats. Grilled steaks are a summer plate when the fish is landed. The cook is short. Swordfish dries out if it is left on the grate past the point where it turns opaque.

Cultural background

Grilled swordfish steaks. A New England port fish, cooked simply. It is not a smoked fish and not a chowder.

Why it tastes this way

Swordfish is dense and mild, so salt, oil, and a hot grate show the meat instead of a sauce. A short cook keeps the center moist. The island's place in the fishery is why the plate is tied to this harbor rather than to a generic grill.

How it is made

Oil and salt swordfish steaks and grill them briefly on both sides.

Authentic touch. Use steaks about an inch thick. Thin pieces overcook between the time you turn them and the time you plate them.

How it is used

Thai Pla Pao

A whole fish steamed inside a salt crust, with moist flesh and a clean taste.

20 min prep · 35 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Pla pao is a Thai way of grilling a whole fish inside a jacket of salt, often set near an open fire. The salt crust keeps the flesh moist and seasons it. River fish and tilapia are both used. The crust is cracked open at the table; it is not a chili curry and not a fried fish; the only heat is the fire, not chili.

Cultural background

Riverside and home cooks in Thailand pack the fish in salt and give it time by the fire. There is fish and salt, and no chili in the crust. Egg white is sometimes used to bind a salt dough. This recipe uses egg white, so it contains egg.

Why it tastes this way

The salt crust blocks direct flame, so the fish steams in its own moisture instead of drying. A little salt reaches the flesh. Herbs in the cavity perfume it without a sauce. When the crust is cracked, the fish tastes clean, which is the point of the method.

How it is made

Stuff a whole fish, pack it in a salt and egg-white crust, and cook it beside a fire or in a hot oven until the flesh flakes.

Authentic touch. Make the crust thick enough that you cannot see the skin. Thin salt burns and does not protect the fish.

How it is used

Crack off the salt crust and eat the fish with lime and a chili sauce on the side if you want heat.

Field notes

  • Pla pao cooks the fish inside a salt crust so the flesh steams.
  • The crust is broken away. It is not a sauce you eat by the spoon.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole fish, about 2 pounds, cleaned and scaled
  • 4 cups coarse salt
  • 2 egg whites
  • 2 stalks lemongrass, crushed
  • 4 lime leaves, torn
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Fill the cavity with lemongrass and lime leaves. Stir the salt, egg whites, and water into a packable crust. The fish is still raw. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  2. Pack the crust all over the fish in a thick layer. Heat an open fire with a grill rack to the side, or heat an oven to 400°F (200°C) and call it an oven. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  3. Cook until the center of the thickest part reaches 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque. Crack a little crust to check, then close it if it needs more time. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.
  4. Rest it briefly, crack away the crust, and serve the fish. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook fish to 145°F (63°C) until it is opaque and flakes.

  • A hot oven can finish the crust if you do not have an open fire. Say that it is an oven.
  • Lemongrass in the cavity is a common herb. Chili is for the table, not the crust.

Trinidadian Grilled Kingfish

Grilled kingfish or mahi with culantro, garlic, and a hot pepper.

25 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Trinidad and Tobago, kingfish is a common grill, and the marinade is built on chadon beni, the local name for culantro, with garlic and thyme. Chadon beni is Eryngium foetidum, a different herb from cilantro, with a stronger leaf. The fish may be kingfish or mahi when that is what the market has. Both are cooked as fish, to the same temperature.

Cultural background

Green seasoning is an everyday Trinidadian blend, not a single bottled brand. This one is culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, and lime. The fish is the meal. It is not a meat dish with a fish name.

Why it tastes this way

Culantro is more pungent than cilantro, so a short marinade still tastes green and garlicky after the grill. Lime keeps the oil from feeling heavy. A hot grate marks the steaks, and the pepper, a Caribbean chile, sits in the fat of the fish rather than in a separate sauce.

How it is made

Marinate kingfish or mahi in culantro and garlic, then grill it until it flakes.

Authentic touch. If you only have cilantro, use more of it and say it is not chadon beni. The two herbs are related and not interchangeable in strength.

How it is used

Eat it with pepper sauce, a slice of avocado, and coconut-scented rice if that is on the table.

Field notes

  • Chadon beni is culantro, Eryngium foetidum, which Trinidadian cooks use by the bunch.
  • It is a different herb from cilantro, though the two are related.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds kingfish steaks, or mahi-mahi if kingfish is not sold
  • 1 cup chopped chadon beni (culantro), or a larger handful of cilantro labeled as a substitute
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 4 sprigs fresh thyme
  • 1/2 Scotch bonnet, seeded
  • 1 lime
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Blend culantro, garlic, thyme, pepper, lime juice, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. If you used cilantro, tell people it is not chadon beni. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Refrigerate the fish if it sits more than a few minutes. Do not leave the garlic marinade out. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill over a hot fire until the thickest steak reads 145°F (63°C) and the flesh is opaque and flakes.
  4. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Serve it hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Mahi-mahi replaces kingfish when kingfish is not sold. Name the fish you actually cooked.
  • Leave the seeds out of the pepper if you want the plate less hot.

Yucatán Tikin Xic

Fish stained with achiote and sour orange, gently charred, sometimes steamed a little by a leaf.

25 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Tikin xic is a Yucatecan fish cook: achiote and sour orange on a fillet or a split fish, sometimes wrapped in banana leaf and grilled. Achiote is a native American seed. Sour orange arrived after Spanish contact. The leaf is a wrapper cooks use when they have it. This page does not turn the grill into a ceremony.

Cultural background

The dish is fish, in the same seasoning family as Yucatecan pork, with a different animal and a shorter cook. A home kitchen without banana leaves can use parchment and should say so.

Why it tastes this way

Achiote colors the fish and adds a peppery earth taste, and sour orange keeps it bright. If a banana leaf is on the grill, the fish steams a little as well as browns. Without the leaf, the same paste tastes more directly of the fire. Either way the fish, not the wrapper, is the center.

How it is made

Coat fish with achiote and sour orange and grill it, in a leaf if you have one, until it flakes.

Authentic touch. Oil the leaf or the parchment so the paste does not stick and tear the cooked fish.

How it is used

Eat it with corn tortillas, pickled onion, and a tomato salsa.

Field notes

  • Achiote and sour orange are the Yucatecan seasoning pair used here on fish.
  • A banana leaf wraps the fish when cooks have one; it is not itself the seasoning.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds firm white fish fillets, such as snapper or grouper
  • 2 tablespoons ground achiote (annatto)
  • 1/4 cup sour orange juice, or 2 tablespoons orange juice plus 2 tablespoons lime juice
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 banana leaf, or parchment paper

Steps

  1. Mix achiote, juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and oil. Coat the fish. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  2. Wrap the fish in a banana leaf, or in parchment if you have no leaf. Parchment is not a banana leaf. Refrigerate it if it waits more than 2 hours. The fish is not ready to eat until it reaches 145°F (63°C) and turns opaque.
  3. Grill the packet over a medium-hot fire, turning once, until the center of the thickest fillet reads 145°F (63°C) and the fish is opaque and flakes.
  4. Open the packet carefully. The fish should already be at 145°F (63°C). Eat it the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Grouper, snapper, or another firm white fish all work. Cook each to the fish temperature.
  • No banana leaf: use parchment, and say the packet is not the leaf.
