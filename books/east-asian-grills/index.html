Cantonese Char Siu

Sweet-salty pork with a lacquered edge, five spice, and a little char.

30 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Char siu means fork-roast. Cantonese cooks hung strips of pork from forks in a roast oven so the fat dripped away and the glaze lacquered the meat. Roast shops in Guangzhou and other Cantonese cities still sell it that way. This recipe uses a rack in a home oven and says so; it is not a restaurant fork oven; soy sauce and hoisin bring soy and wheat.

Cultural background

Cantonese roast shops are the home of this pork. A home oven on a rack borrows the glaze and not the oven. There is pork, soy, wheat, and sesame oil. There is no dairy. The meat is a whole cut, rested, not a pulled shoulder.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar and maltose brown into a lacquer, and five spice and fermented seasoning sit in that sweetness. Hanging the meat in a shop oven dries the surface. A home rack still lets heat reach all sides, which is closer to a fork roast than a pork sitting in a puddle of sauce. The wheat in the soy sauce is part of that Cantonese seasoning, not an accident.

How it is made

Coat pork strips with a soy, hoisin, and five-spice glaze and roast them on a rack in a home oven until the center is done, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Put the pork on a rack over a pan. If it braises in the glaze, the lacquer never forms.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with rice and a few greens.

Field notes

  • Char siu means fork-roast, from the forks that held the pork in a Cantonese oven.
  • A home oven on a rack is a substitute and should be called a home oven.

Ingredients

  • 2.5 pounds boneless pork shoulder, cut into long strips
  • 3 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 2 tablespoons hoisin sauce
  • 1 tablespoon maltose or brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Stir the soy sauce, hoisin, maltose or sugar, five spice, sesame oil, garlic, and neutral oil. Coat the pork and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a home oven to 425°F (220°C). Set the pork on a rack over a pan. This is a home oven, not a Cantonese fork oven. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Roast, turning once, until the center of the thickest strip reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then slice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Fermented red bean curd deepens the color and still counts as soy.
  • Honey in the glaze would add honey. This glaze uses sugar and maltose.

Cantonese Siu Yuk

Roast pork belly with blistered skin, salt, and five spice under the crackling.

30 min prep · 1 hr 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Siu yuk is Cantonese roast pork with a blistered skin, sold in the same roast shops as char siu. The skin is dried, salted, and hit with high heat so it bubbles, while five spice seasons the meat underneath. A restaurant oven runs hotter and drier than most homes. This is a home-oven version, labeled as such; the belly is a whole cut and is rested; there is no soy sauce in this rub, so the written recipe has no wheat.

Cultural background

Cantonese roast shops hang or rack the belly until the skin crackles. Home cooks can dry the skin and use a hot oven. There is pork, salt, and five spice. There is no dairy and no wheat in this rub.

Why it tastes this way

Dry skin plus high heat makes the crackling, and the fat underneath bastes the meat. Five spice stays under the skin so it does not burn. Salt is the main seasoning, which is why siu yuk tastes less sweet than char siu from the same shop. A covered braise would soften the skin and miss the dish.

How it is made

Salt and dry a pork belly, rub five spice on the meat side, and roast it in a home oven until the center is done and the skin has blistered.

Authentic touch. Dry the skin, uncovered, in the refrigerator before you roast it. Wet skin steams and stays leathery.

How it is used

Chop it into pieces and eat it with rice and mustard greens.

Field notes

  • Siu yuk is Cantonese roast pork bought for the crackling skin.
  • This rub has no soy sauce, so the written recipe has no wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2.5 pounds skin-on pork belly, in one slab
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 teaspoon white vinegar
  • 1/2 teaspoon salt, extra, for the skin

Steps

  1. Score the skin in a tight crosshatch, cutting into the skin and not deep into the meat. Rub vinegar and the extra salt on the skin. Rub five spice and the remaining salt on the meat side only. Refrigerate the slab uncovered for at least 4 hours so the skin dries. The pork is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a home oven to 450°F (230°C). Set the belly on a rack, skin side up. This is a home oven, not a roast-shop oven. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Roast until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and the skin has blistered. If the meat is ready and the skin is pale, give it a short hotter blast and check the temperature again. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then chop it. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A final few minutes of very high heat helps the bubbles if the skin is already dry.
  • A spoon of vinegar rubbed on the skin is used by some cooks to help it dry.

Home-Roast Peking Duck

Roast duck with lacquered skin, eaten with scallion, cucumber, and wheat pancakes.

40 min prep · 2 hr cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Peking duck is a Beijing restaurant duck, lacquered and roasted so the skin crisps, then carved and eaten with scallion, cucumber, and a sweet sauce in thin wheat pancakes. Restaurant ovens and a long drying step are part of that service. This is a simplified home roast. It is not a restaurant ticket, and it does not reproduce a hung duck oven; the duck still has to reach a poultry temperature; the pancakes and hoisin contain wheat, and hoisin contains soy.

Cultural background

Beijing restaurants built the dish guests travel for. A home oven can roast a duck and serve the same accompaniments if you say it is the home roast. There is duck, wheat, soy, and sesame in the sauce. There is no dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Duck skin renders and crisps when the bird is dried and roasted in open heat, and the fat underneath keeps the meat rich. Scallion and cucumber are cool against that fat. A sweet wheat-and-soy sauce ties the pancake to the skin. A restaurant oven does this more evenly. The home bird can still taste of rendered duck, which is the center of the plate.

How it is made

Dry a duck, roast it in a home oven until the thigh is done, and serve the carved meat with scallion, cucumber, hoisin, and wheat pancakes.

Authentic touch. Dry the skin. A wet duck steams, and the pancake then wraps soft skin, which is a different meal.

How it is used

Slice skin and meat and eat them in small wheat pancakes with scallion, cucumber, and hoisin.

Field notes

  • Peking duck is a Beijing restaurant roast. This page is a simplified home roast, not a restaurant ticket.
  • Duck is poultry and is cooked to 165°F (74°C).

Ingredients

  • 1 whole duck, about 5 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons maltose or brown sugar, dissolved in 1/2 cup hot water
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 8 wheat pancakes
  • 4 scallions, cut in slivers
  • 1 cucumber, cut in sticks
  • 1/4 cup hoisin sauce
  • 1 teaspoon sesame oil

Steps

  1. Pat the duck dry. Rub the cavity with salt and five spice. Paint the skin with the maltose or sugar water. Refrigerate the duck uncovered so the skin dries. The duck is still raw. Do not leave it on the counter to dry. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Heat a home oven to 375°F (190°C). Set the duck on a rack. This is a home roast, not a restaurant ticket and not a hung oven. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Roast until the thickest part of the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone. Do not serve the duck rare. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest it 10 minutes, carve it, and serve with pancakes, scallion, cucumber, and hoisin stirred with the sesame oil. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A day of uncovered drying in the refrigerator improves the skin.
  • Store-bought wheat pancakes are the wrapper. Rice wrappers would be a different dish.

Xinjiang Lamb Skewers

Lamb skewers with salt, cumin, and dried chili, grilled over a street fire.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Xinjiang, Uyghur street grills cook small lamb skewers over charcoal and dust them with cumin and dried chili. The seasoning is salt, cumin, and chili, put on as the meat cooks. This page is about that street food. It is not a political essay, and it does not treat the cooks as a symbol of anything except the grill they run; the lamb is whole muscle and is rested.

Cultural background

Uyghur grill cooks in cities such as Ürümqi sell these skewers from small fires. There is no yogurt marinade and no wheat on the meat. The fat, the cumin, and the chili are the dish.

Why it tastes this way

Cumin and dried chili bloom in lamb fat over a hot fire, which is why the spices are added to the cooking meat and not only stirred into a raw paste. Salt keeps the lamb direct. Small pieces char at the edge and stay juicy inside. A sweet glaze would hide the cumin that marks this street grill.

How it is made

Skewer small pieces of lamb, grill them hot, and season them with salt, cumin, and chili as they cook, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Sprinkle the cumin and chili in more than one pass while the skewers turn. One heavy dump falls off into the coals.

How it is used

Eat the skewers as soon as they have rested, while the cumin is still fragrant.

Field notes

  • Uyghur street grills in Xinjiang season lamb skewers with cumin and chili.
  • The spices go on the meat over the fire, not only in a marinade bowl.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds boneless lamb, cut in small chunks with a little fat
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 teaspoon dried chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb with oil and half the salt. Thread it on flat skewers. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a charcoal grill until it is very hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, and dust with the remaining salt, cumin, and chili as the fat starts to render. Cook until the center of the thickest chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A hotter chili flake is a personal shake at the stall. The written amount is medium.
  • Fat from the lamb tail is sometimes interleaved. A fatty shoulder chunk is the home stand-in.

Cantonese Roast Goose

Roast goose with a seasoned skin, five spice, and a savory gravy from the pan.

30 min prep · 2 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Cantonese roast goose is a roast-shop bird, especially associated with Hong Kong shops that also sell char siu and siu yuk. The goose is seasoned, lacquered, and roasted until the skin is glossy and the meat is cooked through. A shop oven is not a home oven. This page roasts a goose, or a large goose leg if a whole bird will not fit, in a home oven and says so; goose is poultry and is cooked to 165°F (74°C); soy sauce in the seasoning brings soy and wheat.

Cultural background

Hong Kong roast shops are the reference for this goose. The home oven is a substitute. There is goose, soy, and wheat. There is no dairy. It is not a festival story this page cannot source. It is a roast-shop technique brought into a kitchen.

Why it tastes this way

Goose is fattier than a chicken, so a hot oven renders the skin and leaves the meat rich. Five spice and soy season that fat. In a shop the bird hangs and the skin dries evenly. At home a rack over a pan collects the fat and still browns the skin, which is the taste you can reach without the shop.

How it is made

Season a goose with five spice and soy, roast it on a rack in a home oven, and cook it until the thigh reaches a poultry temperature.

Authentic touch. Prick the skin, not the meat, so fat can escape and the skin can crisp. Deep holes dry the flesh.

How it is used

Chop it into pieces and eat it with rice and a spoon of the defatted pan juices.

Field notes

  • Cantonese roast goose is a roast-shop bird strongly associated with Hong Kong.
  • Goose is cooked to 165°F (74°C), like other poultry.

Ingredients

  • 1 whole goose, about 8 pounds, or 2 large goose legs
  • 2 tablespoons soy sauce
  • 1 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 4 garlic cloves, crushed

Steps

  1. Prick the skin all over. Rub the goose with soy sauce, five spice, salt, and oil, and put the garlic in the cavity. Refrigerate it uncovered if you have time to dry the skin. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The goose is still raw. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  2. Heat a home oven to 375°F (190°C). Set the goose on a rack. This is a home oven, not a Hong Kong roast shop. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  3. Roast until the thickest part of the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C), away from the bone. Do not serve it rare. Pour off fat as it collects so the pan does not scorch. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.
  4. Rest 10 minutes, then chop. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A whole goose leg is the right size when a whole bird will not fit the oven. Check the temperature at the bone line without touching bone.
  • Maltose in the glaze is traditional in some shops. This recipe uses a soy and five-spice rub.

Korean Samgyeopsal

Plain grilled pork belly, salty at the table, wrapped with lettuce and a dab of sauce.

15 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Samgyeopsal is Korean grilled pork belly, cut in strips and cooked at the table or on a restaurant grill. The meat is often unmarinated, so the fat and the grill do the work, and people wrap the slices in lettuce with garlic, chili, and ssamjang. It is a widespread Korean meal, not a single-restaurant invention this page will pretend to date. The belly is a whole cut and is rested; this recipe does not put wheat or sesame into the meat.

Cultural background

Korean homes and barbecue restaurants grill belly this way, often on a round grill in the middle of the table. The written meat is only pork, salt, and pepper. Sauce and sesame live in the ssamjang, which is a separate bowl. There is no dairy.

Why it tastes this way

Belly browns in its own fat on a hot grill, and an unmarinated slice tastes like pork and fire rather than like a soy glaze. Lettuce, raw garlic, and a fermented chili paste then season each bite differently. That is why the meat is left plain: the table does the seasoning after the grill.

How it is made

Salt pork belly lightly, grill it until it is cooked and rested, and serve it for lettuce wraps.

Authentic touch. Cut the strips so they can be turned with chopsticks or tongs, and pour off fat if the grill floods. A pool of fat boils the meat.

How it is used

Wrap slices in lettuce with garlic and ssamjang. Put rice on the table.

Field notes

  • Samgyeopsal is pork belly grilled in strips, often with no marinade.
  • The seasoning at the table is lettuce, garlic, and a paste, not a glaze on the raw meat.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds pork belly, sliced into grillable strips
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 head green lettuce, leaves separated and washed
  • 4 garlic cloves, sliced, for the table

Steps

  1. Pat the belly dry and season it with salt and pepper. The pork is still raw. Keep the lettuce and garlic separate. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill or a table grill pan until it is hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of each strip reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes. Serve it with lettuce and garlic. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A quick dip in sesame oil and salt is common and would add sesame. The meat here is only salt and pepper.
  • Pork neck, moksal, is the leaner neighbor of this belly.

Korean Beef Galbi

Beef short ribs in a sweet soy marinade with pear, garlic, and sesame.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Galbi is Korean grilled beef short rib. The meat is often cut thin across the bone, marinated with soy sauce, sugar, garlic, sesame, and grated pear or apple, then grilled. It is a restaurant and home barbecue dish. Ordinary soy sauce contains wheat, and sesame oil contains sesame; the rib meat is a whole cut, cooked and rested; it is not pork belly, and it is not the thinly sliced bulgogi from a different cut.

Cultural background

Korean barbecue tables grill galbi over charcoal or on a gas grill set into the table. The marinade has soy, wheat, and sesame. There is no dairy. Pear tenderizes and sweetens the surface.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, sugar, and pear brown quickly on a thin rib, and sesame oil perfumes the fat. Garlic stays sharp. The bone and the thin cut mean a short grill, so the marinade becomes a crust instead of a boiled sauce. A thick uncut roast would not taste like galbi even with the same bowl.

How it is made

Marinate thin beef short ribs in soy, pear, garlic, and sesame, then grill them and rest them.

Authentic touch. If the ribs are thick, ask the butcher to cut them flanken-style, across the bones. Thick English-cut ribs are a different grill.

How it is used

Cut the meat off the bone if you like and eat it with rice, lettuce, and ssamjang.

Field notes

  • Galbi is beef short rib, often cut thin across the bone for the grill.
  • Ordinary soy sauce in the marinade contains wheat.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef short ribs, cut thin across the bone
  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1 ripe pear, grated
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 2 scallions, chopped
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Mix the soy sauce, pear, sugar, garlic, sesame oil, scallions, and pepper. Coat the ribs and refrigerate them. The beef is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill until it is hot. Drain extra marinade off the meat so it does not steam. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the ribs 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork galbi uses the same marinade family on pork ribs and still needs a whole-pork temperature and rest.
  • A gluten-free soy sauce would change the wheat allergen. This uses ordinary soy sauce.

Korean Bulgogi

Very thin beef, sweet and savory with soy, pear, and sesame, seared in a minute.

30 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Bulgogi is thinly sliced Korean beef, marinated with soy, sugar, garlic, sesame, and often grated pear or onion, then grilled or seared. The name means fire meat. Home cooks and restaurants both make it, and the thin cut is the modern everyday form. It is not galbi, which is a short rib, and it is not an ancient court menu this page will invent a date for; ordinary soy sauce contains wheat; the beef is a whole muscle, sliced thin, and it is still rested after it reaches temperature.

Cultural background

Korean households keep this marinade for celebrations and ordinary dinners. There is beef, soy, wheat, and sesame. There is no dairy. The slices are thin enough to cook in a very short time on a hot pan or grill.

Why it tastes this way

A thin slice takes the marinade on almost all of its surface, so the soy and pear taste are in every bite. Sesame oil aromatically browns in the fat. High heat sears it before the juice floods the pan. That wide seasoned surface is why bulgogi tastes sweeter and softer than a thick grilled steak with the same soy.

How it is made

Slice beef thin, marinate it in soy, pear, garlic, and sesame, and grill or sear it quickly, then rest it.

Authentic touch. Freeze the beef for 30 minutes so you can slice it thin. Thick slices are a steak, not bulgogi.

How it is used

Eat it with rice, lettuce, and a little ssamjang.

Field notes

  • Bulgogi is thinly sliced beef. Galbi is the short-rib grill.
  • The sweet soy marinade covers almost the whole surface because the slices are thin.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds beef sirloin, sliced very thin
  • 1/4 cup soy sauce
  • 1/2 ripe pear, grated
  • 1/2 onion, grated
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 3 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds

Taiwanese Grilled Sausage

A slightly sweet pork sausage, grilled, with garlic and five spice.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Taiwanese grilled sausage is a night-market pork sausage, a little sweet, cooked over charcoal and often served on a stick. Taipei night markets made it a standard snack. This recipe mixes fresh pork and cooks it the same day. It uses no curing salt and it is not hung to dry; soy sauce in the mix contains soy and wheat; it is a Taiwanese sausage; it does not need a China label to be itself.

Cultural background

Night-market grills in Taiwan cook these links to order. The written mix is fresh ground pork, sugar, soy sauce, and five spice. There is no dairy. Do not cure it. Cook it through as ground meat.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar and a little soy caramelize on the casing, and five spice perfumes the fat. Grilling beats boiling because the skin browns and the sweetness concentrates. Sticky rice is sometimes tucked into a split sausage. The sausage itself is the sweet pork and the fire.

How it is made

Mix fresh pork with sugar, soy sauce, and five spice, shape links or logs, and grill them the same day until the center is done.

Authentic touch. Form small, even logs if you are not using casings. A huge log will brown before the center is safe.

How it is used

Eat them hot, with garlic and a little raw cabbage if you want a sharp bite beside the sweetness.

Field notes

  • The sweet grilled pork sausage is a Taiwanese night-market food.
  • This mix is cooked the day it is made. It is not a cured sausage.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds ground pork
  • 1 tablespoon soy sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 1 tablespoon rice wine
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1/2 teaspoon five-spice powder
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix the pork, soy sauce, sugar, rice wine, garlic, five spice, salt, and oil. Do not add curing salt. Refrigerate the mix and cook it the same day. The pork is still raw. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  2. Shape eight small logs. Heat a grill to medium. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of each log reaches 160°F (71°C). Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).

Mongolian Khorkhog

Lamb cooked in a closed pot with onion, carrot, and potato, salty and rich.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Khorkhog is a Mongolian lamb dish cooked in a closed container with vegetables. The traditional method heats stones and puts those stones in the pot with the meat. This recipe does not do that. It uses a heavy covered pot on the stove; do not heat found river rocks for this dish; rocks from a river can hold water and can crack or explode when they are put in a fire; stones are mentioned here only as the traditional method you are not reproducing with found rocks; the lamb is a whole cut and is cooked at least to a safe temperature.

Cultural background

Mongolian cooks make khorkhog for gatherings, in a large metal churn or a heavy pot in the older method. The home version on this page is the covered pot, without stones. There is lamb, onion, carrot, and potato. There is no wheat and no dairy in the pot.

Why it tastes this way

A closed pot keeps lamb fat with the vegetables, so the potato and carrot taste of the meat and the lamb stays moist. Salt is the seasoning. The traditional stones were a way to carry heat inside the pot. A heavy lid on a stove holds that steam without the risk of a wet rock exploding. The dish is the lamb and the vegetables, not the hazard.

How it is made

Salt lamb and vegetables in a heavy covered pot and cook them until the lamb is at least at a safe temperature and tender. Do not add rocks.

Authentic touch. Use a heavy pot with a tight lid and do not lift it every few minutes. The steam is the cook.

How it is used

Eat the lamb, potato, carrot, and onion together, with the juices in the pot.

Field notes

  • Traditional khorkhog uses heated stones in the pot. This recipe uses a heavy covered pot and no stones.
  • Do not heat found river rocks. They can hold water and can crack or explode.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds boneless lamb shoulder, in large chunks
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 large onion, cut in wedges
  • 3 carrots, cut in chunks
  • 1.5 pounds potatoes, cut in chunks
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Put the lamb, salt, pepper, onion, carrot, potato, and water in a heavy pot with a tight lid. The lamb is still raw. Do not add stones of any kind. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Cover the pot and set it over medium heat on a stove. This is the home method. The traditional stone method is not what you are doing, and found river rocks can explode. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the center of the thickest lamb chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). You may continue until the lamb is tender if the temperature stays at or above that mark. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes in the pot with the lid off the heat. Serve the meat and vegetables. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Korean Ssamjang

Salty, hot, and savory, from soybean paste, chili paste, garlic, and sesame.

10 min prep · 0 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Ssamjang is the Korean paste set on the table for ssam, the lettuce wraps that hold grilled pork or beef. It is stirred from doenjang, a fermented soybean paste, and gochujang, a fermented chili paste, with garlic, sesame oil, and a little onion. Many commercial gochujang brands contain wheat. The paste is not cooked; it is a condiment for the grill, not the meat itself.

Cultural background

Korean homes mix ssamjang by the spoon for a barbecue meal. There is soy, wheat, and sesame. There is no meat, dairy, egg, or honey in this bowl, so it is vegetarian. Keep it cold. It is not a canned preserve.

Why it tastes this way

Doenjang is salty and deeply savory, and gochujang adds chili and a little sweetness. Sesame oil rounds the ferments, and raw garlic keeps the paste sharp against grilled fat. On a Korean table it seasons a plain slice of belly or neck after the fire, which is why those meats can be cooked with only salt.

How it is made

Stir doenjang, gochujang, garlic, onion, and sesame oil into a thick paste.

Authentic touch. Stir it thick enough to sit on a lettuce leaf. A watery mix runs off the meat and seasons the table instead.

How it is used

Put a little on a lettuce leaf with grilled meat and rice.

Field notes

  • Ssamjang is the wrap paste for Korean grilled meat, made from doenjang and gochujang.
  • Many gochujang brands contain wheat, so this paste is not assumed gluten-free.

Ingredients

  • 1/4 cup doenjang (fermented soybean paste)
  • 2 tablespoons gochujang (chili paste; many brands contain wheat)
  • 2 garlic cloves, minced
  • 2 tablespoons minced onion
  • 1 tablespoon sesame oil
  • 1 teaspoon toasted sesame seeds
  • 1 tablespoon water

Steps

  1. Stir the doenjang, gochujang, garlic, onion, sesame oil, sesame seeds, and water into a thick paste. There is no meat in the bowl.
  2. Taste for salt. Doenjang is already salty, so do not add salt first.
  3. Cover it and refrigerate it until the meat is ready. Do not leave garlic sitting in oil at room temperature.
  4. Serve cold or cool with lettuce wraps. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a few days. This is not a canning recipe.

These ingredients are vegetables, bread, or a sauce. Keep them clean, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. This is not a canning recipe.

  • More gochujang makes it hotter. This balance is medium.

Yakitori Tare

A sweet, salty soy glaze with mirin, reduced until it clings to a skewer.

5 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 8 · about 1 cup

History

Tare is the sweet soy glaze brushed on yakitori. Shop pots are sometimes reduced again and again in the same pan. This recipe makes a fresh tare from soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar, with no chicken bones in the pot, so the written sauce has no meat. Some restaurant tare is simmered with chicken, and that would be a different bowl; ordinary soy sauce contains wheat; it is a glaze, not a table salsa.

Cultural background

Tokyo yakitori shops brush tare on in the last moments so the sugar does not burn. This pan has soy and wheat, and no meat, dairy, egg, or sesame. It is vegetarian. Cool it and keep it cold. It is not a canned sauce.

Why it tastes this way

Soy, mirin, and sugar reduce into a lacquer that tastes sweet and salty at once, and a little bitterness from the grill sticks to it. Brushed on late, it glazes the chicken instead of boiling it. A shop pot that has held years of drippings is deeper. A fresh pot is still the same idea: soy and sugar meeting charcoal.

How it is made

Simmer soy sauce, mirin, sake, and sugar until the glaze clings, then cool it.

Authentic touch. Reduce it until it coats a spoon. A thin soy splash runs off the skewer and flares in the coals.

How it is used

Brush it on grilled chicken in the last minute, or serve a spoon of it beside salted yakitori.

Field notes

  • Yakitori tare is a sweet soy glaze. Salt is the other usual finish.
  • This pot has no chicken in it. Some shop sauces are reduced with meat and would not be vegetarian.

Ingredients

  • 1/2 cup soy sauce
  • 1/4 cup mirin
  • 2 tablespoons sake
  • 2 tablespoons sugar
  • 1/4 cup water

Steps

  1. Stir the soy sauce, mirin, sake, sugar, and water in a small pan. There is no meat in this tare.
  2. Simmer it over medium heat until it thickens enough to coat a spoon, about 10 to 15 minutes.
  3. Cool it. Do not brush a boiling glaze onto food you are about to serve without letting the chicken finish at a safe temperature first.
  4. Refrigerate leftovers and use them within a week. This is not a canning recipe.
