Abruzzo Lamb Arrosticini

Little lamb cubes with salt and the taste of a narrow coal fire.

30 min prep · 10 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Arrosticini are small cubes of lamb on thin skewers from Abruzzo, cooked over a long, narrow coal trough so the sticks sit at the edges and the meat hangs in the heat. They came from the mountain pastoral kitchen and are now a regional grill food far beyond the sheepfolds. Salt is the seasoning. The cubes stay small so they cook in minutes.

Cultural background

Abruzzo cooks, from mountain towns to roadside stands, make these by the dozen. They are lamb, not pork spiedini. A regular grill works if you keep the heat high and the skewers close together.

Why it tastes this way

Small pieces of lamb brown on all sides before the inside dries, which is why the skewer is short and the cube is small. A coal trough puts every stick in the same heat. Salt and muttony fat are the flavor. Rosemary is optional and easy to overdo on meat this size.

How it is made

Thread small lamb cubes on thin skewers and grill them quickly over coals, then rest briefly.

Authentic touch. Cut the lamb smaller than a souvlaki cube, about a half inch, and salt just before the fire. Serve them as they come off, in batches.

How it is used

By the handful, with a simple salad. They are eaten off the stick.

Field notes

  • Arrosticini are small lamb skewers from Abruzzo, cooked over a narrow bed of coals.
  • The cubes are kept small so a batch cooks in a few minutes.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb boneless lamb, cut in 1/2 inch cubes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • Black pepper
  • 16 thin metal or wooden skewers

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb with oil. Thread three or four cubes on each thin skewer. Salt them.
  2. Heat a narrow coal bed or a hot grill. Lay the skewers so the meat is over the heat.
  3. Grill, turning often, until the cubes reach at least 145°F (63°C). Give them a 3-minute rest.
  4. Pepper lightly and eat off the stick. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A mix of slightly fattier pieces so the lean cubes do not dry.
  • A standard grill with the skewers packed in a row.

Adana Kebab

Hand-minced lamb, hot pepper, and tail fat, smoky from a close charcoal fire.

30 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 wide skewers

History

Adana kebab is a hand-minced spicy lamb kebab from the city of Adana, pressed onto wide flat skewers. Hot red pepper and lamb fat are part of the mix, not optional decorations. It is a distinct kebab from the milder Urfa version. The meat is ground, so it is cooked through. This home mix uses a knife or a coarse grind and does not claim a guild certificate.

Cultural background

Adana's kebab cooks and the home cooks who follow them make this spicy skewer. It is not Urfa kebab, not chicken shish, and not a stew. The heat is the city's signature.

Why it tastes this way

Fat from the lamb bastes the mince as it sits over charcoal, and hot pepper blooms in that fat. Hand mincing leaves a coarser bite than a fine supermarket grind. A wide skewer keeps the soft meat from falling apart. The result is smoky, rich, and hot, which is why it is not interchangeable with a mild cousin.

How it is made

Knead spicy minced lamb onto wide skewers and grill over hot coals until the center is cooked through.

Authentic touch. Work the mince until it sticks to a flat skewer. Keep a little fat in the mix or the kebab will dry before it browns.

How it is used

With sliced onion, parsley, and grilled tomato. Sumac can be sprinkled at the table.

Field notes

  • Adana kebab is hand-minced lamb with hot pepper, formed on a wide skewer.
  • It is a different kebab from Urfa, which is the milder cousin.

Ingredients

  • 1 1/2 lb ground lamb, coarse
  • 3 oz lamb fat, finely chopped
  • 2 tablespoons hot red pepper flakes
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 4 wide flat metal skewers

Steps

  1. Knead the lamb, fat, pepper flakes, salt, and black pepper until the paste is sticky. Chill 15 minutes.
  2. Wet your hands and press the meat along the wide skewers in a long sausage shape.
  3. Grill over hot coals, turning carefully, until the center reaches 160°F (71°C). Ground lamb is not served pink.
  4. Rest a minute and serve with onion. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook ground meat, including fresh sausages, to 160°F (71°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A coarse grinder if you cannot hand-mince, with the same pepper and fat.
  • A less fiery amount of pepper for children at the table, still not the Urfa recipe.

Afghan Lamb Tikka

Lamb chunks with salt, cumin, and a little chili, grilled over charcoal.

25 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Afghan tikka is lamb in chunks on skewers, seasoned with salt, fat, cumin, and often a little chili, and cooked over charcoal. It is a Central Asian grill from Afghanistan, cooked in Kabul and in other cities by Afghan grill cooks. The seasoning is spare next to a yogurt-heavy North Indian marinade. The meat is whole muscle and is rested after it reaches temperature.

Cultural background

Afghan cooks grill this lamb over charcoal, often with naan nearby in the market. This recipe does not put bread or yogurt into the meat. It is a Kabul-style skewer of lamb, from Afghanistan.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat, salt, and cumin are enough when the charcoal is hot. A little chili warms the crust without covering the meat. The dry climate of the plateau is not a flavor by itself. The flavor is the lamb and the fire, which is why the marinade stays short.

How it is made

Season lamb chunks with salt, cumin, and chili, skewer them, and grill them over a hot fire, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Salt the lamb early enough for the salt to dissolve on the meat, and keep the chunks similar in size.

How it is used

Eat the lamb with onion, a squeeze of lemon, and cilantro if you want a green bite.

Field notes

  • Afghan tikka is lamb on skewers, seasoned simply and cooked over charcoal.
  • The meat is whole chunks, rested after grilling.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds boneless lamb leg, cut in 1.5-inch chunks
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1/2 teaspoon chili flakes
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 small onion, sliced, for serving
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb with salt, cumin, chili flakes, and oil. Refrigerate it for 20 minutes. The lamb is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the chunks on skewers. Heat a charcoal grill until the coals are hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of the thickest chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes. Serve with onion and lemon. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Some cooks add a spoon of oil and crushed coriander. The salt and cumin stay.
  • Chicken tikka in Afghan shops would need a poultry temperature. This is lamb.

Armenian Khorovats

Lamb with onion, salt, and a clean charcoal char.

25 min prep · 16 min cook · Serves 4 · 4 skewers

History

Khorovats is the Armenian grill of meat on skewers, cooked for weekends and gatherings in Yerevan and in the regions. Pork and lamb are both common. This page picks lamb and says plainly that pork is also common. The marinade is usually onion and salt rather than a long list of spices. Vegetables are often grilled beside the meat. It is a Caucasus fire, related to neighboring skewers and still its own table.

Cultural background

Armenian hosts tend the khorovats while guests are already together. This version is lamb. It is not a dolma and not the Russian home shashlik described separately.

Why it tastes this way

Onion juice tenderizes the surface and smells sweet as it cooks off. Lamb fat over charcoal is the main flavor, which is why the spice list stays short. Grilled pepper and tomato on the same fire pick up the same smoke and make the plate feel complete. The choice of lamb or pork changes the fat, not the method.

How it is made

Skewer lamb cubes, grill them over charcoal, and rest them. Pork can be cooked the same way as another common choice.

Authentic touch. Salt the lamb and leave most of the onion on the side to eat raw. Burnt onion on the skewer turns bitter.

How it is used

With grilled peppers, tomato, and raw onion.

Field notes

  • Khorovats is Armenia's skewer grill, and both pork and lamb are common.
  • This recipe uses lamb and treats pork as the other usual meat, not as a mistake.

Ingredients

  • 2 lb boneless lamb, cut in 1 1/2 inch cubes
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon oil
  • 4 metal skewers

Steps

  1. Toss the lamb with onion, salt, pepper, and oil. Rest 20 minutes, then thread the meat on skewers.
  2. Grill over hot charcoal, turning so the faces brown evenly.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) and give it a 3-minute rest. Pork, if you use it instead, follows the same temperature and rest.
  4. Serve with onion and grilled vegetables. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • Pork shoulder cubes, still a whole cut, if that is the meat on hand.
  • Eggplant and pepper grilled beside the skewers.

Balinese Sate Lilit

Minced fish and shrimp with coconut, lime leaf, and chili, grilled on lemongrass.

35 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Sate lilit is a Balinese skewer of minced seafood pressed around a stalk, often lemongrass, and grilled. Coconut, chili, lime leaf, and shallot are mixed into the mince. It is temple-offering food and also everyday food in Balinese homes and warungs. The fish and shrimp are cooked until opaque; this is a seafood satay, not the pork spit of babi guling.

Cultural background

Balinese cooks shape this mince by hand around the stalk. There is fish and shrimp. Coconut milk is not dairy. There is no peanut and no wheat in the mix. It is a different Balinese fire from the spit-roasted pig.

Why it tastes this way

Coconut fat keeps the mince moist, and lime leaf and chili perfume it. Lemongrass used as the skewer shares a little of its oil with the paste as it grills. The result is aromatic and richer than a plain fish cake, which is why the stalk is part of the dish and not only a stick.

How it is made

Mix fish and shrimp with coconut and spice, press the paste onto lemongrass stalks, and grill until the seafood is opaque.

Authentic touch. Press the mince on firmly and oil your hands. A loose coating falls into the grate.

How it is used

Eat the skewers hot, with rice and a little extra lime.

Field notes

  • Sate lilit is minced seafood pressed onto a stalk, a Balinese grill.
  • Lemongrass often serves as the skewer, not only as a flavor in the bowl.

Ingredients

  • 1 pound firm white fish, minced
  • 1/2 pound shrimp, peeled and minced
  • 1/3 cup coconut milk
  • 4 lime leaves, minced
  • 2 shallots, minced
  • 2 hot chilies, minced
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon ground turmeric
  • 8 lemongrass stalks, for skewers

Steps

  1. Mix the fish, shrimp, coconut milk, lime leaf, shallot, chili, salt, and turmeric. Keep it cold. The seafood is still raw. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  2. Press the paste around the upper part of each lemongrass stalk. Oil your hands so it sticks. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  3. Grill over medium heat, turning carefully, until the fish and shrimp are opaque and the center reaches 145°F (63°C). Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.
  4. Serve hot. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook the fish and the shrimp to 145°F (63°C), until the flesh is opaque.

Cook fish to 145°F (63°C), until it is opaque and flakes.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • All fish, with no shrimp, is a common batch and would drop the shellfish allergen.
  • A hotter chili is used for some ceremonies and home meals. This batch is medium.

Bihari Kebab

Thin beef with mustard, mustard oil, ginger, and chili, grilled on skewers.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Bihari kebab is beef, often cut thin, marinated with mustard and mustard oil and grilled on skewers. The name points to Bihar, and the kebab is also cooked in North Indian cities by cooks who carried that seasoning with them. Mustard is the marker, more than a yogurt sauce. The meat is whole muscle, so it is rested after it reaches temperature; it is not a minced seekh.

Cultural background

This is skewered beef in a Bihari mustard marinade. Mustard and mustard oil are in the paste. There is no yogurt and no wheat. It is a grill kebab, not a stew.

Why it tastes this way

Mustard oil is sharp and aromatic, and ground mustard clings to thin beef. Chili heat sits on top of that. A hot skewer browns the oil instead of boiling it. The result is pungent in a way a yogurt boti is not, which is why the two kebabs can share a street and still be different.

How it is made

Marinate thin beef in mustard, mustard oil, ginger, and chili, then grill it on skewers and rest it.

Authentic touch. Slice the beef thin across the grain. Thick pieces hide the mustard in the middle and take too long on the skewer.

How it is used

Eat it with onion and lemon. The heat is meant to be hot.

Field notes

  • Mustard and mustard oil are the seasoning that marks Bihari kebab.
  • The beef is kept in pieces on skewers, not minced.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds beef sirloin, sliced thin across the grain
  • 2 tablespoons mustard oil
  • 1 tablespoon ground mustard seed
  • 1 tablespoon grated ginger
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon chili powder
  • 1.5 teaspoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon lemon juice

Steps

  1. Mix the mustard oil, ground mustard, ginger, garlic, chili, salt, and lemon. Coat the beef and refrigerate it. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. The beef is still raw. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Thread the slices on skewers without folding them into thick bundles. Heat the grill. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill until the center of the thickest slice reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the beef 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). Home cooking, not a professional food-safety instruction.

Variations

  • A little raw papaya is sometimes used to soften the meat. It is left out of this batch.
  • Green chili can replace some of the red chili. Keep the mustard either way.

Bolivian Pacumutu

Salted beef skewers with a browned edge, eaten with yuca.

20 min prep · 25 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Pacumutu is a grilled meat skewer served in Santa Cruz and the Bolivian Chaco. The meat is usually beef, cut in large pieces and cooked over a hot fire, with yuca beside it. This page keeps the dish regional and does not invent a single origin story for the name. It is a plains and lowland grill, not the Andean earth oven and not an Argentine parrilla.

Cultural background

The written recipe is beef on skewers, salted, cooked as a whole cut. Yuca is a pairing you boil separately. A hot sauce at the table is optional and is not mixed into the meat.

Why it tastes this way

Large cubes take a crust on the outside and stay juicy if you stop at the whole-muscle temperature. Salt is the seasoning, so the taste is beef and charcoal. Yuca, mild and starchy, is the contrast people in Santa Cruz expect, rather than a wheat tortilla.

How it is made

Salt beef cubes, skewer them, and grill them to a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Cut the cubes large and even. Tiny pieces will dry before they taste like this skewer.

How it is used

Eat the skewers with boiled yuca, rice, and a simple onion salsa.

Field notes

  • Pacumutu is a grilled meat skewer of Santa Cruz and the Bolivian Chaco.
  • Yuca, not a wheat tortilla, is the usual starch beside it.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef steak, in 1 1/2 inch cubes
  • 1 teaspoon coarse salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Metal skewers

Steps

  1. Toss the beef with oil, salt, and pepper. It is raw and is not ready to eat. Thread it onto skewers. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a grill until the coals are hot. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of the thickest cube reads 145°F (63°C). Rest the skewers 3 minutes.
  4. The beef should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Serve it with yuca. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

  • A thicker piece cooked beside the fire, rather than over it, is still this dish if the temperature is met.

Lamb chunks with yogurt, chili, and ginger, charred at the edges.

30 min prep · 18 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Boti kebab is lamb cut in chunks and threaded on skewers, not minced. It sits in the same North Indian kebab tradition as seekh, and the difference is the cut of meat. Yogurt, ginger, and chili are the usual marinade in restaurant and home grills. The chunks are whole muscle, so they are cooked and rested like other whole lamb, not like ground meat.

Cultural background

This is cubed lamb from a leg or shoulder, grilled by North Indian kebab cooks. Yogurt makes it a dairy dish. It is not seekh, which is mince, and it is not a whole roasted leg.

Why it tastes this way

A chunk of lamb keeps a juicy center while the yogurt marinade spots and browns. Ginger and chili season the surface. On charcoal the fat at the edge crisps. That is a different bite from minced seekh, where the spice is mixed all the way through.

How it is made

Marinate lamb chunks in yogurt and spice, skewer them, and grill them to a whole-muscle temperature with a rest.

Authentic touch. Cut the cubes the same size so they finish together. A thick cube next to a thin one will leave one of them overcooked.

How it is used

Eat the chunks with onion, a green herb chutney if you have one made fresh, and lemon.

Field notes

  • Boti is cut lamb on a skewer. Seekh is the minced kebab in the same grill tradition.
  • Whole lamb chunks are rested after they leave the fire.

  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

  2. Thread the chunks on skewers. Heat a grill or a tandoor until it is hot. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of the thickest chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes before serving. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Brazilian Chicken Heart Skewers

Salted chicken hearts with a browned outside and a fully cooked center.

20 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Chicken hearts, coração, are a standard skewer at Brazilian churrascarias. They are poultry offal, salted and grilled as everyday food. Because they are poultry, this page cooks them to 165°F (74°C), the ground-poultry temperature used here for hearts. History

Picanha is the rump cap of the beef, grilled with the fat cap left on and with coarse salt as the seasoning. It is a defining cut of Brazilian churrasco, strongly associated with the south and now cooked across the country. It is not carne de sol, the salted beef of the Northeast. Cultural background

The fat cap is not trimmed off. It bastes the meat. A long spice paste is not what makes the cut picanha. Steaks or a C-shaped skewer are both used. This page uses a skewer and allows a hot grill.

Why it tastes this way

The fat cap melts over the lean cap of the rump, so each slice tastes richer than a lean steak from the same animal. Coarse salt stays on the surface and crackles. Southern Brazilian churrasco is a wood or charcoal fire, and that dry heat browns the fat instead of steaming it.

How it is made

Salt the fat-on rump cap and grill it on a skewer, fat toward the heat, to a whole-beef temperature.

Authentic touch. Score the fat cap in a crosshatch, without cutting into the meat, so the salt has somewhere to sit and the fat can render.

How it is used

Slice it against the grain and eat it with farofa, a vinaigrette relish, and rice. Those sides have their own pages.

Vietnamese Nem Nuong

Sweet grilled pork mince on skewers, with garlic and a little sugar.

30 min prep · 15 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Nem nướng is grilled ground pork, shaped on skewers or as small logs, and it is especially associated with south-central cities such as Nha Trang. The mince is slightly sweet, with garlic and fish sauce, and it is wrapped at the table with herbs and rice paper. It is street and feast food, not a dried cured sausage. Ground pork has to be cooked through.

Cultural background

Cooks in Nha Trang and other southern kitchens grill this mince to order. Fish sauce contains fish. There is no wheat in the meat. Rice paper at the table is a wrapper, not a wheat tortilla, when it is made from rice.

Why it tastes this way

Sugar browns on the skewer and keeps the pork from tasting plain. Fish sauce and garlic season the mince all the way through, unlike a sauce poured on at the end. Herbs and rice paper make each bite fresh against that sweet grill.

How it is made

Season ground pork, shape it on skewers, and grill it until the center reaches the temperature for ground meat.

Authentic touch. Keep the logs small. A thick roll of ground pork browns outside and stays under temperature in the center.

How it is used

Wrap pieces in rice paper with herbs and lettuce, and dip them in a fish-sauce dip.

Field notes

  • Nem nướng is grilled ground pork, strongly associated with Nha Trang.
  • It is cooked fresh. It is not a cured sausage.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds ground pork
  • 2 tablespoons fish sauce
  • 1 tablespoon sugar
  • 4 garlic cloves, minced
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon salt

Steps

  1. Mix the pork, fish sauce, sugar, garlic, pepper, oil, and salt. Refrigerate the mince. Do not leave garlic in oil at room temperature. It is still raw. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  2. Shape the mince around flat skewers in small logs. Heat a grill. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  3. Grill, turning, until the center of each log reaches 160°F (71°C). Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).
  4. Rest 3 minutes. Serve hot and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook ground meat to 160°F (71°C).

Xinjiang Lamb Skewers

Lamb skewers with salt, cumin, and dried chili, grilled over a street fire.

20 min prep · 12 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

In Xinjiang, Uyghur street grills cook small lamb skewers over charcoal and dust them with cumin and dried chili. The seasoning is salt, cumin, and chili, put on as the meat cooks. This page is about that street food. It is not a political essay, and it does not treat the cooks as a symbol of anything except the grill they run; the lamb is whole muscle and is rested.

Cultural background

Uyghur grill cooks in cities such as Ürümqi sell these skewers from small fires. There is no yogurt marinade and no wheat on the meat. The fat, the cumin, and the chili are the dish.

Why it tastes this way

Cumin and dried chili bloom in lamb fat over a hot fire, which is why the spices are added to the cooking meat and not only stirred into a raw paste. Salt keeps the lamb direct. Small pieces char at the edge and stay juicy inside. A sweet glaze would hide the cumin that marks this street grill.

How it is made

Skewer small pieces of lamb, grill them hot, and season them with salt, cumin, and chili as they cook, then rest them.

Authentic touch. Sprinkle the cumin and chili in more than one pass while the skewers turn. One heavy dump falls off into the coals.

How it is used

Eat the skewers as soon as they have rested, while the cumin is still fragrant.

Field notes

In Yangon, street grills sell small pork skewers seasoned with turmeric, garlic, and chili and cooked over charcoal. This page uses that plain description. Yangon street cooks thread the pork and grill it to order. Turmeric colors the meat. There is no wheat, dairy, or peanut in this mix. The name on this page is a description of the food and the city, not a fake classical title.

Why it tastes this way

Turmeric, garlic, and chili make a direct, savory crust, and pork fat browns over a small charcoal fire. The skewers are small, so the seasoning stays on the surface where you taste it. A long sauce would turn them into a different dish. The street version is the spice and the grill.

How it is made

Toss pork chunks with turmeric, garlic, and chili, grill them on skewers, and rest them.
