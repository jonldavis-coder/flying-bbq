United States

Who settled the flavor

Regional barbecue in the United States is a creole food. No one people owns the whole map. This note stays with what can be said plainly, and it links to the stops.

A map, not a single origin

United States barbecue is regional because the country was settled, enslaved, and immigrated into in different ways. German-speaking butchers, English and Scots-Irish farmers, enslaved West and Central Africans, Mexican and Tejano pit cooks, and Native nations did not contribute the same thing, and they did not contribute it in the same decade.

This page names those strands and points at the stops. It does not hand a modern recipe to a nation that did not write it down that way. Where a story is widely repeated and still unsettled, it says so.

Fire before the word

People in the Americas cooked with fire, smoke, and earth ovens long before English had the word barbecue. Practices were not the same from the Eastern Woodlands to the Plains to the Northwest Coast to the Caribbean. Drying meat and fish, roasting on wooden frames, and pit cooking are documented broadly. A single “Native American barbecue sauce” is not.

The English word comes through Spanish barbacoa, from a Taíno word for a framework of sticks. That is a Caribbean and Spanish colonial path into English, not a claim that one Taíno recipe became pulled pork. South Texas barbacoa and cochinita pibil are later, specific foods on that wider map. Salmon cooked with cedar is a Northwest Coast fire, not a Southern pit.

The Carolinas are more than one sauce

Eastern North Carolina whole-hog cooking is chopped pork with a vinegar-and-pepper sauce, sharp enough to cut fat. The Piedmont, around Lexington, cooks shoulder and reddens the dip with a little tomato. The South Carolina Midlands are known for a mustard sauce. A vinegar habit fits British and Scots-Irish settlers. The mustard style is widely linked to German settlement in the Midlands. A single inventor for either sauce is not something this journal can verify, so the pages describe the regions instead of crowning one cook.

Read Eastern North Carolina whole hog, Lexington shoulder, and Midlands mustard pork as neighbors, not as stages of one recipe.

Enslaved cooks and the public pit

On Southern plantations, enslaved people did the work of the pits: the wood, the night watch, the whole animals. Pepper, vinegar, and whole-animal cooking have West and Central African parallels, and Black cooks carried that knowledge. After emancipation, African American pitmasters built much of the public barbecue business in the Carolinas, Memphis, Texas, and Kansas City. That is labor history, not a garnish.

It is also not a claim that one African nation “invented” American barbecue and everyone else copied it. The food is creole. The pages for Memphis dry ribs, whole hog, and Central Texas brisket say who tended the fire in those places without turning people into a flavor adjective.

Central Texas meat markets

In the late nineteenth century, German and Czech immigrants in towns such as Lockhart, Luling, and Elgin opened meat markets and smoked beef, including the tough chest cut that became brisket, over post oak. Sausage was part of that butcher’s case. The seasoning that defines the style now is salt and coarse black pepper. Sauce, if a market has it, is not the point.

African American pitmasters worked in those markets and in their own businesses. Central Texas barbecue is both a Central European butcher tradition and a Black Texas craft. Brisket, smoked sausage, and the salt-and-pepper rub are the stops. South Texas barbacoa de cabeza is a different fire, closer to Mexican pit cooking than to a Lockhart market.

Crossroads, white sauce, and mutton

Kansas City sat on rail lines that moved people and meat. Henry Perry, a Black cook from the Memphis area, is the person local history credits with selling barbecue there in the early twentieth century. The thick, sweet, smoky sauce associated with the city came to define restaurant plates. Burnt ends are the point of the brisket, cubed and returned to the pit. See burnt ends.

Memphis is pork, ribs and shoulder, dry-rubbed or sauced, tied to Black neighborhoods and to restaurants that made the city a barbecue capital. Alabama’s white sauce is a documented restaurant invention: Big Bob Gibson in Decatur, in the 1920s, mayonnaise and vinegar for smoked chicken. Owensboro, Kentucky, made mutton its barbecue, closely tied to Catholic parish picnics. Those are three different settlements of flavor, not one Southern sauce with nicknames.

The stops are Memphis dry ribs, Alabama white-sauce chicken, and Owensboro mutton.

Later arrivals

The map did not freeze in 1920. Santa Maria tri-tip belongs to the California central coast and its ranch and vaquero beef culture. Lebanese shawarma in central Mexico became tacos al pastor. Korean table grilling and Texas brisket now share American cities without becoming the same dish. Those are in the passport as their own stops, not as footnotes that erase the older regions.

Appalachian stops in this issue are the ones that can be tied to a real pot or a real smoke: hickory shoulder, sorghum chops, a purchased country-ham steak, ramp-season trout, vinegar chicken, chow-chow, and soup beans. Scots-Irish and English settlers, German-speaking families, Black Appalachian cooks, and Cherokee and other Native nations of the mountains do not all get the same recipe. The book says which food is whose.
