Fire 101

A short course in the fire itself: heat, smoke, the stall, safe temperatures, wood, and earth ovens.

What this journal means by barbecue

Barbecue here means food cooked with live fire, coals, smoke, or a pit, plus the rubs, mops, sauces, and sides that belong on that table. A weeknight sheet-pan dinner is not a stop. A sauce simmered with no fire is not a stop unless it is the mop or dip a real fire tradition uses.

Low and slow is one method. So are a hot grill, a skewer, a spit, a tandoor, and an earth oven. They are not the same craft. The page names the one the recipe uses.

Heat, smoke, and the stall

Direct heat browns the surface fast. Indirect heat, with the food away from the coals or beside them, lets a large cut cook through before the outside carbonizes. A covered cooker holds heat. An open grate does not.

Smoke taste comes from wood that is burning cleanly, not from smoldering wet chips that make a bitter cloud. A little smoke over a long cook is plenty.

The stall is the stretch, often around 150–170°F (65–77°C) inside a brisket or pork shoulder, when surface evaporation cools the meat almost as fast as the cooker heats it. It is physics, not a broken thermometer. Wrapping in paper or foil shortens it. Leaving the bark unwrapped keeps it drier. Either choice is a cook’s decision, and the page says what is usual for that stop.

Temperatures this journal uses

These are home-cooking targets used throughout the passport, in Fahrenheit and Celsius. They are not a substitute for a tested canning guide or for a local food-safety officer.

Poultry, including ground poultry and chicken hearts: 165°F (74°C). Ground meat such as beef, pork, and lamb sausages: 160°F (71°C). Whole cuts of beef, pork, veal, and lamb: 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest. Fish: 145°F (63°C).

Pulled pork and sliced brisket are taken hotter than the safety line, usually about 195–205°F (90–96°C), because collagen needs that time and heat to soften. The safety line has already been passed. Rest large cuts before slicing. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and reheat them to 165°F (74°C).

A tradition that serves beef rare, such as a Tuscan steak, is described honestly. The recipe still gives the 145°F rest guidance and lets the cook decide.

Wood, coal, and climate

People burn what grows nearby or what the market sells. Post oak in Central Texas, hickory in much of the inland South, mesquite in West Texas and northern Mexico, pimento wood in Jamaican jerk, vine cuttings in Rioja, binchotan or ordinary charcoal for yakitori. The page names the traditional wood and a workable substitute.

Climate changes the cook. A humid day stalls a brisket longer. A dry wind dries a goat on an open sajji fire. An earth oven is a way to hold heat in the ground when a village is cooking for a crowd.

Rubs, mops, and sauces

A rub is dry seasoning on the surface. A mop or spritz is a thin liquid brushed or sprayed during the cook. A sauce may go on at the end, on the side, or not at all. Central Texas brisket is famous for skipping the sauce. Eastern North Carolina pork is famous for a vinegar sauce that is the seasoning.

Do not reuse a marinade that has touched raw meat unless you boil it. Sugar burns. Put sweet glazes on late, over a cooler part of the fire.

Earth ovens are not one recipe

A Hawaiian imu, a Māori hāngi, a Fijian lovo, a Yucatecan pib, and a Mexican barbacoa pit all cook with heat held in the ground or in a covered pit. They are related techniques and separate cultures. A home oven or a covered grill in this journal is labeled as a home version. It is not a ceremony and not a claim that the cook has reproduced the original oven.
