Mexico City Al Pastor

Next stop: Sonoran Carne Asada · continuing the voyage

Pronounced al pahs-TOR

Chile-stained pork with pineapple, crisp at the edge and juicy where the slice is thin.

40 min prep · 50 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

In the twentieth century, Lebanese shawarma in central Mexico became the vertical trompo of al pastor. Taquerías in Mexico City stacked pork, stained with achiote or guajillo, and set pineapple on the spit. The home cook who grills thin slices on a grate or a sheet is not running a vertical spit unless a spit is actually in the kitchen. The seasoning can still be the same.

Cultural background

Al pastor is a Mexico City taco of marinated pork and pineapple. It is not Yucatecan cochinita, and it is not Lebanese shawarma served as lamb. The written steps are the home grill, labeled as such.

Why it tastes this way

Guajillo and achiote give the pork a dried-chile color and a mild heat, and pineapple adds acid and sugar at the edge of the slice. The trompo bastes the meat in its own fat as it turns. A home grill still browns thin pork quickly, which is the part of the taste you can reach without the spit.

How it is made

Marinate thin pork in guajillo and achiote, then roast it on a vertical spit if you have one. Otherwise grill or sheet-cook the slices and call that a home version, not a trompo.

Authentic touch

Slice the pork thin before it cooks. Thick chunks will not taste like pastor even if the chile paste is right.

How it is used

Chop the cooked pork with a little grilled pineapple and eat it in small corn tortillas with onion and cilantro.

Field notes

  • The vertical trompo is a Levantine shawarma spit adapted in central Mexico in the twentieth century.
  • Pineapple on the pastor spit is a Mexican addition to that spit method.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds boneless pork shoulder, sliced about 1/4 inch thick
  • 4 dried guajillo chiles, stemmed and seeded
  • 2 tablespoons ground achiote (annatto)
  • 1/2 cup pineapple juice
  • 4 garlic cloves
  • 1 teaspoon ground cumin
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 cup fresh pineapple chunks
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil

Steps

  1. Toast the guajillos in a dry pan until fragrant, cover them with hot water for 15 minutes, and blend them with achiote, pineapple juice, garlic, cumin, and salt. Coat the raw pork. It is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. If you have a vertical spit, stack the pork, oil the outside, and roast it, shaving slices as the face browns. If you do not, this is not a vertical spit: grill the slices or lay them on a hot sheet. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook every slice until the thickest piece reads 145°F (63°C). Grill the pineapple until it browns. Rest the pork 3 minutes.
  4. Chop the rested pork. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Eat it in tortillas the same day, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Achiote alone, without guajillo, is a common paste. This recipe uses both.
  • If you own a vertical spit, stack the slices and cook them there. That is the only version that is a trompo.

Pairings

  • corn tortillas
  • cilantro and onion
  • lime
  • salsa verde
