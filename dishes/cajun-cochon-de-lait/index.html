Cajun Cochon de Lait

Pronounced koh-SHOHN duh LAY

Peppery roasted pork with garlic, paprika, and a crisp edge.

History

Cochon de lait is a Cajun pig roast, a community fire often turned on a spit for fundraisers and family gatherings in south Louisiana. Cajun cooking grew in the rural parishes among descendants of Acadian settlers, while Creole cooking grew in New Orleans with French, Spanish, African, and Caribbean influences, so the two names are not interchangeable. The written recipe is a pork shoulder for a home cook, not a whole pig unless you have the pit and the crew.

Cultural background

A home shoulder standing in for a Cajun spit-roasted pig. It is labeled as a shoulder. It is not a Creole tomato sauce dish.

Why it tastes this way

Cayenne, black pepper, and garlic are the seasoning that shows up on Cajun roast pork, and a turning spit or a covered roast browns the fat. The taste is pepper and pork drippings, not a sweet barbecue glaze. Rural south Louisiana heat is why the pepper level is part of the style.

How it is made

Season a pork shoulder hard with Cajun spices and roast it on a spit or, at home, covered until it will pull.

Authentic touch

If you roast a whole dressed pig, this seasoning still applies, but do not pretend a shoulder in a covered cooker is a community spit.

How it is used

Chopped or pulled, with the drippings, on rice or on bread for a crowd.

Field notes

  • Cochon de lait names a pig roast in south Louisiana Cajun community cooking.
  • Cajun and Creole are related Louisiana foods with different histories, not two words for the same pot.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 2 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon paprika
  • 2 teaspoons black pepper
  • 2 teaspoons cayenne
  • 1 tablespoon garlic powder
  • 2 teaspoons dried thyme

Steps

  1. Mix salt, paprika, black pepper, cayenne, garlic powder, and thyme. Rub it over the shoulder. This is a home shoulder, not a whole-pig spit unless you are actually cooking a whole pig.
  2. Roast on a spit over coals or in a covered grill with indirect heat at about 300°F (149°C).
  3. Cook until the center reaches about 195–205°F (90–96°C) and the pork pulls. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; this shoulder goes further so a crowd can chop it.
  4. Rest, then chop and moisten with the drippings.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A covered grill can replace a spit for the home shoulder. Say that you used a covered grill.

Pairings

  • rice
  • sliced bread
  • potato salad
