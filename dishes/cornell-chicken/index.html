Cornell Chicken

Next stop: Chesapeake Oyster Roast · continuing the voyage

Grilled chicken with a tangy, savory baste that carries egg and vinegar.

20 min prep · 55 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Doctor Robert Baker, a food scientist at Cornell University in Ithaca, developed this vinegar-and-egg chicken baste in the mid-twentieth century. It became a standard upstate New York grill. The egg is in the baste, so the dish contains egg and is not vegan. It is not an old folk sauce of unclear authorship.

Cultural background

Half chickens grilled with the Cornell baste. The egg is required for this version. Salt potatoes are the usual companion and are a separate boil, not part of the grill.

Why it tastes this way

Oil and egg help the vinegar and herbs stick to the skin instead of flashing off the coals. The taste is tangy and savory rather than sweet. Upstate fire companies and backyard grills spread the baste because it stays put on a chicken over coals.

How it is made

Grill chicken while brushing on a cooked-on baste of vinegar, oil, and egg.

Authentic touch

Keep the baste cold, brush it on as the chicken cooks, and discard any that touched raw chicken and was not cooked. Do not save raw egg baste on the counter.

How it is used

Half a chicken, often beside Syracuse salt potatoes. The potatoes are boiled, not grilled.

Field notes

  • Dr. Robert Baker at Cornell developed this vinegar-and-egg baste in the mid-twentieth century.
  • The egg in the baste means the dish contains egg and is not vegan.

Ingredients

  • 2 chickens, halved
  • 1 egg
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup neutral oil
  • 1 tablespoon kosher salt
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper
  • 1/2 teaspoon dried sage
  • 1/4 teaspoon dried thyme

Steps

  1. Whisk the egg, vinegar, oil, salt, pepper, sage, and thyme. Keep the baste in the refrigerator. The herbs are listed separately so a commercial blend with wheat is not required.
  2. Heat a grill with medium coals and a cooler zone.
  3. Grill the chicken halves, brushing with baste as they cook, until the thigh reaches 165°F (74°C) away from the bone. The baste on the bird must cook.
  4. Rest a few minutes. Discard leftover baste that touched raw chicken. Do not serve the chicken rare.

Cook poultry to 165°F (74°C) in the thickest part, not touching bone.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Chicken halves fit a home grill more easily than several whole birds.

Pairings

  • salt potatoes
  • green salad
  • rolls
