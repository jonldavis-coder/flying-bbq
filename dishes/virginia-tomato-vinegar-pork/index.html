Virginia Tomato-Vinegar Pork

Next stop: Chicago South Side Rib Tips · continuing the voyage

Chopped pork with a thin, sharp sauce that is more vinegar than tomato.

30 min prep · 10 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Virginia chopped pork is often dressed with a thin tomato-and-vinegar sauce. It is not Lexington dip, which belongs to the North Carolina Piedmont, and it is not the wet shoulder of Memphis. The sauce here is mostly vinegar and water, tinted with a little tomato paste, so it stays thin. This page does not name a single Virginia pit as the inventor.

Cultural background

A Virginia chopped-pork plate with its own thin sauce. Distinct from Lexington and from Memphis by place and by how little tomato it uses.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar still does the main work, which keeps the chop sharp, and tomato paste adds color and a small sweetness without a ketchup dip. Smoke sits on the pork before the sauce is folded in. The plate tastes lighter than a molasses sauce and redder than eastern Carolina.

How it is made

Smoke a pork shoulder and dress the chop with a thin vinegar sauce colored by a little tomato paste.

Authentic touch

Dissolve the tomato paste fully so there are no thick streaks. The sauce should pour like spiced vinegar.

How it is used

Chopped, with sauce on the side, on a plate or a sandwich.

Field notes

  • This Virginia sauce is thinner and more vinegar-forward than a Memphis wet sauce.
  • It is not Lexington dip, which is a Piedmont North Carolina sauce with more ketchup.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 cup apple cider vinegar
  • 1/2 cup water
  • 3 tablespoons tomato paste
  • 1 teaspoon brown sugar
  • 1 teaspoon kosher salt, for the sauce

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) over oak or hickory.
  2. Whisk vinegar, water, tomato paste, brown sugar, and sauce salt until the paste disappears. Keep it thin.
  3. Cook the shoulder to about 195–205°F (90–96°C). Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; chopped pork continues until it pulls.
  4. Rest, chop, and moisten with the sauce. This is a Virginia plate, not a Lexington or Memphis copy.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A bone-in shoulder gives more drippings to moisten the chop than a boneless roast.

Pairings
