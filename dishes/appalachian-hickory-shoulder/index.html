Appalachian Hickory Pork Shoulder

Next stop: Appalachian Sorghum Pork Chops · continuing the voyage

Pork shoulder with a clean hickory smoke, salt, and black pepper.

History

In the mountains of east Tennessee, West Virginia, and western North Carolina, hickory is a common smoke wood for a pork shoulder. This plate is that mountain smoke, salted and peppered, not a single-town sauce style. Scots-Irish and English settlers, Black Appalachian cooks, and Cherokee and other Native nations of the mountains do not all share one recipe, so this page claims only the hickory shoulder a home cook can document.

Cultural background

A hickory-smoked pork shoulder from the southern Appalachians. It is not eastern Carolina whole hog and not a sorghum glaze.

Why it tastes this way

Hickory is a hardwood of these ridges, and its smoke is stronger than a fruit wood. Salt and pepper let that smoke stay the main flavor. A long cook softens the shoulder the same way a lowland pit does, but the wood is the mountain part.

How it is made

Salt and pepper a pork shoulder and smoke it over hickory until it pulls.

Authentic touch

Use hickory if you can name the wood. Oak is the honest substitute. Do not soak the chunks until they smolder bitter.

How it is used

Pulled or chopped, with the juices. A vinegar splash is optional and is not required for this plate.

Field notes

  • Hickory grows through the southern Appalachians and is the smoke wood this shoulder uses.
  • This is a mountain pork shoulder, separate from the vinegar whole-hog plate of eastern North Carolina.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in pork shoulder, about 8 pounds
  • 3 tablespoons kosher salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • Hickory wood, or oak if hickory is unavailable

Steps

  1. Salt and pepper the shoulder and let it stand in the refrigerator while the fire starts.
  2. Smoke it indirectly at about 250°F (121°C) over hickory.
  3. Cook to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), until a probe slides in. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest; a pulled shoulder continues higher so the collagen softens.
  4. Rest, then pull or chop. Keep the juices.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A spoon of the vinegar-pepper sauce from eastern Carolina can be served on the side without changing the smoke.

Pairings

  • soup beans
  • skillet cornbread
  • chow-chow
