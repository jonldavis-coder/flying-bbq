Monterrey Cabrito

Next stop: Norteño Discada · continuing the voyage

Pronounced kah-BREE-toh

Young goat, salted, with a browned skin and a mild, meaty center.

History

Cabrito in Monterrey is kid goat, cooked on a spit or opened over an open fire. Nuevo León made the dish a restaurant and family standard in the twentieth century, in a city already tied to northern ranching. The animal is young, which is what the word means. A full-grown goat is a different cook.

Cultural background

This is Monterrey goat, not a lamb barbacoa and not a pork pibil. Restaurants often open the kid flat beside glowing coals. A home cook with a leg or a small shoulder should say that the cut is not a whole kid.

Why it tastes this way

Kid goat is leaner than a pork shoulder, so the taste is mild and a little sweet if the fire is gentle. Mesquite or oak coals in this dry northern city brown the skin without a sweet glaze. Salt is the seasoning that lets the meat stay the point of the plate.

How it is made

Salt a kid, or a home-size leg labeled as not the whole animal, and roast it on a spit or beside coals to a whole-muscle temperature.

Authentic touch

Keep the fire beside the meat or under a steady bed of coals. A roaring flame dries a lean kid before the center is done.

How it is used

Cut it into pieces and eat it with flour or corn tortillas, salsa, and beans. The tortillas are pairings.

Field notes

  • Cabrito means a young goat, not a full-grown animal.
  • In Monterrey it is cooked on a spit or opened flat beside the fire.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in kid goat leg, about 4 pounds, labeled as a home cut and not a whole kid
  • 1 tablespoon coarse salt
  • 4 garlic cloves, crushed
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • Wood or charcoal for a spit or an open grill

Steps

  1. Rub the leg with oil, salt, pepper, and garlic. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it until the fire is ready. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Spit the leg or set it beside a bed of coals, not in open flame. This is the Monterrey fire, scaled to one leg. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the thickest part, away from bone, reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Carve the rested leg. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A bone-in leg is the workable home cut. Call it a leg, not a whole cabrito.
  • Some asadores use only salt. A little garlic is a home addition and should stay light.

Pairings

  • salsa
  • beans
  • tortillas
  • grilled onions
