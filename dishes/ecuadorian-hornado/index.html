Ecuadorian Hornado

Next stop: Argentine Chimichurri · continuing the voyage

Pronounced or-NAH-doh

Roast pork with garlic, cumin, and achiote, sliced, with a browned outside.

30 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Traditional hornado is a long roast in a wood-fired oven, often a whole pig, in the Ecuadorian highlands. Ambato is one city known for it, and the cook is wider than one town. A home oven shoulder is the workable version and is not that long wood-oven pig. Garlic, cumin, and achiote are the seasoning. This recipe uses vinegar instead of beer so the written shoulder has no wheat.

Cultural background

The shoulder is pork, cooked until it is safe to slice, and longer if you want it to pull. The skin can crisp if it stays uncovered and dry. Call the pan a home oven roast.

Why it tastes this way

A long oven visit gives the garlic and cumin time to season the fat, and achiote colors it without a chile burn. Highland wood ovens add a gentle roast flavor that a covered braise does not. The home oven keeps the spice and loses some of the wood. The pork still tastes of those spices.

How it is made

Rub a pork shoulder with garlic, cumin, and achiote and roast it in a home oven to a pork-shoulder temperature.

Authentic touch

Salt the skin and leave it uncovered if you want it crisp. A tight lid will steam it soft.

How it is used

Slice it and eat it with llapingachos or potatoes, and a simple onion relish. Those sides are pairings.

Field notes

  • Traditional hornado is a long wood-oven roast, often a whole pig, in the Ecuadorian highlands.
  • A home oven shoulder is the workable version and should be called that.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds skin-on pork shoulder, a home hornado
  • 8 garlic cloves, mashed
  • 1 tablespoon ground achiote (annatto)
  • 2 teaspoons ground cumin
  • 1 tablespoon salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons vinegar
  • 2 tablespoons neutral oil

Steps

  1. Mix garlic, achiote, cumin, salt, pepper, vinegar, and oil. Rub it over the pork and under the skin if you can. The pork is raw and is not ready to eat. Refrigerate it if it waits. Do not leave the paste at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Heat a home oven to 325°F (165°C). This is not a wood-fired highland oven and not a whole pig.
  3. Roast uncovered until the center is at least 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes to slice. For pullable pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C), then rest.
  4. Slice or pull the rested pork. The center should already have reached the temperature you chose. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • For sliced pork, stop at 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest. For meat you can pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  • Beer appears in some marinades. This one uses vinegar so the written recipe has no wheat.

Pairings
