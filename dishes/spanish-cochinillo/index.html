Segovia Suckling Pig

Next stop: Lisbon June Sardines · continuing the voyage

Pronounced co-chee-NEE-yo

Crisp salted skin and soft pork, with the scent of a wood oven.

History

Segovia is known for suckling pig roasted in wood-fired ovens until the skin blisters and the meat slumps. The dish is a city specialty served in asadores, and it does not have a single inventor this page can name. A home oven is a labeled stand-in, not that baker's oven. The seasoning is salt and water or oil, not a barbecue sauce. A small shoulder or a very small pig is the realistic home cut.

Cultural background

This is Segovia's roast, cooked by oven houses and copied at home with a clear label. It is not a spit hog and not a Cuban lechon. The home oven must be named as a home oven.

Why it tastes this way

A young pig has thin skin and delicate fat, so dry heat turns the outside to crackling while the inside stays soft. Wood ovens hold an even heat that home ovens can imitate but not duplicate. Salt is the only seasoning because the skin needs to stay dry. The Castilian plateau town built a reputation on that plain roast.

How it is made

Salt a small pork shoulder or a very small suckling piece and roast it in a hot oven until the skin is crisp and the meat is safe to carve or pull.

Authentic touch

Dry the skin thoroughly and salt it. A wet surface will steam instead of blister. This home version is an oven roast, labeled as such.

How it is used

Cut into pieces with the crackling intact, with a green salad. The traditional plates are simple.

Field notes

  • Segovia's suckling pig is roasted in wood-fired ovens, and the home version here is a labeled oven roast.
  • Salt and a dry skin matter more than any sauce.

Ingredients

  • 1 skin-on pork shoulder, about 5 lb, as the home stand-in for suckling pig
  • 2 tablespoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon water
  • 1 tablespoon olive oil
  • Black pepper

Steps

  1. Heat a home oven to 425°F (220°C). This is a home oven, labeled as a stand-in for Segovia's wood oven. Pat the skin dry and rub it with salt and a little oil.
  2. Set the meat on a rack. Roast until the skin starts to blister, then lower the heat to 350°F (175°C).
  3. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For a shoulder you want soft enough to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  4. Rest, then cut through the crackling. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A pork shoulder with the skin on, which is the practical home cut.
  • A true wood-fired baker's oven if you have access to one.

Pairings

  • Green salad
  • Roasted potatoes
  • Peppers
