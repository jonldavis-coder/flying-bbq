Mongolian Khorkhog

Next stop: Korean Ssamjang · continuing the voyage

Pronounced HOR-hog

Lamb cooked in a closed pot with onion, carrot, and potato, salty and rich.

25 min prep · 1 hr 30 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Khorkhog is a Mongolian lamb dish cooked in a closed container with vegetables. The traditional method heats stones and puts those stones in the pot with the meat. This recipe does not do that. It uses a heavy covered pot on the stove; do not heat found river rocks for this dish; rocks from a river can hold water and can crack or explode when they are put in a fire; stones are mentioned here only as the traditional method you are not reproducing with found rocks; the lamb is a whole cut and is cooked at least to a safe temperature.

Cultural background

Mongolian cooks make khorkhog for gatherings, in a large metal churn or a heavy pot in the older method. The home version on this page is the covered pot, without stones. There is lamb, onion, carrot, and potato. There is no wheat and no dairy in the pot.

Why it tastes this way

A closed pot keeps lamb fat with the vegetables, so the potato and carrot taste of the meat and the lamb stays moist. Salt is the seasoning. The traditional stones were a way to carry heat inside the pot. A heavy lid on a stove holds that steam without the risk of a wet rock exploding. The dish is the lamb and the vegetables, not the hazard.

How it is made

Salt lamb and vegetables in a heavy covered pot and cook them until the lamb is at least at a safe temperature and tender. Do not add rocks.

Authentic touch

Use a heavy pot with a tight lid and do not lift it every few minutes. The steam is the cook.

How it is used

Eat the lamb, potato, carrot, and onion together, with the juices in the pot.

Field notes

  • Traditional khorkhog uses heated stones in the pot. This recipe uses a heavy covered pot and no stones.
  • Do not heat found river rocks. They can hold water and can crack or explode.

Ingredients

  • 3 pounds boneless lamb shoulder, in large chunks
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 large onion, cut in wedges
  • 3 carrots, cut in chunks
  • 1.5 pounds potatoes, cut in chunks
  • 1 cup water
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Put the lamb, salt, pepper, onion, carrot, potato, and water in a heavy pot with a tight lid. The lamb is still raw. Do not add stones of any kind. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Cover the pot and set it over medium heat on a stove. This is the home method. The traditional stone method is not what you are doing, and found river rocks can explode. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Cook until the center of the thickest lamb chunk reaches at least 145°F (63°C). You may continue until the lamb is tender if the temperature stays at or above that mark. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes in the pot with the lid off the heat. Serve the meat and vegetables. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A covered pot set beside a controlled outdoor fire is still a pot, not a bag of hot rocks.
  • Mutton is used where it is the local sheep. Treat it as a whole cut and check the temperature.

Pairings

  • carrot
  • pickled vegetable
