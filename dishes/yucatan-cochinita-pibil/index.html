Yucatán Cochinita Pibil

Next stop: Poc Chuc · continuing the voyage

Pronounced ko-chee-NEE-tah pee-BEEL

Sour orange, achiote, and slow pork, dyed red and soft enough to shred.

40 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

The pib is a Maya pit: a hole of heated stones used to cook food in the ground. Pork and sour orange arrived in Yucatán after Spanish contact. Achiote, the seed of Bixa orellana, is native to the tropical Americas and is what turns the pork red. A covered pan in a home oven can borrow the seasoning, and it should be labeled as not a pib.

Cultural background

Cochinita pibil is young pig, or a pork shoulder in a home kitchen, wrapped and cooked until it shreds. It is Yucatecan fiesta food and also a weekday taco filling. The oven version is a substitute for the pit, not the pit itself.

Why it tastes this way

Achiote is earthy and slightly peppery, and sour orange cuts the fat of the shoulder. In a pib the meat steams against hot stone and banana leaf instead of searing. That covered heat is why the pork tastes dyed and tangy rather than like a grilled chop. The home oven can echo the steam, without the wood and the ground.

How it is made

Marinate a pork shoulder in achiote and sour orange, wrap it, and cook it covered until it shreds. A home oven is not a pib.

Authentic touch

Grind the achiote with the garlic and juice so the paste stains the meat, and do not skip the rest after it leaves the heat.

How it is used

Shred it and eat it with warm corn tortillas, pickled red onion, and a habanero salsa on the side if you want more heat.

Field notes

  • Achiote comes from Bixa orellana, a plant native to the tropical Americas.
  • Sour orange and pork reached Yucatán after Spanish contact; the pib is older than those ingredients.

Ingredients

  • 4 pounds boneless pork shoulder
  • 1/4 cup ground achiote (annatto) seed
  • 1/2 cup sour orange juice, or 1/4 cup orange juice plus 1/4 cup lime juice
  • 8 garlic cloves, peeled
  • 1 tablespoon dried Mexican oregano
  • 2 teaspoons salt
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper
  • 2 large banana leaves, passed over a flame until flexible, or parchment if you have no leaves

Steps

  1. Blend the achiote, sour orange juice, garlic, oregano, salt, and pepper into a paste. Coat the pork. The meat is still raw and is not ready to eat. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  2. Wrap the coated pork in banana leaf or parchment and set it in a heavy pot with a tight lid. Refrigerate it if it sits more than 2 hours. Do not leave the paste at room temperature. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  3. Heat a home oven to 325°F (165°C). This oven is not a pib. Cook the wrapped pork until the center is at least 145°F (63°C). For cochinita that shreds, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  4. Rest the pork 3 minutes, then shred it. The center should already have reached the temperature in the cooking step. Serve warm, and refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. The pork is not ready to eat until the center reaches at least 145°F (63°C) and rests 3 minutes. For meat you want to pull, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • Some cooks add a little habanero to the recado; this version keeps the chile in a side salsa.
  • Banana leaves are the usual wrapper when they are sold. Parchment works in a home oven and is not a leaf.

Pairings

  • pickled red onion
  • corn tortillas
  • habanero salsa
  • black beans
