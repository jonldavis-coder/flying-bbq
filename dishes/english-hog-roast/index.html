English Hog Roast

Next stop: Cornish Grilled Mackerel · continuing the voyage

Pronounced hog rohst

Salted pork, crackling, and wood smoke, with enough fat to baste the meat.

30 min prep · 4 hr 30 min cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Turning a pig over a fire is an old English way to feed a gathering. The everyday Sunday roast is usually an oven joint, and the spit is the older fire method. A whole animal needs a long spit, a steady cook, and more meat than most kitchens can hold, so this home version uses a pork shoulder. Salt, time, and a dry skin do the work that a sweet glaze would only cover.

Cultural background

This is crowd food for English celebrations, not a weekly supper. The shoulder stands in for a whole hog when the spit and the animal are out of reach. It is still a roast, carved, not a pulled-pork sandwich recipe.

Why it tastes this way

Pork shoulder carries fat and collagen, and a wood fire browns the skin while the interior stays moist. English cooks have long used that contrast: a crisp outside and a sliceable or shreddable inside. The oven Sunday joint tastes of the kitchen; the spit tastes of the fire that came before it.

How it is made

Salt a pork shoulder and turn it on a spit over hardwood coals, or roast it on a rack over a pan if the spit is small. Carve when the temperature and the skin are both ready.

Authentic touch

Score the skin and salt it ahead of the fire so the crackling dries. Keep the flames under the fat, not licking the skin into soot.

How it is used

Carved onto plates with apple sauce and a sharp salad. Leftover meat is good cold.

Field notes

  • A home Sunday roast in England is usually cooked in an oven, while the spit is the older open-fire method.
  • A whole hog is still a fair and wedding roast; a shoulder is the practical home cut.

Ingredients

  • 1 pork shoulder, about 6 lb, skin on
  • 2 tablespoons coarse salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • Hardwood chunks or charcoal for the fire

Steps

  1. Pat the shoulder dry, score the skin, and rub it with salt, pepper, and oil. Leave it uncovered in the refrigerator for at least a few hours if you can.
  2. Build a hardwood or charcoal fire with a space to one side so the shoulder is not in direct flame. Set it on a spit or on a rack over a drip pan.
  3. Turn it steadily. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For meat you want to pull or chop, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) in the thickest part.
  4. Rest it, then carve. Crackling should blister, not blacken. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A whole small pig on a proper spit, for a crew that can tend it all day.
  • The same salted shoulder in a hot oven when there is no spit.

Pairings

  • Apple sauce
  • Roast potatoes
  • Savoy cabbage
  • Sharp cider
