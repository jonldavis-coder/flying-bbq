Greek Easter Spit Lamb

Next stop: Adana Kebab · on Mediterranean by balloon, by Hot air balloon

Pronounced ah-RNI sti-SOU-vla

Lamb with oregano, lemon, garlic, and wood smoke from a long turn on the spit.

40 min prep · 4 hr cook · Serves 10 · about 10 servings

History

Spit-roasted lamb is a Pascha food for many Greek Orthodox families. After the fast, the lamb is seasoned with oregano, lemon, and garlic and turned over charcoal or wood, often in a courtyard. Not every household sets up a spit, and the meal is a celebration rather than a rule. A leg is the home version of the whole animal. The cooking is slow enough that people stand around the fire for hours.

Cultural background

The cooks are families marking Orthodox Easter, especially in towns and villages where a courtyard can hold a spit. It is not a weeknight chop and not a kebab shop souvlaki.

Why it tastes this way

A whole-muscle lamb roast picks up smoke slowly while lemon and oregano perfume the fat. The long turn renders the outside without boiling the meat in a pot. Spring lamb and the end of the fast give the meal its meaning. Charcoal is common; fruitwood or vine cuttings show up where people have them.

How it is made

Season a lamb leg and spit-roast it over coals until it is safe to rest and carve. A whole lamb is the celebration scale if you have the spit and the people.

Authentic touch

Truss the leg so it turns evenly, and baste with lemon and oil late so the herbs do not burn black.

How it is used

Carved at the table with bread, salad, and roasted potatoes. It is a Pascha meal for the households that keep it.

Field notes

  • Spit-roasted lamb is a Pascha food for many Greek Orthodox families, not a requirement for every house.
  • Oregano, lemon, and garlic are the usual seasoning on the spit.

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb leg, about 6 lb
  • 3 tablespoons olive oil
  • 2 lemons, juiced
  • 4 garlic cloves, chopped
  • 2 tablespoons dried oregano
  • 1 tablespoon salt

Steps

  1. Stir oil, lemon juice, garlic, oregano, and salt. Rub the leg and truss it so it will turn evenly.
  2. Spit the leg over a bed of coals, or use a rotisserie. Keep the heat moderate.
  3. Cook whole lamb to at least 145°F (63°C) in the thickest part and give it a 3-minute rest. Many cooks take it further until the meat is softer, which is a preference after the safe temperature.
  4. Carve and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A whole lamb when the gathering and the equipment match.
  • A covered grill used as a rotisserie for a single leg.

Pairings

  • Bread
  • Greek salad
  • Roasted potatoes
