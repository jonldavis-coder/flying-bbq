Sardinian Porceddu

Next stop: Croatian Raznjici · continuing the voyage

Pronounced por-CHED-doo

Pork with myrtle, salt, and wood smoke, and a crisp skin if the fire is steady.

History

Porceddu is Sardinian spit-roasted suckling pig, basted or rested with myrtle, which grows wild on the island. It is feast food in the interior and at celebrations. A whole piglet on a spit is the full version. This home recipe uses a skin-on pork shoulder and says so: it is a home version, not a whole animal over a pit in the countryside. Myrtle is the herb that marks it.

Cultural background

Sardinian hosts cook porceddu for gatherings. The home shoulder is labeled as a home version. It is not Segovia's oven pig and not a sweet-glazed ham.

Why it tastes this way

Myrtle is resinous and slightly bitter, and it perfumes pork fat when the branches are used as a brush or a resting bed. Wood smoke and salt do the rest. Young pork skin crackles over a spit; a shoulder in a home setup can get part of the way there if the skin is dry. The herb is the island's signature, not a generic poultry blend.

How it is made

Salt a pork shoulder, spit or rack-roast it, and baste with myrtle and oil. Cook it to a safe temperature and rest it.

Authentic touch

Use fresh myrtle if you can buy it, and keep the leaves out of the fire so they do not turn acrid. This is a home shoulder, labeled, unless you are truly cooking a piglet on a spit.

How it is used

Sliced or pulled, with a bitter green salad. Myrtle can line the platter.

Field notes

  • Porceddu is Sardinian spit-roasted suckling pig flavored with myrtle.
  • The recipe here is a labeled home version on a pork shoulder unless you have a whole pig and a spit.

Ingredients

  • 1 skin-on pork shoulder, about 5 lb, as the home version
  • 2 tablespoons salt
  • 1 handful fresh myrtle leaves, or 2 teaspoons dried myrtle
  • 2 tablespoons olive oil
  • 1 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Pat the shoulder dry. Rub it with salt, pepper, and oil. Tuck some myrtle against the meat. This is a home version, not a whole pig on a countryside spit.
  2. Spit-roast over coals or roast on a rack in a hot covered grill, turning as needed.
  3. Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For a shoulder you want pullable, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C).
  4. Rest it on the remaining myrtle if you have fresh branches that did not burn. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Whole pork is safe at 145°F (63°C) with a 3-minute rest. For pulled or chopped pork, continue to about 195–205°F (90–96°C) so the collagen softens, then rest it.

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A whole suckling pig on a spit for a real gathering, still cooked to a safe temperature.
  • Bay and rosemary if myrtle is impossible to find, named as a substitute herb, not as myrtle.

Pairings

  • Bitter greens
  • Roasted potatoes
  • Olive oil
