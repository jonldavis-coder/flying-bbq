Hokkaido Jingisukan

Next stop: Japanese Tsukune · continuing the voyage

Pronounced JIN-ghee-soo-kahn

Lamb and onion seared on a hot domed pan, salted, with a little cabbage.

20 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 4 · about 4 servings

History

Jingisukan takes its name from Genghis Khan. In Japan that name is twentieth-century marketing, tied to lamb grilling in Hokkaido, not a recipe from the Mongol court. Sapporo restaurants cook lamb and vegetables on a domed metal pan at the table. Sheep farming on Hokkaido is the local supply; the lamb is a whole cut, cooked and rested; this recipe salts the meat and does not use soy sauce.

Cultural background

Hokkaido cooks grill lamb this way in specialty rooms and at home on the same style of pan. A heavy skillet is the honest substitute if you do not own the dome. There is lamb and vegetables. There is no wheat and no dairy in this version. It is not a Mongol khorkhog.

Why it tastes this way

Lamb fat seasons the onion and cabbage on the same pan, and salt keeps the meat direct. The dome, when you have one, lets fat run to the rim where the vegetables sit. The name is a sales name from the twentieth century. The taste is Hokkaido lamb and a hot pan, which does not require a story about a medieval army.

How it is made

Sear lamb and vegetables on a domed pan or a heavy skillet, and rest the lamb.

Authentic touch

Cook the lamb in batches on a very hot pan. If you fill the pan, the meat boils in onion water.

How it is used

Eat the lamb and vegetables together, with rice.

Field notes

  • The name Jingisukan, from Genghis Khan, is twentieth-century marketing in Japan.
  • It is a Hokkaido lamb grill. It is not a recipe from the Mongol court.

Ingredients

  • 1.5 pounds boneless lamb, sliced 1/4 inch thick
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 onion, sliced
  • 1/4 small cabbage, cut in wedges
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 1/2 teaspoon black pepper

Steps

  1. Season the lamb with salt and pepper. The lamb is still raw. Have the vegetables ready beside the pan. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Heat a domed jingisukan pan or a heavy skillet until it is very hot. Oil it lightly. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Sear the lamb and vegetables until the center of the lamb reaches at least 145°F (63°C). Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  4. Rest the lamb 3 minutes. Serve with the vegetables and rice. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours. Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A soy dip is served in some Sapporo shops and would add soy and wheat. This pan is salted.
  • A heavy skillet stands in for the domed pan.
