Salvadoran Carne Asada

Next stop: Honduran Carne Asada with Chimol · continuing the voyage

Pronounced kar-neh ah-SAH-dah, koor-TEE-doh

Grilled beef with a tangy cabbage curtido.

30 min prep · 20 min cook · Serves 6 · about 6 servings

History

Salvadoran carne asada is grilled beef eaten with curtido, the cabbage relish also served with pupusas. The name matches Sonoran carne asada in Spanish and not in the plate: there is no mesquite rule here, and the relish is curtido rather than a flour-tortilla steak house spread. This curtido is a quick refrigerator mix, not a canned preserve.

Cultural background

The steak is the cook. Curtido is included because it is how many Salvadoran tables finish the plate. It is cabbage, carrot, onion, oregano, and vinegar, used cold.

Why it tastes this way

Vinegar and oregano in the cabbage cut the richness of grilled beef. The steak itself is simply salted, so the char stays clear. Curtido is crunchy and sour, which is a different contrast from the tomato chirmol of Guatemala or the chimol of Honduras.

How it is made

Grill beef to a whole-muscle temperature and serve a quick curtido beside it.

Authentic touch

Salt the curtido and let it sit while the grill heats, so the cabbage softens slightly without becoming a jam.

How it is used

Eat the steak with curtido, corn tortillas or rice, and fried plantains if you have them.

Field notes

  • Curtido is the cabbage relish Salvadoran cooks also use beside pupusas.
  • This carne asada is not the mesquite steak of Sonora, even though the Spanish name matches.

Ingredients

  • 2 pounds beef steak
  • 1 teaspoon salt
  • 1 tablespoon neutral oil
  • 3 cups finely shredded green cabbage
  • 1 carrot, grated
  • 1/2 onion, sliced thin
  • 1/2 cup distilled white vinegar
  • 1 teaspoon dried oregano
  • 1 jalapeño, sliced

Steps

  1. Toss the cabbage, carrot, onion, jalapeño, oregano, vinegar, and 1 teaspoon salt. This is a quick curtido, not a canned preserve. Refrigerate it and use it within five days. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  2. Oil and salt the steak. It is still raw and is not ready to eat. Whole cuts are not ready to eat until they reach at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes.
  3. Grill the steak until the thickest part reads 145°F (63°C). Rest it 3 minutes.
  4. Slice the rested steak. It should have reached 145°F (63°C) before the rest. Serve it with the cold curtido. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A longer curtido, fermented a day or two in the refrigerator, is traditional in some kitchens. This one is the quick version.
  • Thin sliced pork shoulder is sometimes grilled the same day. Pork still needs 145°F (63°C) and a 3-minute rest. This recipe is beef.

Pairings

  • curtido
  • corn tortillas
  • fried plantains
  • rice
