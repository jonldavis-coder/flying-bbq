Icelandic Hot-smoked Lamb

Next stop: Norwegian Pinnekjøtt · continuing the voyage

Pronounced HAHN-yee-kyuht

Lamb with a light birch or hardwood smoke, salted, and still a fresh roast rather than a dried ham.

25 min prep · 3 hr cook · Serves 8 · about 8 servings

History

Traditional hangikjöt is hung meat given a long cold smoke, then kept as a preserved lamb that is often simmered before eating. This recipe is a hot-smoked leg in that direction, cooked to 145°F, not the preserved product. A home smoker finishes the meat for dinner. It does not replace the Christmas hangikjöt that Icelandic households buy from people who still do the long smoke.

Cultural background

The cooks are Icelandic families who want the smoke of lamb without claiming a legal or traditional copy of hangikjöt. It is a fresh roast. It is not air-dried and not left in a cold shed for weeks.

Why it tastes this way

Icelandic lamb is grazed on open land, and the meat is already distinct before any smoke. A hot smoke lays a light wood note over that lamb and cooks it through. Birch has been a traditional smoking wood where it was available. The long cold smoke of hangikjöt concentrates and preserves; this shorter heat does not.

How it is made

Salt a lamb leg and hot-smoke it until the center is cooked. Do not call the result hangikjöt.

Authentic touch

Keep the smoker around a gentle roast, not a blazing grill. If you want the preserved hangikjöt, buy it from a producer instead of trying to cure a leg at home.

How it is used

Sliced warm, with potatoes, peas, and a white sauce if that is your table. It is a dinner roast, not a cold cut from the curing shed.

Field notes

  • Hangikjöt means hung meat and is traditionally a long cold smoke, not a same-day roast.
  • This hot-smoked leg is cooked to 145°F and is not the preserved product.

Ingredients

  • 1 lamb leg, about 5 lb
  • 2 tablespoons salt
  • 1 tablespoon black pepper
  • 1 teaspoon dried thyme
  • Hardwood or birch chips for a light smoke

Steps

  1. Rub the leg with salt, pepper, and thyme. This recipe does not include a home cure or a cold smoke.
  2. Set a smoker or covered grill for indirect heat and a thin smoke.
  3. Hot-smoke the leg until the thickest part reaches at least 145°F (63°C), then give it a 3-minute rest. It is a fresh roast, not preserved hangikjöt.
  4. Slice and serve. Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours.

Cook whole cuts of beef, pork, lamb, or goat to at least 145°F (63°C) and rest 3 minutes. A shoulder you want to pull can continue toward 195°F (90°C).

Refrigerate leftovers within 2 hours and use within 3 to 4 days, or freeze. Reheat leftovers to 165°F (74°C). This is a home recipe, not professional food-safety advice.

Variations

  • A boneless leg tied into an even shape so it smokes more evenly.
  • Served cold the next day after proper refrigeration, still as cooked meat.

Pairings

  • Potatoes
  • Peas
  • Green beans
